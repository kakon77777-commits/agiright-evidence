Universal Music signs ElevenLabs to build a licensed AI remix app

The deal is ElevenLabs' first with a major label, letting fans remix participating artists' tracks. No terms or launch date were given, and voice rights sit at the centre.

creative industries
Copyright
intellectual property

TL;DR

Universal Music Group has struck a multi-year agreement with ElevenLabs covering both licensing and joint product work, starting with an AI platform that lets fans remix music from artists who opt in. It is the audio company’s first deal with a major label. For the UK copyright debate, it is further evidence that rights holders want AI access on licensed terms rather than through a legal exception.

What was agreed

The two companies announced the partnership on 10 September. The first product, still being built, will let fans produce remixes, mashups and fresh versions of songs, plus what the announcement calls “personalized vocal experiences”, all drawn from catalogue by artists and songwriters who have chosen to take part. Further tools aimed at artists themselves are planned over the coming years.

ElevenLabs says the platform will run separately from its existing music offerings, which include a developer API and a song-generation app called ElevenMusic.

Neither side disclosed financial terms or a launch date. Both chief executives framed the arrangement around payment: Sir Lucian Grainge spoke of “new revenue opportunities for the creative community”, while ElevenLabs co-founder Mati Staniszewski said artists and songwriters would be “fairly compensated”.

Why the structure matters

Labels are pursuing two tracks at once. On one, publishers are going to court: Sony and Warner sued Anthropic over song lyrics this month. On the other, catalogue owners are licensing directly to AI companies that agree to pay. A deal like this one strengthens the argument that a market for licensed training and generation already exists, which is the case UK creative industries have made against loosening copyright rules for AI developers.

The voice element is the part to watch. A consumer product built around personalised vocals depends on artists consenting to synthetic use of their voices, exactly the protection that more than 80 UK performers recently asked the prime minister to put into law.

Looking forward

Two things remain unclear: how many artists will actually participate, and how revenue will be split between label, songwriter and performer. Those details will decide whether this becomes a template or a showcase.

For UK businesses using synthetic audio in advertising, e-learning or customer service, the direction is the useful signal. Licensed, consent-based voice and music is becoming the commercial norm the largest rights holders will expect, and procurement checks on audio provenance are likely to follow.
