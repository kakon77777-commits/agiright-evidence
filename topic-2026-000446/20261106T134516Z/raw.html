In 2026, voters decided or will decide on at least 50 local ballot measures related to data centers in 13 states: Arkansas, California, Florida, Illinois, Kansas, Maine, Michigan, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, North Dakota, Ohio, and Wisconsin.

The majority of measures, 41 of 50 (82%), were scheduled for the general election on November 3, with six appearing earlier in the year and three later in the year.

A data center is a physical facility that houses computer systems and related equipment used to store, manage, process, and transmit digital information. Data centers typically contain rows of computer servers, data storage systems, and networking equipment, along with the power and cooling infrastructure needed to operate them.[1][2]

Data centers vary in size and type. A large, warehouse-sized facility is known as a hyperscale data center. According to the Congressional Research Service, increasing demand for data storage and computing power, particularly for AI, has driven the construction of hyperscale data centers.[2][3]

This page includes a full list of data-center-related ballot measures that Ballotpedia has tracked. For a list of recalls related to data center development, click here.

Measures by state

Voters in 13 states — Arkansas, California, Florida, Illinois, Kansas, Maine, Michigan, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, North Dakota, Ohio, and Wisconsin — have decided or will decide on data center-related ballot measures in 2026.

  • Ohio accounts for the largest share of measures—21 of 50 (42%), across 18 distinct jurisdictions.
  • Illinois has seven measures, four of which are concentrated in Madison County.
  • The other 22 measures are spread across 11 states: Maine (4), Wisconsin (3), Kansas (3), California (2), Arkansas (2), New Hampshire (2), North Dakota (2), Florida (1), Michigan (1), Nevada (1), and New Jersey (1).

List of ballot measures

In 2026, voters have decided or will decide on at least 50 ballot measures related to data centers. The measures would either:

  • allow or facilitate data center development, or advise government officials to support their development; or
  • limit or prohibit data center development, advise government officials to oppose their development, or establish additional requirements affecting such development.

As of November 4, 2026, voters have decided 47 of the measures. In 46 cases, a majority favored greater limits on data center development, either by approving restrictions or requirements or by rejecting a measure that would have facilitated or supported development. In Harwood, North Dakota, voters rejected additional review requirements for certain industrial developments, such as data centers.

Proposed measures that did not make the ballot

Ballotpedia tracked the following proposed state and local ballot measures related to data centers that did not make the ballot in 2026:

State

  • Ohio: The ballot initiative would prohibit the construction of data centers that are used for digital data processing with an aggregate power demand exceeding 25 megawatts.[4] Attorney General Dave Yost (R) approved the ballot initiative for signature gathering on March 26, 2026.[5] The campaign, Ohio Residents for Responsible Development, needed to collect 413,487 valid signatures by July 1, 2026.[6] On June 17, the campaign announced that it would not submit signatures by the deadline but said, “Based on our progress so far, we feel confident in making the deadline for the 2027 general election.”[7]

Local

Arizona

  • Marana, Arizona: On February 4, 2026, the town clerk confirmed that Arizonans for Responsible Development submitted about 2,800 signatures for a referendum on the town's rezoning ordinance allowing a 600-acre data center campus.[8] The town clerk said the submitted signatures were invalid because the petitions did not include the legal descriptions of the properties.[9] On February 17, Arizonans for Responsible Development submitted a request to withdraw the petitions, but the town clerk rejected the request.[10] Marana Citizens for Ethical and Transparent Government filed a lawsuit challenging the town’s decision to reject the petitions, while Fremont Peak Properties LLC filed a lawsuit challenging the town’s decision not to allow the petitions to be withdrawn.[11] On May 1, a Pima County judge upheld Marana’s rejection of two referendum petitions over missing ordinance and property details.[12]

Georgia

  • Coweta County, Georgia: Citizens for Rural Coweta launched a referendum campaign against an ordinance to allow data centers as a special use in unincorporated areas, such as the 829-acre, 900-megawatt Project Sail.[13] Steve Swope, organizer of Citizens for Rural Coweta, said, "If we have more than 12,000 valid signatures, the county must call a special election and put the question on the ballot."[14]

Kansas

  • Edgerton, Kansas: Supporters submitted signatures on July 15, 2026, which the Johnson County Election Office verified on July 20.[15] The Edgerton City Council voted 3–1 on July 23, 2026 to seek a declaratory judgment from Johnson County District Court on whether the ordinance was legally eligible for the citizen-petition process. On September 3, 2026, Johnson County District Court Judge Stephanie Goodenow dismissed the city's lawsuit, ruling it violated the residents' rights under the Kansas Public Speech Protection Act.[16] A separate lawsuit, filed by Eric Twente against the Edgerton city clerk, sought to compel actual placement of the question on the ballot. Judge Goodenow denied an emergency motion for immediate placement but ordered the city to show cause why the measure shouldn't go on the ballot, with a hearing set for September 24, 2026.[17]

Maryland

  • Frederick County, Maryland: The Frederick County Data Center Referendum Committee filed a referendum against an ordinance that rezoned 838 acres for data center use and created a 2,615-acre data center overlay zone near Adamstown.[18] The campaign had until March 20 to collect 15,611 valid signatures.[19][20] On March 19, the committee submitted 24,053 signatures to the Board of Elections.[21] On April 3, the Frederick County Board of Elections determined that supporters had submitted enough valid signatures to move forward, reporting that 21,029 of the 24,053 signatures were valid.[22] A total of five legal challenges were filed against the referendum. One was from Quantum Maryland, LLC, which sued the Frederick County Board of Elections and the Frederick County Data Center Referendum Committee, alleging that the referendum is invalid because the subject matter is not law under the county's lawmaking procedures.[23][24][25] On June 18, Judge James Bonifant ruled that an ordinance is not a law subject to referendum under the Frederick County charter.[26] On June 27, the Maryland Supreme Court agreed to hear the case.[27] On June 30, the Maryland Supreme Court ruled that the referendum could not appear on the ballot.[28]

Michigan

  • Mason, Michigan: Supporters of a referendum submitted 700 signatures to overturn the city’s ordinance regulating potential data center development, arguing that the ordinance was not adequate.[29] On March 16, 2026, the Mason City Council voted 4-2 to repeal the targeted ordinance, rather than sending the referendum to the ballot.[30] Mayor Russell Whipple, who voted to repeal the ordinance, said, "We can turn around and re-enact it at the next meeting. Or we could re-enact another ordinance that's different. There would be no issues with that. If we wait for the vote, then we wouldn't be able to do anything for the next five months because it would be suspended."[31]

Missouri

  • Independence, Missouri: On March 3, 2026, opponents of a tax incentive ordinance for a proposed data center filed a referendum petition to challenge the law.[32] On March 5, a government spokesperson said the city's charter "does not permit a referendum on this specific matter, and approving a petition for circulation would be in violation of the Charter."[33] Supporters of the referendum filed litigation against City Clerk Susanne Holland, saying officials could not block the referendum effort.[34] On March 25, Judge Jennifer Phillips ruled that the ordinance was not subject to a referendum.[35]
  • Joplin, Missouri: The Joplin Sustainable Technology Alliance proposed referendums targeting the annexation and rezoning of part of Wildwood Ranch for industrial use to allow data center development.[36] Supporters of a referendum submitted about 2,800 signatures for the referendums; about 5,000 were required.[37][38]

Montana

  • Butte-Silver Bow County, Montana: 406 People First proposed an initiative to prohibit the city-county government from authorizing the construction or expansion of a data center without approval from two-thirds of registered voters. The group needed to collect 3,652 valid signatures to qualify the initiative for the November ballot.[39] Petitioners gathered enough signatures based on the deadline initially supplied to them, but election officials subsequently determined that the actual deadline had passed.[40]
  • Yellowstone County, Montana: Yellowstone County Voices proposed an initiative to require approval from two-thirds of registered voters before a data center could be constructed or expanded in the county. The proposal would apply to Quantica Infrastructure’s planned Big Sky Digital Infrastructure Campus near Broadview. Proponents must collect 16,650 valid signatures to qualify the initiative for the November ballot.[41] On August 4, county officials said the initiative would not appear on the November ballot because proponents missed a July 13 signature-submission deadline after the county elections office had previously told the group that signatures were due August 12. The group, which had collected about 10,000 signatures, continued gathering signatures and said it was considering a legal challenge.[42] On August 15, a district judge ruled that the signature-submission deadline had already passed.[43]

New York

  • Massena, New York: Residents launched a petition asking the Massena Town Board to authorize a ballot measure on a three-year moratorium on the approval, permitting, or construction of new or expanded data centers. Petition organizers said they needed 174 signatures, equal to 5% of town residents who voted in the 2022 gubernatorial election, but planned to collect 300 to 400 signatures before presenting the petitions to the town board on August 19.[44] The board did not authorize the referendum.

Nevada

  • Boulder City, Nevada: A citizen initiative that would have required majority voter approval for agreements to construct data centers larger than 10,000 square feet on city-owned land did not qualify for the ballot. Proponents collected 1,284 signatures, short of the 1,541 required, and therefore did not formally submit the petition for verification by the June 26 deadline.[45]

Oklahoma

  • Claremore, Oklahoma: On May 29, 2026, Ron Durbin filed referendums against three ordinances that authorized a data center project, called Project Mustang.[46] Durbin was required to collect 356 signatures for each petition. On June 17, Durbin reported, "We got 702 on the first, 730 on the second, 763 on the third, so we more than doubled the number that was required of registered Claremore city voters."[47] On June 25, the city government announced that the petitions were invalid because proponents did not file a copy of the petitions with the city clerk before obtaining signatures from voters.[48]

Texas

  • Lubbock, Texas: Save Lubbock submitted an initiative petition seeking an 18-month moratorium on high-water-use and high-energy-use data centers in the city. The group submitted signatures for the petition on August 3, 2026. The petitioners were required to submit 2,410 signatures.[49][50] On September 23, Save Lubbock's Stephen Sanders announced he would not move forward with the initiative, following the city council's decision to start a 90-day moratorium process.[51]
  • Taylor, Texas: The HALT Taylor Data Centers campaign submitted an initiative petition seeking to amend the city’s land development code and prohibit data centers in existing zoning districts unless the city created a zoning district specifically for digital infrastructure uses. After reviewing the proposal, the city determined that it could not be adopted through the initiative process or placed on the ballot because Texas law requires zoning changes to undergo Planning and Zoning Commission review, notice to affected property owners, and public hearings rather than approval through a popular vote.[52]

Utah

  • Box Elder County, Utah: On May 5, 2026, Box Elder Accountability Referendum (BEAR) filed two referendum petitions with the Box Elder County Clerk's office challenging Resolutions 26-11 and 26-12, which the Box Elder County Commission had approved on May 4. The resolutions granted the county's consent for the Military Installation Development Authority (MIDA) to establish the 40,000-acre, 9-gigawatt hyperscale data center called the Stratos Project.[53][54] Proponents would have needed to collect signatures from 5,422 of the county's registered voters.[55][56] On May 28, the Box Elder County Attorney's Office rejected the referendum proposals, saying that the resolutions were administrative actions rather than laws, and therefore could not be put to a public vote.[57][58] BEAR appealed the decision to the Utah First Judicial District Court.[59][60]

Wyoming

  • Cheyenne, Wyoming: Katherine McAdams and M. Lee Hasenauer launched a referendum campaign to overturn Ordinance No. 4687, which annexed and rezoned land on the city’s southeastern edge for a proposed Microsoft data center expansion. The Cheyenne City Council approved the ordinance on July 13, 2026. Under Wyoming law, petitioners must collect signatures from at least 10% of registered city voters within 20 days of the ordinance’s passage. Supporters needed to gather 2,739 valid signatures by August 2.[61] Proponents reported filing about 3,200 signatures.[62]
