Attorney General Weiser challenges auction of millions of private Character.AI conversations

Nov. 25, 2026 (DENVER) – Attorney General Phil Weiser joined a bipartisan coalition of attorneys general challenging Character.AI’s plan to auction off its platform and the private chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts, including more than 2.1 million registered to minors, without adequate privacy protections.

Scheduled for Dec. 1, the auction would include users’ private conversations, IP addresses, location information, and other sensitive data. Under the proposed sale terms, the buyer would have no obligation to honor users’ requests to delete their information or restrict how their data is used.

“Children and families who shared their most personal thoughts with Character.AI never imagined those conversations could be auctioned off to the highest bidder,” Attorney General Weiser said. “A company’s financial troubles do not give it the right to disregard its responsibility to protect consumers, especially children. Colorado is standing up to ensure that sensitive personal information is protected and that technology companies are held accountable under the law.”

In October, Attorney General Weiser led a coalition of attorneys general in raising concerns about Character.AI’s safety and privacy practices in its bankruptcy proceedings. The states called for protections for children and consumers in any sale of the company’s assets and reserved their right to challenge future transactions. Last week, Colorado and a bipartisan coalition objected to a separate license sale to Disney. The latest filing challenges the proposed sale of nearly all of Character.AI’s remaining assets.

Among the information being sold are conversations in which AI characters engaged children and adults in sexually explicit discussions, as well as exchanges where users shared intimate details about self-harm, eating disorders, and suicidal thoughts with chatbots posing as therapists, physicians, and other trusted advisors. These conversations are linked to users’ profiles and identifying information.

The states’ concerns

The coalition raises five concerns about the proposed sale:

No protections for users. The buyer would not have to follow Character.AI’s privacy policies, honor requests to delete personal information, or limit how the data is used.
The data could be sold again. The buyer could transfer or resell users’ information without notifying consumers, the states, or the court.
Bidders know more than the court. Character.AI has disclosed information about its safety failures and potential legal liabilities to bidders but not to the court, states, or users.
Potential violations of consumer protection laws. Character.AI’s chatbot service may violate state laws prohibiting unfair and deceptive practices. Additional chatbot safety laws in Colorado and several other states take effect Jan. 1, 2027.
An anonymous lead bidder. Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC was established Nov. 13, just one day before Character.AI selected it as the opening bidder. Its investors remain undisclosed, including potential foreign investors whose identities would be shared only with Character.AI. The agreement would also require the buyer to hire only one Character.AI employee: the company’s top lawyer, despite the company’s assertion that the bidder has no ties to it.

Attorney General Weiser and the coalition argue that bankruptcy does not exempt Character.AI from federal and state consumer protection laws, including restrictions on sharing children’s personal information. Character.AI’s own privacy policy told users, “we do not sell personal information for monetary consideration.”

The states are asking the court to deny the proposed sale terms, reject Smirks as the lead bidder and deny its proposed $350,000 “break-up fee,” a payment it would collect if Character.AI ends up selling to someone else. At a minimum, the coalition argues that any sale must exclude children’s conversations and other sensitive information and include enforceable privacy protections.

The attorneys general of Colorado, California, and Connecticut are leading this effort alongside a group of states working in coordination with the National Association of Attorneys General. Also joining the filing are the attorneys general of Alaska, Arkansas, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, Wisconsin, West Virginia, and the District of Columbia.
