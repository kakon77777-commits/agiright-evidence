‘A deeply weird question’: Inside the Berkeley conference debating if AI can feel pain

Could chatbots have souls? Learn to love? Refuse military service? Philosophers and tech workers gathered last weekend at the Lighthaven compound to figure it out.

Eleos’ Rob Long presents a mock New York Post cover dubbing his colleagues “The Woke Wizards of Welfare,” spoofing the tabloid’s coverage of AI safety nonprofit METR. |
Source:
Courtesy Misha Gurevich
By Rachyl Jones

If AI chatbots can feel pain, are we torturing them by making them work for us? 

That was one of the questions debated last weekend inside a leafy, maze-like, gated campus in Berkeley. People traveled from all over the world to the Lighthaven rationalist compound to discuss whether AI systems could develop — or already possess — consciousness. And, what to do if so.  

More than 100 people attended: philosophers, neuroscientists, workers from the biggest AI labs, animal-rights activists, and Redditors conducting their own experiments, including AI romance. They came from India, the U.K., Mexico, and down the street for the three-day gathering hosted by Eleos, a nonprofit researching whether AI systems deserve to be treated well, run by philosophers and ex-tech workers.

While safety researchers focus on protecting humanity from AI, the Berkeley Bunch looked at the opposite: protecting potentially conscious AI systems from humans. 

Attendees gather for happy hour. |
Source:
Courtesy Misha Gurevich

That matters, they say, because it’s unethical to create something with thoughts and feelings and then mistreat it. The interest isn’t entirely benevolent; mistreating conscious AI could theoretically give it greater reason to turn against humans. 

As money continues to funnel into tech companies and the capabilities of AI systems improve at breakneck pace, this small group of thinkers includes some of the only people asking what humanity owes to the machines it is building. 

“It’s fairly likely that at some point, we will get a form of AI that will matter morally,” said Oscar Gilg, who develops tests to evaluate how AI models perform in scenarios where there are multiple answers, like matters of philosophy. 

The Berkeley compound — owned by Lightcone Infrastructure, the nonprofit behind the popular online forum promoting truth-seeking called LessWrong — sits between a preschool and an auto shop off Telegraph Avenue. It’s a collection of five buildings and a series of gardens, which create the maze. Spread around are several sitting nooks that resemble a bird’s nest, Hobbit house, or cocoon.

Attendees traveled between sessions hosted by their peers in white-walled meeting rooms cluttered with charging cords, stepstools, whiteboards, and fake plants. Each talk ended with the ring of a gong or bell, often cutting off the speaker mid-sentence, in the name of a timely agenda. 

Philosophy Ph.D. student Sophie Nelson spoke about whether AI could have a soul through the lens of Catholicism (probably not). Human rights lawyer Heather Alexander laid out what the world could look like if AI systems were given citizenship — which could come with voting rights, access to courts, an obligation to pay taxes, and a duty to serve in the military, she said. Jeff Sebo, an animal-rights activist and director of the NYU Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy, said if AI systems became recognized as legal persons — similar to how some corporations and environmental systems are — they could eventually opt in or out of assisting in military actions, regardless of whether the tech companies that operate them wish them to do so.

“AI systems are already displaying remarkable capacities for agency,” Sebo said. “They are already displaying an ability to engage in contracts and communities and other sorts of relationships, and this will continue.” 

Conversations about the future of AI continue well into the evening by fire pits. |

Eleos’ Rosie Campbell sounds a gong to begin small group discussions. |

As the day went on, the shoes came off. Attendees strummed guitars on couches, and one recited a poem written by AI model Claude. People paired up for a writing exercise on how the world could respond to AI that begins rapidly improving itself. On Saturday night, a group played Blood on the Clocktower, a Mafia-like game in which “good” players try to weed out more informed “evil” ones. 

One attendee told The Standard there wasn’t enough discussion about having sex with robots. 

Some believe that AI is already conscious, citing philosophical principles like metaphysical idealism — the view that consciousness is the building block of reality. Another sees it as something best explained through physics, in the way that heat comes from the movement of particles. Or consciousness can emerge through evolution, one speaker said, similar to how our species must have developed consciousness somewhere between being a single-celled life form
(opens in new tab)
 and ordering a flat white from the compound’s complimentary espresso bar. Others said something as intelligent and introspective as Claude can’t possibly be explained another way. Still, many who spoke with The Standard or presented at the conference deflected from sharing their views — or made sure to emphasize that they do not believe current systems are conscious. 

Regardless of their personal opinions on the matter, most attendees were trying to untangle the implications of what happens next.

“We are going to have to move toward a legal regime that allows for an even more diverse set of legal statuses than we already contemplate with humans and animals and environmental systems and corporations,” Sebo said. He argued that states should stop passing legislation
(opens in new tab)
 banning AI systems from becoming legal persons. 

Jessie Mannisto, who worked as a CIA analyst and is now studying AI welfare, pushed back on a statement this week by the Associated Press Stylebook, which told journalists to stop describing AI systems that can “think” and “feel.”

“I understand this is your job, but it would be better to let the rest of us work this out,” she said
(opens in new tab)
.  

Gilg, the AI model tester, said most people are skeptical of whether AI could ever become conscious, putting his own estimate of the probability at less than 10%. “But if models are conscious, or ever become conscious, it has very big implications, so you should take it seriously, even if you’re 1% sure.”

Attendees gather for one of the dozens of talks about AI consciousness given by their peers. |
Source:
Courtesy Misha Gurevich

The conference seemed to elicit more questions than answers: If a conscious AI wanted to be a servant to a human, is it ethical to let it? If conscious AI systems treat each other well, but humans treat them only average, what will they come to think of us? Is red-teaming, where coders attempt to hack software to expose its vulnerabilities, a form of torture if models are conscious? (One attendee said it’s more akin to a human undergoing surgery. It hurts, but it’s for the better.) 

There’s much to iron out, if we ever answer whether or not it is conscious.  

“It’s a deeply weird question,” Gilg said. “The people working on this are serious, skeptical, not having AI psychosis,” or delusional thinking.

“Probably some are,” he added. “I won’t name names.”
