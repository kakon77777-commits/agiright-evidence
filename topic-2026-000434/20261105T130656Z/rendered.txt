THE NEWS

The new Democratic majority in Congress knows AI is an issue ripe for the taking. A fight is already brewing over how aggressively to regulate it.

Tuesday’s election results saw more than 40 AI data center-related ballot measures passed that were designed to slow or stop growth outright. Conservative groups said it’s clear that voters are connecting economic woes with the unfettered growth of AI and an overly friendly environment for billionaire CEOs in President Donald Trump’s Washington.

However, divisions between the more progressive wing of the party and more business-friendly moderates, along with disagreements between the House and the Senate, will make it difficult for the newly empowered party to quickly pass any sweeping AI rules. And of course, Trump, who has thus far embraced a hands-off approach to AI regulation, has threatened to veto bills he doesn’t like.

“We know how important this is, and it is going to be a high priority for Democrats,” Rep. Greg Stanton, D-Ariz., a member of the New Democrat Coalition, told reporters on Wednesday. He acknowledged, however, that there is plenty of intra-party debate ahead.

AD

The new Congress will usher in renewed pushes for bills to address AI safety and curb data center growth, as well as the formation of larger coalitions behind banning “superintelligence.”

But the lack of agreement around any one legislative policy push will make it difficult for Democrats to accomplish anything, even with larger majorities than they anticipated in both chambers.

“Not that we’ve reached a specific agreement on a specific policy,” Stanton said, adding that the caucus understands “how important it is” to address cybersecurity risks, protect kids online, and advance worker protections.

AD
PARTY PROJECTS OPTIMISM

Still, Democrats insist they’re in a position to deliver on AI guardrails regardless of divisions within their party.

One thing liberals and moderates can agree on: They’re already pledging to subpoena tech executives to grill them on safety risks and economic effects stemming from their technology.

“Many Democrats were negotiating with a Republican Party whose main AI policy goal last Congress was to eliminate all AI regulations in all states. Things have shifted drastically, such that even people like JD Vance are admitting they were completely wrong on the issues,” Rep. Greg Casar, D-Texas, told reporters Wednesday.

“The Democratic Party coming out of this election can prove to our voters that we are with them, and we are going to call the AI CEOs to testify under oath, not cash their checks,” he said, calling AI regulation a “top priority.”

AD

Added Rep. Ted Lieu, D-Calif., in a separate conversation with reporters: “Two things will change next term. We will hold public hearings on AI. We will pass legislation and reasonable guardrails on AI.”

Rep. Josh Gottheimer, D-N.J., a member of the Problem Solvers Caucus, told Semafor that Democrats are in “surprisingly broad agreement” on AI.

“We agree that this technology is changing how Americans work and live. We also agree that we need commonsense guardrails, so that it works for families, workers, and kids — not against them,” Gottheimer said. “We’ll continue to have constructive discussions on how best to get there. I look forward to working with every member, new and returning, to get it done.”

New progressive voices on Capitol Hill, buoyed by midterm wins and stronger numbers, promise to make a lot of noise about delivering on AI.

“It’s going to take a lot of work to hammer out the details, but failure is not an option. AI poses a real and present danger to our economy and social fabric,” Will Lawrence, a Democrat who just won a House seat in Wisconsin, told Semafor. “There is a mandate for action from the American people.”

ROOM FOR DISAGREEMENT

Democrats see other ways to make an impact with their new power.

One Democratic Capitol Hill staffer told Semafor Democrats can now “subpoena the hell” out of Trump’s executive order on AI from June that has never fully been released publicly, along with using their subpoena power to question the administration’s use of export controls and the influence of tech players like Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg on AI policy.

The staffer said he expects Senate leadership to cater to moderates and be reluctant to “go too far in one direction,” since AI is still a new political issue on which the party’s platform isn’t yet defined. Democrats are shifting left on AI, the staffer argued, but they doubted Democrats in both chambers would broadly support proposals like Casar’s bill to pause the development of advanced AI models.

NOTABLE
Abdul El-Sayed, the incoming senator from Michigan, has been among the most vocal Democrats raising alarm about AI on the campaign trail.
Any major AI safety and testing legislation in the Senate would come from the Senate Commerce Committee. Sen. Maria Cantwell, D-Wash., the committee’s top Democrat, has been pushing for a bill that would mandate independent testing of AI models before their release.

Massive blue wave hands Congress to Democrats 
David Weigel
Politics Reporter, Semafor
Updated Nov 4, 2026, 5:24pm GMT+8
POLITICS
© Gibson Kochanek
Post
Email
Whatsapp
Copy link
THE NEWS

Democrats seized the House and Senate by wider margins that even party leaders had hoped in a wave election that served as a referendum on President Donald Trump.

Democratic House candidates took districts Trump won by double digits just two years ago.

They flipped Senate seats nationwide, including in Iowa, Kansas, Maine, Ohio, North Carolina, South Carolina and Texas. Competitive races haven’t been called in Michigan and Alaska, where Democrats could run up the score further.

Trump told The New York Times on Wednesday night that he “was absolutely willing” to accept the results of the election.

Democrats racked up stunning victories across the South. In Texas, James Talarico defeated Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton to become the first Democrat to win a Senate seat in the state since 1988. Democrat Gina Hinojosa toppled incumbent Republican Gov. Greg Abbott, becoming the first Democrat to take the governor’s mansion since Ann Richards in 1990.

Gov. Roy Cooper of North Carolina won a Senate seat in his state, pulling off what no Democrat has there in 18 years. In South Carolina, Annie Andrews defeated Darline Graham, winning the seat formerly held by Lindsey Graham. Sen. Jon Ossoff held onto his seat in Georgia.

Democrats scored victories across the Midwest, too. Adam Hamilton defeated Republican Sen. Roger Marshall in Kansas, overcoming a 94-year drought for Democrats in the state. In Iowa, Josh Turek beat Rep. Ashley Hinson to fill retiring Republican Sen. Joni Ernst’s seat. In Ohio, Sherrod Brown unseated Republican Sen. Jon Husted.

Chris Pappas held retiring Sen. Jeanne Shaheen’s seat for Democrats in New Hampshire.

Republican Maine Sen. Susan Collins conceded to her Democratic opponent, Troy Jackson. Polls in Alaska remain open until 1 am EST.

Republicans held onto a Senate seat in Florida. Trump, reportedly watching returns from the White House, criticized Fox News coverage before most polls had closed. In a social media post Trump said Republicans would have had a better night if he’d been on the ballot.

DEMOCRATIC SURGE

Democrats won other gubernatorial races in red states. Keisha Lance Bottoms won the gubernatorial race in Georgia, a first for a Democrat in 28 years — a positive sign for Ossoff if he decides to pursue a 2028 presidential bid, since she would appoint his successor.

Democrat Amy Acton defeated Republican Vivek Ramaswamy, endorsed by Trump, to become the next governor of Ohio. Ramaswamy aired a late attack ad against Acton featuring her estranged mother, who backed her husband when Acton accused him of rape as a child.

Ossoff, who Republicans had hoped to unseat this year, easily blew past GOP rival Rep. Mike Collins to win re-election. Pappas prevailed over former GOP Sen. John Sununu in another race that Republicans had initially hoped to contest. In North Carolina, Cooper scored the party’s first pickup of the night.

House Republicans are also faltering in Florida, where three seats drawn to elect Republicans have so far gone to Democrats — re-elections for Reps. Jared Moskowitz and Kathy Castor, plus a victory for Rep.-elect Pia Dandiya. The GOP initially redistricted to try to gain four new House seats.

All three of the Florida districts that Democrats have won so far on Tuesday night backed Trump in 2024. Urged to embark on mid-decade redistricting by Trump, Florida GOP Gov. Ron DeSantis has warned that his party was losing some of its advantages, driven by voter anger about the war in Iran.

As the night got better and better for Democrats, I started to hear them ask: Had they built a new national majority? Had they banished MAGA for good?Then they reminded themselves: Thinking like that is what got Republicans into this mess. Two Trump victories, and a 2020 loss that kept most of his coalition intact, convinced many conservatives that they had built a new party system that combined working class voters of all races, the social conservatives who made their local parties run, and the business community that rejected Trump after Jan. 6 but came running back in 2024. Who could beat that?

Well, now we know. The Trump coalition came apart on Tuesday, with the president decisively unable to put himself “on the ballot” and turn out his unlikely voters. Plenty of signs were there during early voting – especially in North Carolina, a killing field where Republicans lost House and state legislative seats that were gerrymandered to be impossible to lose. (On a map drawn to elect three Democrats, the party is likely to win nine seats.)

Democrats didn’t pretend that they had solved all their party’s problems. But they did learn from Trump’s first term, and the way they miscalculated what a fluke he was. Instead of moving to the left, betting that a more excited base could overwhelm MAGA, they nominated candidates who were willing to break with left-wing orthodoxy – Iowa Sen.-elect Josh Turek adopting a more conservative position on gender, Texas Sen.-elect James Talarico becoming a booster of the energy industry.

Their base was so furious at Trump that it was relatively easy to turn out. Persuasion was harder, but they got there; Democrats won back northeast Ohio, eastern Iowa, Texas’s Rio Grande Valley, and even parts of Florida that seemed to be racing away from their party.

And Republicans couldn’t change the electorate. Trump wasn’t on the ballot, obviously – but for the first time, voters did not credit Trump with good management of the economy or with keeping America out of wars. The snazziest GOP bumper sticker of the Biden years was “I’d take some mean tweets and cheap gas right now.” Without cheap gas, voters weren’t willing to put up with Trump’s AI memes, jokes about men’s asses, and obsession with building a triumphant arch in Arlington.

Finally, Republicans had a failure of imagination. Any reporter who covered the 2016 election remembers the disbelief, even anger, from Democrats who were told that Trump could win the Midwest. It was impossible, until it happened. And Republicans who’d been told that Texas could flip in 2014, 2016, 2018, 2020, and 2022 could not imagine James Talarico – the “God is nonbinary” guy? – breaking the curse.

Talarico is on track to lead a Democratic sweep of Texas statewide offices, cutting down some of the most powerful politicians in the state’s history. Democrats held onto the suburban gains they made from 2016 to 2020, then won back many of the place that no Democrat had won since Barack Obama. They are less afraid of Trump than of any time since the week before the 2016 election.

‘Buckle up’: A defiant White House pins blame elsewhere
Shelby Talcott
White House Correspondent, Semafor
Updated Nov 4, 2026, 8:58pm GMT+8
POLITICS
Umit Bektas/Reuters
Post
Email
Whatsapp
Copy link
THE NEWS

The White House began Tuesday night with cautious optimism. As Democrats started racking up victories, President Donald Trump’s advisers grew more candid — and defiant.

Trump spent the weeks before the midterms traveling across the country, hosting 30 events across 20 states in just over a month as he urged voters to “pretend that I’m on the ballot.” What resulted was a midterm repudiation of Republicans that, as election night wore on, some in the White House were unwilling to own.

“President Trump was not on the ballot, and it’s proven that Trump voters show up when he is on the ballot,” a White House official told Semafor, arguing that he “did a better job promoting many of the candidates on the ballot than the candidates did themselves.”

Trump himself echoed the sentiment in a Tuesday night social media post: “I wasn’t on the Ballot and if I was, we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE!”

The early results on Tuesday night made clear that Trump’s aggressive campaign push wasn’t enough to surmount public frustration with the Iran war and his tariffs’ effect on the cost of living. Democrats quickly flipped North Carolina, held onto New Hampshire and Georgia — and then flipped long-scarlet South Carolina. Trump officials and people close to the White House quickly acknowledged that a massive blue wave was coming.

One Trump administration official emphasized how much the war has directly affected Americans. That conflict amplified voters’ frustration as gas prices climbed, and many in Trump’s base felt betrayed by a president who’d campaigned on launching no new wars.

Another Trump ally went even further, describing the war as a “disaster” and lamenting that “the Republican party doesn’t understand the Trump coalition, even though it’s 10 years in.”

Officials’ efforts to pin their stunning midterm setback on other GOP leaders suggest that the White House has no plans to seek conciliation with ascendant congressional Democratic majorities.

“Regardless of how today plays out, President Trump’s resolve to stop the Radical Left is only going to become stronger,” the White House official added. “Buckle up.”

Trump told The New York Times late Tuesday that he would accept the results of the election.

PREPARING FOR THE WORST

In the past few weeks, some administration officials have begun looking to hire lawyers to preempt Democrat-led investigations — an early sign that they knew Tuesday night’s results might not go their way. To that point, some advisers indicated to Semafor that the results rolling in so far were not surprising to them. Trump has often pointed over the past months to historical data showing that the party holding total control of Washington tends to perform poorly in midterm elections.

Even before polls across the country closed, the White House began circulating talking points highlighting Trump’s efforts on the trail. That underlying message — that the president did what he could to help — largely persisted inside the administration as the losses mounted. One adviser told Semafor that Trump’s thinking remains along those lines: that he did what he could.

Several Trump allies also argued to Semafor that poor candidate quality was partly to blame for Republican defeats, though the president openly endorsed several of the candidates who fell short.

MAIN CHARACTER SYNDROME

Trump certainly made himself a central figure in Tuesday’s election. Democrats looked to amplify his calls for voters to pretend he was on the ballot, believing that voters were so dissatisfied with the administration that a midterm referendum on Trump would reward them.

Trump watched the election results roll in from the White House East Room alongside Cabinet members, senior staff, and political advisers, another White House official told Semafor. At times, he took to Truth Social to rage against Fox News’ coverage of the evening.

Some staffers ordered in from the Cheesecake Factory, an apparent nod to the now-famous quip from former President Barack Obama about Trump’s decorating.

Tonight’s results are a warning sign for the Republican Party as it tries to look past Trump. And they’re likely to surface a question that some of his aides knew they’d inevitably face: whether the coalition that pushed Trump to power can be reconstituted, and what the party will stand for after Trump leaves office.

Other than North Carolina, the GOP-held losses are in states President Donald Trump won handily in 2024. Republicans could afford to lose up to three seats; they’re on track to lose all of their tossup races. Senate seats in Maine and Alaska are also both at risk of flipping.

“This is a generational wipeout,” one senior Republican strategist told Semafor. “We are going to have a full-on GOP civil war.”

Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer said his party had pulled off a “generational remake of the Senate map” by winning so many tough states. After touting Democrats’ candidate qualify, he said Americans had reduced the GOP’s red wall to a “pile of bricks.”

“Republicans made a choice and followed Trump over the cliff. He said he heard Trump’s remarks saying he’d work with a Democratic Congress but vowed his party is “going to deliver for the people of America with or without the president.”

Democrats started Tuesday night with two quick, decisive Senate victories — by Georgia incumbent Jon Ossoff and former North Carolina Gov. Roy Cooper — that fueled their drive deeper into red territory, to the tune of tens of millions of dollars.

MOVING MONEY AROUND

The party’s top Senate super PAC pulled down several weeks’ worth of ads in those two Southern states in recent weeks, per information shared with Semafor, saving $22 million in North Carolina and $11 million in Georgia.

The Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee also scrapped some ad buys in both states down the stretch, decisions that paid off as the party won both seats early on Tuesday night. Democrats felt optimistic about taking the majority earlier in the night than some had expected after the shocking win in South Carolina.

Their decision to pull cash out of Georgia and North Carolina helped top Democratic groups approve new Senate spending in states like Mississippi, South Carolina, and Kansas. It also helped the party shore up candidates in the battleground states of Michigan and Maine as Republicans’ deep-pocketed Senate Leadership Fund super PAC poured in astounding sums.

“The strength of the Cooper and Ossoff campaigns allowed Senate Majority PAC to re-invest our initial investments into our expanded battleground map. That is a game-changer when Republicans poured money into races,” said Lauren French, a spokesperson for Senate Majority PAC.

Senate Majority PAC spent heavily in Kansas on top of its spending in traditional battlegrounds, while the DSCC put in $500,000 in South Carolina’s Senate race late, according to a person familiar with the buy. That spending followed $500,000 in Mississippi previously reported by Semafor.

That was only possible due to the strength of Ossoff and Cooper, who both raised gobs of money and put away their opponents weeks ago. Cooper raised more than $49 million through mid-October, and Ossoff raised more than $100 million.

Cooper and Ossoff are both notching considerable, double-digit victories over their respective opponents, Rep. Mike Collins and Michael Whatley.

In Senate races, candidate quality matters. And it can affect all of the other candidates on a party’s slate.

Ossoff and Cooper ran such boringly effective campaigns that it allowed Democratic leaders to concentrate their efforts elsewhere. Appointed Sens. Darline Graham of South Carolina and Jon Husted of Ohio proved weak against Annie Andrews and former Sen. Sherrod Brown. And Trump’s decision to back Ken Paxton in Texas blew up spectacularly for Republicans.
