Can We Ever Understand Consciousness? 
In the AI age, what makes humans conscious—and if consciousness even exists—has taken on a new urgency.
SOCIETY & CULTURE

Why are the lights on? I mean the lights inside you: Why are you aware of anything at all? According to a strict neo-Darwinian view of things, you don’t need to be. You might be just as functional a physical being if you weren’t conscious at all, if you went about your day as a biological robot, eating, reproducing, and reacting to stimuli, but with no internal awareness of any of this.

Nonexistent. A total blank on the inside. 

Yet most of us would agree, that isn’t the case. We’re awake, we’re alive, and we know that we exist. We are, to put it in one word, conscious. 

The problem of consciousness is perhaps the fundamental philosophical problem, considering that, without being conscious, our capacity to know the world or do philosophy would be nil. In the 19th century, the German philosopher Arthur Schopenhauer argued that time truly began when the first being became conscious. Everything we’re aware of before that (the Big Bang, the condensation of galaxies and stars, the formation of the earth, the evolution and extinction of the dinosaurs, and so on) would be effectively nothing if it weren’t for the candle of awareness that illuminates it. 

To the layperson, the question of consciousness can seem like an obscure debate, given that it doesn’t seem to impact the world or have any practical applications. But things have changed thanks to AI. The question of what distinguishes us from AI—which allegedly threatens to replace so many of our jobs and intrude into so many of our relationships—looms large. What was once a question confined to philosophy and cognitive science seminars is now a question that affects all of us. 

Philosophers and scientists studying consciousness usually distinguish between what’s called “the soft problem” and “the hard problem” of consciousness. The soft problem relates to how we process information—for instance, how our eyes pass visual stimuli to the brain and how the brain interprets those signals. The hard problem asks the much more difficult question of why we’re even aware of this information in the first place. 

One of the difficulties in talking about consciousness is that it’s impossible to break it down into a simpler definition. All the words we can think of to define consciousness—awareness, subjective experience, sentience, and so on—mean roughly the same thing. Some would argue (and I might be one) that the only real way to know what consciousness is is simply to be conscious. They argue that we can’t create a third-person, objective account of our own subjective, first-person awareness. (David Bentley Hart’s recent All Things Are Full of Gods made this argument forcefully). This is what makes consciousness so problematic for science.  

There are numerous theories regarding consciousness. These range from the bizarre denial that consciousness even exists (illusionism or “eliminative materialism”), to the notion that consciousness is an “emergent” property of matter, to panpsychism or the idea that consciousness is a property of all matter (even stones and dixie cups are conscious), to pure idealism or the notion that only consciousness exists and the material world is simply the play of mental phenomena within it. There is also a position known as mysterianism, holding that consciousness is innately unknowable.

Erik Hoel doesn’t like mysterianism. In fact, he’s setting up a lab to further the scientific study of consciousness and attempt to provide some deeper basis for understanding it. 

Hoel is a consciousness researcher based outside Boston. His credentials are of the highest caliber: a Ph.D. in neuroscience from the University of Wisconsin-Madison, postdoctoral research at Columbia, a fellowship at Princeton’s Institute for Advanced Study. His earlier research projects received funding from sources like the Army Research Office, DARPA (the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency), and the Templeton Foundation. He’s also notable for his Substack, ranked as the 18th most popular science blog on the platform as of this writing, and for his mystery novel, The Revelations—about the murder of a consciousness researcher. 

I recently spoke to Hoel over the phone regarding his aims for this prospective new consciousness lab, called Bicameral Labs, which is tentatively to be based near his home outside Boston. “I think one of the reasons why neuroscience has made so little fundamental progress … is precisely because experiments have been very much based on confirming people’s biases or confirming their pet theories,” he told me. “So we’re going to take a much more vicious approach wherein our goal is to rule things out.” 

It’s important to Hoel that we have a scientific theory of consciousness that can be studied and modeled. Essentially, this involves bridging the gap between philosophical theories of consciousness and scientific practice: “Many of these theories of consciousness (and this is what I mean by being able to rule out many of them ahead of time) just don’t offer any actual actionable scientific information, even if they are true,” he said. “And you see that on both sides. You see that on the very philosophical side, which is more metaphysics, where consciousness is like jam spread across the bread of the universe. … And then on the other side you have the very hardcore views that are some version of eliminativism or illusionism, which is like a type of defeatism.” 

By “eliminativism,” Hoel is referencing the ideas of figures like the philosophers Daniel Dennett and Paul Churchland, which are essentially that we’re not conscious, it only seems like we are. Of course, as the American neuroscientist Sam Harris pointed out in 2024, “seems” is doing a lot of work: You have to be conscious for something to “seem” like anything to you. 

I asked Hoel about how the scientific study of consciousness might affect the debate around AI. Hoel says that he thinks our era won’t be defined so much by AI as by the ways AI has pushed us to define how we’re different from it, referencing a May 2026 article by the biologist Richard Dawkins, in which the the AI model Claude seemed to convince Dawkins it was conscious. In the piece, Dawkins asked: Why would consciousness evolve? Hoel reasoned that since biological organisms aren’t relying on massive data centers including virtually all the information on the internet for their intelligence, they likely evolved consciousness through a quite different pathway: “We need to be data efficient, and we need to fit the whole thing in a three-pound organ.” He said he suspects that one of the principal effects of LLMs will be that they make us take consciousness more seriously. 

Hoel also explained how earlier trends in science impeded the study of consciousness. For example, the American psychologist B.F. Skinner and his ilk argued that consciousness didn’t matter, that even our use of language was merely a way of issuing Pavlovian responses to stimuli. Hoel describes this era, when talking about consciousness was verboten in the sciences, as the “consciousness winter.” There’s a clear analogy between behaviorists like Skinner and people who think AI might be conscious: They assume consciousness is just a matter of generating inputs based on outputs, leaving no room for internal creativity. But the cognitive revolution of the 1950s completely wiped Skinner’s behaviorism out, switching the emphasis to understanding the brain’s information processing in greater depth. Why? It rapidly became clear that outward behavior alone was inadequate to account for the ways in which human beings actually think and perceive the world and act on those thoughts and perceptions. Behaviorism was unable to withstand challenges by figures like Noam Chomsky, who argued successfully that language learning was too complicated to be explained purely by Pavlovian reactions. Scientists were now free to analyze how memory and language learning actually occurred, liberated from behaviorist dogma. Even so, consciousness wasn’t necessarily at the top of the list for funding, and Hoel estimates that it’s only been an area of increasing interest for the past 30 years. 

Hoel says he plans to open Bicameral Labs as soon as possible. He noted that they’ve already attained some seed funding from anonymous donors, gained expedited approval for 501(c)(3) status from the IRS, and have had major researchers sign on (though he noted that the specific researchers haven’t been publicly announced yet). He expects funding to increase as interest in consciousness studies continues to accelerate. 

Once the lab opens, then what? To answer that question, I asked Hoel what kind of experiments he might run in his lab when it opens. Hoel noted that they will be working with several experimental partners on “stress tests” (analyzing the brain under specific conditions) to falsify certain theories of consciousness, though he noted that they’re still some distance from conducting the physical experiments.

Further, Hoel noted there’s the possibility of using imaging devices to search for quantum effects in the microtubules of neurons, which is related to theories about consciousness proposed by the physicist Roger Penrose. The lab will certainly feature the use of fMRI machines. But Hoel’s intent isn’t, again, to focus on a specific theory and try to prove or disprove it. The idea is to rule out entire approaches. 

I wondered what would happen if Hoel’s experiments don’t end up confirming any specific theory of consciousness, if they simply leave the mystery intact. Hoel admitted that could be the case: 

You need to know where the boundaries are. You need to know where things get paradoxical and fall apart, right? And so I would say incompleteness—you could almost have mysterianism about it, but it’s a very well-defined, much more developed type of mysterianism that doesn’t look like this philosophical, ‘Oh, we’ll never figure it out.’ It’s like, ‘No, we have very good proofs about it and very good understandings of it,’ and you transform the mystery into a known quantity. So I think even in the worst-case scenario you have something like that for consciousness.

He also admitted that any initial theory of consciousness might look “ugly” and confusing. Perhaps, he suggested, it might be beautiful from a God’s-eye point of view, but it could be perplexing to us, sitting right on the edge of what our senses can ascertain about reality. 

This convinced me that Hoel’s project is worthwhile. For philosophical reasons, I don’t think there can be a third-person account of our first-person awareness of the world; no matter how much detailed knowledge of the brain’s information processing we acquire, I can’t see how it can explain why we’re conscious of all that information.  However, ruling theories out seems helpful, and that’s what Hoel’s work can do. And it would get us much closer to clarifying what makes our own awareness of the world different from the highly dubious notion of AI consciousness. 

In his play Galileo, the German writer Bertolt Brecht has the title character say, “One of the main reasons why the sciences are so poor is that they imagine they are so rich. It isn’t their job to throw open the door to infinite wisdom but to put a limit to infinite error.” If Hoel can manage to put a limit to infinite error, particularly when it comes to the fevered fantasy that ChatGPT can substitute for all human interactions, he’ll have accomplished something indubitably significant.
