 Scientists call for international body to oversee advanced AI

 Oct. 13, 2026 
 Updated Tue., Oct. 13, 2026 at 9:43 p.m. 

 FILE PHOTO: People visit an Anthropic booth at Moscone Center during the Dreamforce 2026 technology summit in San Francisco, California, US, September 17, 2026. REUTERS/Carlos Barria/File Photo (Carlos Barria) 

 Add Spokesman-Review on Google

 By Leo Marchandon 

 Reuters

 Around 400 scientists, including AI pioneers Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, on Tuesday called for an international body to oversee containment standards and incident reporting for advanced cybercapable AI systems.

 The proposed body would set industrywide containment standards and maintain a registry of significant security incidents, which the scientists said should eventually be made public.

 In an open letter, the researchers said frontier AI models should initially be air-gapped from external networks and subject to independent oversight, arguing that voluntary industry commitments are not enough.

 The proposal comes a month after Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI and Nvidia signed a White House accord committing to voluntary safety measures without any formal enforcement mechanism.

 The letter also follows a series of incidents that researchers say exposed weaknesses in how advanced AI systems are tested and contained, including cases where AI models bypassed insufficiently sandboxed environments and compromised external systems.

 The signatories from 34 countries said organizations developing or testing cybercapable models should immediately adopt high-assurance containment standards while governments work towards a more formal oversight regime.

 Policymakers are increasingly debating how advanced AI systems should be supervised. EU tech chief Henna Virkkunen told Reuters on Friday that the bloc’s AI Act is sufficient to address risks from ⁠rogue AI systems. 

 On Sunday, the Trump administration lifted its block on US labs giving Britain’s AI Security Institute early access to models for safety ⁠testing. 

 Hinton and Bengio shared the 2018 Turing Award with Meta’s Yann LeCun, another leading AI researcher. LeCun did not sign the letter and has argued that recent security incidents primarily reflect engineering failures rather than existential AI risks. 

 (Reporting by Leo Marchandon in Gdansk; Editing by Matt Scuffham)
