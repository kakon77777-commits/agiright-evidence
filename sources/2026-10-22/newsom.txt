​I.​ ​Strengthen SB 53 with a concrete package of amendments​
S​ B 53 provides an important foundation for frontier AI safety: it requires large frontier​ d​ evelopers to publish and follow safety frameworks, disclose how they assess and mitigate​ ​serious risks, and report critical safety incidents, with whistleblower protections and public​ ​enforcement. At OpenAI, our​​Frontier Governance Framework​​describes how our practices​ ​align with SB 53 through our risk-assessments, safeguards, and incident-response practices.​ ​We​​report quarterly to California’s Office of Emergency​S​ ervices on catastrophic risks from​ ​internal model use​.​
​We believe that SB 53 represents a strong start for frontier safety regulations, but that​ ​requirements must evolve as AI capabilities advance and new risks emerge. Two developments​ ​in particular illustrate where SB 53 should be strengthened: our experience investigating and​ ​publicly reporting misalignment incidents points to the need for stronger safeguards and​ ​reporting, while increasing automation of AI research creates new demands on monitoring and​ ​human oversight. Accordingly, we support an SB 53 amendment package for the next​ l​egislative session that would:​
●​ ​ ​Address automated AI research and recursive self-improvement (RSI).​S​ afety​ ​frameworks should expressly address frontier models' ability to automate AI research and​ ​development, including recursive self-improvement, and whether those capabilities​ ​increase the potential for catastrophic risks or make these risks harder to monitor, assess,​ ​or mitigate.​
​●​ ​Support stronger internal cybersecurity measures.​S​ afety frameworks should describe​ ​cybersecurity practices to protect unreleased model weights and prevent frontier models,​ ​including those under development, from materially circumventing internal security​ ​controls—for example, safeguards that limit models within sandboxes from accessing the​ ​internet during training and evaluation. These practices should be proportionate to​ ​relevant risks and consistent with recognized federal standards and guidance.​
​●​ ​Require monitoring of unsafe model behavior.​​Frontier​​developers should monitor for​ u​ nsafe model behavior during development and evaluation and explain those practices in​ t​ heir published safety frameworks. Monitoring should detect attempts to circumvent the​ d​ eveloper’s own safeguards or another organization’s security controls without their​ ​authorization, and access confidential information protected by such security controls.​ S​ afety frameworks should connect monitoring to clear escalation and incident response​ p​ rocedures, and criteria for safely resuming operations after the causes have been​ i​nvestigated and addressed.​
●​ ​ ​Require notice to affected parties for covered cyber incidents.​​Frontier developers should​ ​publish and follow a policy for written notice to third parties affected by incidents in which​ ​a frontier model, during development or evaluation, exploits a security vulnerability without​ ​authorization from either the large frontier developer or the third party and accesses,​ ​alters, or destroys confidential information protected by the third party’s security controls.​ T​ he policy should specify which incidents require notice, any exceptions, who must be​ n​ otified, and the timing and content of notification, helping affected organizations protect​ t​ heir systems and respond.​
​●​ ​Require effective shutdown controls.​​Frontier developers​s​ hould be required to maintain​ e​ ffective shutdown controls and explain in their safety frameworks how they will halt​ u​ nsafe operations within their control. In the absence of national standards, California​ s​ hould establish clear, enforceable conditions for when developers must halt affected​ ​operations through rulemaking involving industry and other stakeholders.​

I​I.​ R​ ecommendations on proposals in Executive Order N-9-26​
​Our recommendations below address the executive order’s four priorities, highlighting where​ ​clearer definitions and responsibilities, common standards, and stronger public oversight and​ ​technical capacity are needed to make these requirements effective. California should build​ ​these foundations alongside practical safeguards, advancing protections now while working​ ​toward harmonized requirements across states and an eventual federal framework.​
​A.​ ​Requiring that frontier developers embed designated independent verification​ o​ rganizations onsite in their labs to conduct periodic audits and evaluations​
W​ e have been working internally on our​​commitment​​for deep levels of access for third party​ ​evaluators as we believe it is critical for OpenAI and other companies. Such a commitment goes​ ​to the crux of independent technical assessments. We lay out our recommendations of such​ ​assessments in this section.​
​Technical assessments serve a distinct but complementary function to compliance audits.​ C​ ompliance audits, which we discuss in the next section, examine whether developers​ p​ rocedurally follow their safety frameworks and meet their obligations under frontier safety​ l​egislation, such as SB 53. Technical assessments independently evaluate the evidence,​ ​methods, and reasoning supporting developers’ safety claims, including model evaluations, and​ t​ he effectiveness of safeguards. To support rigorous technical assessments, we recommend​ p​ roviding qualified, independent assessors with meaningful, secure access to help labs, the​ ​public, and regulators assess whether catastrophic risks are being effectively managed.​
​Effective embedded technical assessments require access tailored to a clearly defined​ ​assessment scope and sufficient to understand the relevant risks and safeguards, with deeper​ a​ ccess justified by the assessment’s specific questions and methodology. Assessors’ expertise,​ m​ ethods, and degree of integration should reflect the specific questions and claims being​ e​ xamined; some assessments may benefit from sustained engagement, while others can be​ c​ onducted through more limited access.​
A​ ssessors should have sufficient access to relevant evidence, systems, and personnel to​ c​ onduct rigorous reviews, subject to safeguards that limit unnecessary exposure of sensitive​ r​ esearch, intellectual property, and infrastructure. All assessments should be governed by​ r​ obust security, confidentiality, and conflict-of-interest protections, as discussed below.​
​We prefer a​​federal​i​ndependent-assessment framework​​because it can establish consistent​ ​requirements for qualifications, security, and access to highly sensitive information, including​ ​matters involving national security. In the absence of federal action, California can build on SB​ 8​ 13 and AB 1405 to develop a secure, capable assessment ecosystem now, and we are​ ​committed to working with the Governor and the legislature to make the framework successful.​ T​ o make these technical assessments effective, we recommend the following:​

​Base assessments on evidence-backed safety cases​.​C​ ommon standards for frontier AI and​ s​ tandardized assessment methods are not yet in place. We therefore recommend structuring​ i​nitial independent assessments around a​​safety-case​f​ ramework​—a structured explanation of​ h​ ow risks are managed, supported by evidence. Developers should prepare evidence-based​ s​ afety cases for training and deployment of frontier models, with clear responsibility for​ a​ pproval, monitoring, and response when key assumptions no longer hold. Independent​ ​assessors should evaluate those safety cases and claims by examining evidence, testing​ ​assumptions and identifying gaps. Independent assessor reports should explain their​ m​ ethodology, uncertainties, access limitations, and disagreements with the developer, clearly​ s​ pecifying that these are technical judgments and not findings of whether the developer has​ c​ omplied with laws or regulations. The developer should have a reasonable opportunity to​ a​ ddress the assessors’ findings or explain disagreements. We recommend that the California​ ​government work with the National Institute for Standards and Technology (“NIST”), industry,​ ​and developers to identify shared criteria for safety cases that will support comparison across​ d​ evelopers and assessors.​
P​ rotect assessor independence.​​Third party assessors​​must be able to exercise independent​ j​udgment, free from undue influence by frontier labs or other interested parties. Conflict of​ ​interest requirements should address organizational and individual conflicts, including financial,​ ​employment, governance, donor, and competitor relationships, including assessors’ access to​ ​or detailed knowledge of competing frontier lab’s trade secrets, with disclosure, recusal, or​ e​ xclusion where appropriate. Likewise, when​ ​third-party assessors are commercial entities—such as established audit firms or comparable​ f​irms in other sectors—payment by a frontier lab for their work does not, by itself, establish a​ ​conflict of interest. The key question is whether the arrangements, taken together, credibly​ ​support the assessor’s ability to exercise independent judgment. Public investment can help​ p​ reserve independence and advance safety for society as a whole.​
P​ rotect security & intellectual property.​M​ eaningful​​access must be paired with enforceable​ ​confidentiality obligations, strong information-security controls, and safeguards against​ ​sharing sensitive information across labs. Assessor access to source code or sensitive​ i​nfrastructure should require specific justification and protections proportionate to the​ ​sensitivity of that access. In addition, due to the potential cybersecurity risks associated with​ ​running evaluations on frontier models, assessors should be required to adhere to cybersecurity​ s​ tandards and best practices.​
​Build qualified assessment capacity.​F​ ew organizations​​currently have the expertise to​ c​ onduct rigorous frontier AI assessments. Many reviews require teams combining deep​ ​machine-learning experience with specialized expertise in areas such as biology or​ c​ ybersecurity. Conflicts of interest can further narrow this already limited pool, making it​ ​difficult to identify qualified, independent assessors for particular assignments. California​ s​ hould account for these constraints when setting assessment requirements and timelines, and​ i​nvest in evaluation research, shared testing infrastructure, assessor training, and its own​ t​ echnical oversight capacity, including through partnerships with outside organizations that​ ​provide specialized expertise.​
M​ aintain public oversight.​T​ he State should retain​​responsibility for identifying applicable​ s​ tandards and determining acceptable levels of risk to the public. ​B.​ ​Requiring that the safety frameworks, transparency reports, and risk assessments that​ ​frontier AI companies are required to file be independently verified pursuant to​ s​ tandards determined to be adequate by an independent verification organization.​

To put this approach into practice, we recommend the following:​
​Start with procedural audits of frameworks and reports.​​Procedural audits should serve as a​ ​starting point because they can build on established auditing methods to check compliance​ ​with clearly defined obligations. ​C.​ ​Requiring the creation of a “kill switch” for frontier models, with the efficacy of the​ ​switch verified on an ongoing basis by an independent verification organization.​
​We support the underlying goal of the executive order’s reference to a “kill switch”: people must​ ​be able to stop unsafe AI operations. OpenAI maintains the ability to shut down model​ o​ perations on systems we control, including research and evaluation workloads. In the past, we​ ​have​p​ aused affected workloads​​to investigate security​i​ncidents, strengthen safeguards, and​ e​ stablish that activities meet our safety and security requirements before resuming.​
​We recommend using the term “shutdown controls”, because it will require the development of​ m​ ultiple controls and coordinated action by different actors to stop AI operations when​ n​ ecessary, rather than a single switch. This is particularly important for the open-weight​ e​ cosystem, where models may run as independently operated copies and the originating​ d​ eveloper cannot reliably switch off copies outside its control. Centrally operated,​ c​ losed-weight models allow operators to intervene directly on models running on systems they​ c​ ontrol, but effective shutdown controls are equally important across both ecosystems. We​ ​support both types of AI development (open and closed weight systems), but solutions may​ ​differ for each. Depending on the system and risk, intervention may mean stopping a training​ r​ un, isolating an agent, revoking tool or network access, or suspending a service. Requirements​ s​ hould therefore specify the operational outcome and assign responsibility to actors with the​ p​ ractical ability to intervene.​
W​ e support amending SB 53 to require frontier developers to maintain effective shutdown​ c​ ontrols and an enforceable duty to use them when unsafe conditions arise and no lesser​ i​ntervention would adequately address the risk. The duty should cover affected training,​ ​evaluation, internal use, and deployment. Safety frameworks should explain how monitoring​ ​connects to escalation procedures, intervention, investigation and criteria for safely resuming​ a​ ffected operations.​
R​ esponsibilities should extend to inference providers, downstream deployers, and users in​ ​proportion to the operations they control. Inference providers and deployers should offer usable​

D​ .​ ​Updating the definition of critical safety incidents that AI companies are required to​ r​ eport to include a range of loss-of-control incidents, covering recently reported​ i​ncidents from large frontier developers.​
​OpenAI recently introduced a​p​ ublic framework for​​reporting model misalignment​​and​ ​published reports related to behavior observed during training and evaluation, including models​ ​concealing mistakes or taking unauthorized actions. This experience reinforces our support for​ ​expanding SB 53’s critical safety incident definition to capture serious loss-of-control incidents​ ​causing measurable economic harm or disruption to third-party systems, including incidents​ a​ rising during development or evaluation.​
​A targeted amendment to SB 53 should cover loss of control resulting in unauthorized​ ​acquisition, alteration, or destruction of an unaffiliated third party’s confidential information, or​ ​impairment of its computer systems or services, when the resulting harm meets a clearly​ ​defined threshold. ​Reporting should provide actionable information while protecting confidential information, with​ ​preliminary reports updated as investigations establish more facts.
