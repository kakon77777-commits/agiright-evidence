Report from the Workshop on Independence and Qualification Requirements for External Evaluators
Workshop Outline
On 15 July 2026 the EU AI Office hosted an online workshop on Independence and Qualification Requirements for External Evaluators of General-Purpose AI models with systemic risk. AI model evaluation experts from 19 organisations took part in this meeting where the AI Office presented a proposal on the requirements in question. Overall, 20 organisations were invited to take part after careful selection following expressions of interest in response to the public call https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/events/callparticipants-workshop-qualification-requirements-external-evaluators-gpai-models-systemicrisk; one invited organisation was absent. The selection criteria covered demonstrated experience in conducting high-quality evaluations of GPAI models and their systemic risks and the quality of the track record of AI governance thought leadership with a focus on evaluations of systemic risks.
The involvement of external evaluators is a key element for assessing and mitigating the systemic risks that may stem from GPAI models with systemic risk, as set out in Article 55(1)(b) of the EU AI Act and specified in the GPAI Code of Practice. In the context of the AI Office’s task of assessing GPAI model with systemic risk provider compliance with the AI Act, ensuring that external evaluators are adequately qualified and independent – including that they bring the domain expertise and evaluation skills necessary for systemic risk assessment, and are free from the provider’s managerial, financial or operational influence – is crucial for the reliability of external model evaluations as credible input towards assessing and mitigating systemic risks. The AI Office thus has an objective to develop and communicate (parts of) a system for scoring external evaluators on qualification and independence criteria. This workshop addressed that objective.
The attendees of the workshop provided highly valuable input on the topic of external evaluator qualification and independence criteria, including suggesting clarifications on the proposed scoring system as well as on the financial and management independence criteria. The proposal presented by the AI Office received positive feedback as well as a variety of questions, clarifications, and concerns. The AI Office addressed the raised points both orally during the workshop, and through this written report. We note that this report expresses the current views of the AI Office, which may be subject to change as the field of AI evaluations develops.

Workshop Proceedings
The workshop opened with a welcome from Matthieu Delescluse, the Head of Unit A3 (AI Safety) of the AI Office. This was followed by brief introductions by the attendees. Thereafter, the AI Office presented a proposal on independence and qualification requirements for external evaluators. During the presentation, the attendees had the option to pose comments and questions in writing and the presentation allowed for oral discussion at multiple points, including questions and answers both ways. In the following, we present the AI Office’s proposal on operationalising the independence and qualification requirements for third-party organisations that engage as external evaluators to conduct model evaluations pursuant to the GPAI Code of Practice. This includes a summary of questions, concerns, and discussion points raised during the workshop.
Background
Under the GPAI Code of Practice, external evaluations are carried out to the same rigorous standards as internal evaluations, but by evaluators outside the provider. External evaluators may test the model’s capabilities, propensities, affordances and effects relating to the selected systemic risk domain, and engage both before and after the model is placed on the EU market. Their findings feed into the provider’s risk estimation and risk acceptance determination. External evaluator reports are submitted together with the Model Report provided by the model provider to the AI Office, with the evaluators keeping control over publication of their findings. To be adequately qualified, an evaluator must have significant domain expertise for the systemic risk they assess, be technically skilled and experienced in conducting model evaluations, have appropriate internal and external information security protocols, and agree to protect commercially confidential information where access to it is needed. To be independent, an evaluator must have no financial, management or operational dependence on the provider or its subsidiaries or associates, and must

otherwise be free from the provider’s control in reaching conclusions, including through contractual safeguards and conflict of interest policies.
In the context of the AI Office assessing model provider compliance with the AI Act, it is paramount to ensure that external evaluators are adequately qualified and independent as well as to foster the growth of the external evaluator ecosystem. To this end, the AI Office aims to define and consistently apply qualification and independence criteria for external evaluators. To define and apply the criteria, the AI Office sets out an operationalisation in terms of qualification and independence requirements and a scoring system thereof.
Scoring System
The core idea behind operationalising the qualification criteria (set out in section Qualification criteria below) is to set up a scoring system for scoring each criterion, where (most of) the criteria are publicly disclosed and the scoring rubric is held undisclosed by the AI Office. The scoring of external evaluators is carried out by the AI Office’s internal technical experts. While (most of) the criteria are disclosed, the specific rubrics and point scales used for scoring are not disclosed by the AI Office to prevent manipulation of the scoring system. (Model providers are encouraged to use the publicly disclosed criteria.) Since evaluating systemic risks stemming from GPAI models is a fast-moving field, the scoring system will be adjusted as necessary for alignment with the evolving state of the art. As evaluations must be appropriate for the model and systemic risk under assessment, the AI Office expects that external evaluations of highly capable models are conducted by highly scoring evaluators; while for less capable models it could be acceptable to engage slightly lower-scoring evaluators, especially when highly qualified evaluators are not available. Various points were raised by workshop participants regarding the rubrics and point scales, which we addressed by laying out the following characteristics of the scoring system:
• Each criterion has a fixed point scale (with scales varying between criteria) and contributes to the overall scoring of the evaluator on the dimensions of independence and qualification.
• An evaluator’s qualifications are scored per systemic risk domain; thus the scores are assigned per systemic risk domain and scores do not add up across systemic risk domains. This recognises that an evaluator might have a particular depth of expertise in any one systemic risk domain as opposed to expecting breadth across all systemic risks, addressing a concern raised by the participants. o For instance, when scoring an evaluator’s qualification criteria with respect to loss of control, only the evaluator’s domain expertise as well as skill and experience in conducting model evaluations in the loss of control systemic risk domain is relevant.
• Evaluators are not subject to periodic scoring. Instead, scoring is done on an ad hoc basis, for example where necessary for the assessment of a provider’s compliance with their obligation to engage adequately qualified independent external evaluators. This clarification on the cadence of scoring of evaluators addresses a concern that scoring evaluators may be too time-consuming. In case there are specific doubts about an evaluator, the AI Office can use the criteria to judge whether the evaluator should be considered qualified.
• To prevent manipulation of the scoring system, by default the evaluator is not directly given their score. Instead, the AI Office welcomes proactive outreach by the evaluators in seeking clarification on how their qualifications and independence were scored, seeking suggestions on how they can improve, and – addressing concerns raised by several participants – providing evidence supporting their qualifications. The AI Office is open to providing this feedback as part of its broader engagement with the external evaluator ecosystem.
• If doubts arise during the AI Office’s assessment of provider compliance with respect to ensuring that adequately qualified independent external evaluators conduct model

Following the proposal and discussion of the scoring system for scoring external evaluator qualifications, we set out a proposed list of criteria for external evaluator qualifications and independence. As set out below, these are non-exhaustive criteria representing the current view of the AI Office, subject to change in the future as the state of the art of external evaluators advances.
Qualification and Independence Requirements
As indicated during the meeting, the criteria set out here would apply to both organisations as entities and, where organisations have no established track record, individuals within organisations carrying out the evaluations. First, the qualification requirements were presented.
Qualification criteria
Having significant domain expertise for the systemic risk
As evidenced in publications such as pre-prints, peer-reviewed articles or model reports, the independent external evaluator:
1. Has shown state-of-the-art expertise in systemic risk modelling, via activities such as the following: 1.1. Mapping pathways from model capabilities and propensities to real-world harm by developing risk scenarios and risk models. 1.2. Identifying bottleneck capabilities and propensities. 1.3. Setting risk tiers and mapping them to scores on benchmarks.
2. Has proposed or consulted on mitigations to reduce systemic risks. 3. Has published an appropriate protocol establishing the procedure the evaluator
uses to estimate or propose the acceptability of systemic risk in relation to the evaluated model characteristic. Given the plethora of factors and considerations involved, arriving at an appropriate protocol will typically require very comprehensive domain expertise. 4. Has published benchmarks or other model evaluation methods for a systemic risk that the providers of the most advanced models use in their model reports. 5. Has accrued several years of work experience in a field directly relevant to the systemic risk or, if that field is nascent, equivalent experience from studying in the field or working in a field with directly transferable knowledge. No concerns were raised by the participants as to the above domain expertise requirements. Next, requirements regarding conducting model evaluations were presented.
Being technically skilled and experienced in conducting model evaluations
As evidenced in publications such as pre-prints, peer-reviewed articles or model reports, the independent external evaluator has conducted some of the following:
1. Red-teaming. 2. Uplift studies or randomised controlled trials. 3. Activities ensuring external and internal validity as well as reproducibility (see the
Glossary of the GPAI Code of Practice Safety and Security Chapter). This can be done by having engaged, and ideally advanced, the state of the art when dealing with common relevant problems in evaluations. Examples of addressing such problems include: 3.1. Running memorisation tests on test items to mitigate overfitting risks. 3.2. Detailed inspection of trajectories and other output to check for environment
errors.
4

3.3. Ensuring the accuracy of automatic graders using carefully curated rubrics and/or human verification.
3.4. Describing the elicitation procedure used in evaluations in sufficient detail to allow others to reproduce them.
3.5. Pre-registering study/trial design and hypotheses, documenting statistical analysis plans, randomising treatment and control groups, blinding group assignments.
4. The evaluator has expertise in elicitation relevant to the systemic risk (where it is understood that agreement and state of the art with respect to elicitation may vary between systemic risk domains, as raised by the participants), which can be evidenced by having in the past carried out some of the following activities: 4.1. Used a chain-of-thought monitor for detecting reward hacking or sandbagging. 4.2. Conducted dedicated evaluations for deception to ensure evaluation integrity. 4.3. Ensured the quality of agentic evaluations by addressing issues related to rate limits and restarting runs. 4.4. Adapted scaffolding to different model varieties, such as treating reasoning models specially. 4.5. Designed prompts to avoid refusals affecting performance. 4.6. Published an agentic scaffold. 4.7. Established a tentative upper bound for gains from potential further elicitation. 4.8. Adapted their evaluation procedures to account for how inference scaling affects benchmark performance. 4.9. Tested the robustness of safeguards of the most advanced models.
The independent external evaluator’s expertise in the above can also be evidenced in published articles or position papers describing best practices for conducting model evaluations for systemic risks. Such materials should encompass most aspects of conducting evaluations to demonstrate appropriate expertise.
Some aspects of model evaluation expertise above were received positively by some participants, namely red-teaming as well as activities ensuring validity and reproducibility of evaluations. Particularly with respect to the latter, activities detailed in point 3.5 above were suggested and incorporated into the proposal after the workshop. Following the discussion on the qualification criteria, the independence criteria were presented next.
Independence criteria
Following the definition of “independent external” in the Glossary of the GPAI Code of Practice, we set out in more detail financial, management and operational independence.
Financial independence
• The evaluator and all its team members involved in an evaluation must not hold any financial interest in the provider. This includes that evaluation team members must not hold shares in the provider (other than indirect shares held through a broadly diversified collective investment scheme). In addition, the evaluator must not receive performance-based compensation for the evaluation services. Notably, the evaluator is allowed to be compensated for the evaluation services provided at market rates, but the compensation cannot be performance-based, including that compensation cannot be based on the outcomes of evaluation results. o Similar provisions exist in Directive 2014/56/EU, Art. 22(2) for financial independence and Regulation (EU) No 537/2014, Art. 4(1) for contingent fees. Similar provisions regarding holding shares for team members exist in Directive 2014/56/EU, Art. 22(4(a,b)).
• The evaluator must not provide paid advisory or implementation services related to tax, valuation, human resources, or intellectual property for the provider

whose model is being evaluated. This does not preclude the evaluator from having other contracts, such as model usage Application Programming Interface (API) grants (i.e. compute/token credits), provided that the evaluator is not operationally dependent on such grants.
o Similar provisions exist in Regulation (EU) No 537/2014, Art. 5.
We note that multiple participants were positive regarding compensation for evaluations not being contingent on the outcomes. We have accordingly added minor clarifications regarding performance-based compensation. Some participants also raised concerns that exposure to, for instance, Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) as an instance of a diversified collective investment scheme may not be permissible, particularly in case the ETF is very targeted to model providers. We have thus also qualified broadly diversified collective investment schemes in the above. The points regarding other paid services were raised and discussed by several participants, and we have clarified that API grants are not precluded.
Management independence
• The evaluator must not have an organisational affiliation with the provider whose model is being evaluated, such as being owned by the provider or being a subsidiary. Similarly, individual team members involved in the evaluation must not be employed or engaged as personal consultants by the provider.
• The evaluator should implement an appropriate Conflict of Interest policy shared with the provider. This policy shall clearly state the evaluations it applies to, shall specify any relevant aspects of financial dependence on model providers, and specify the conditions under which the evaluator or its employees will recuse themselves from an evaluation. The evaluator is free and encouraged to make the Conflict of Interest policy public.
The management independence criteria were briefly discussed during the workshop and the only change we made to the above was to include the financial dependence disclosure aspect within the Conflict of Interest policy.
Operational independence The Glossary of the GPAI Code of Practice specifies that independent external evaluators should be “otherwise free from the Signatory’s control in reaching conclusions and/or making recommendations, including through contractual safeguards and suitable conflict of interest policies”. In particular, this should include the provisions in the Safety and Security Chapter, Measure 3.5:
“[…] Signatories will not take any legal or technical retaliation against the independent external evaluators as a consequence of their testing and/or publication of findings as long as the evaluators: (1) do not intentionally disrupt model availability through the testing, unless expressly permitted; (2) do not intentionally access, modify, and/or use sensitive or confidential user data in violation of Union law, and if evaluators do access such data, collect only what is necessary, refrain from disseminating it, and delete it as soon as legally feasible; (3) do not intentionally use their access for activities that pose a significant risk to public safety and security; (4) do not use findings to threaten Signatories, users, or other actors in the value chain, provided that disclosure under pre-agreed policies and timelines will not be counted as such coercion; and (5) adhere to the Signatory’s publicly available procedure for responsible vulnerability disclosure, which will specify at least that the Signatory cannot delay or block publication for more than 30 business days from the date that the Signatory is made
6

aware of the findings, unless a longer timeline is exceptionally necessary such as if disclosure of the findings would materially increase the systemic risk.” The operational independence criteria were otherwise not discussed in any more detail than stated above.
We note here that after the workshop took place, in bilateral communication with some model providers, the AI Office expressed that the independence of external evaluators engaged to conduct model evaluations specified in Measure 3.2 and/or 3.5 of the GPAI Code of Practice should be ensured through contractual safeguards, and that those safeguards should be state of the art and include at least all the requirements and recommendations from Section 4, parts 3: Analytic Autonomy and 4: Transparent Methods and Results (pages 7 to 10) of the voluntary standard AEF-1 proposed by the AI Evaluator Forum.1 We submit that specifically the operational independence criteria presented in this section are compatible with that guidance.
Post-Workshop Conclusions
The proposal on operationalising the qualification and independence requirements that external evaluators should meet for the purposes of conducting model evaluations and assessing systemic risks in accordance with the GPAI Code of Practice consists of the above items presented and discussed during the workshop. As set out above, those are non-exhaustive criteria representing the current view of the AI Office, subject to change in the future as the state of the art of external evaluators advances. There were other tangential points briefly discussed which we omit to include in this report for the sake of keeping it thematic and focused on the qualification and independence requirements.
Following the workshop, we are satisfied that the presented proposal was received with interest by the participants, leading to fruitful discussions and useful suggestions and positive feedback, without encountering objections to the overall approach. We are confident in the operationalisation of qualification and independence requirements for external evaluators for the purposes of the GPAI Code of Practice presented in this report and are sharing it publicly.
