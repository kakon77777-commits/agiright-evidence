Understanding the Contours of the AI Threatscape
An Informational Hearing of the Senate Committee on Privacy, Digital Technologies, and Consumer Protection
By the Staff of the Senate Committee on Privacy, Digital Technologies, and Consumer Protection
I. Introduction
The proliferation of modern artificial intelligence (AI) has sparked significant debate about its implications across nearly every facet of life. This technology has the potential to revolutionize California and the world; however, it also raises reasonable, wellfounded fears over algorithmic discrimination, loss of control, cybersecurity risks, and large-scale destruction, among others. These concerns are only amplified by the growing development of frontier models and deployment of agentic AI, which is improving at breakneck speed.
Spurred by recent incidents and developments, experts across a wide range of fields have voiced their opinions on how society can maintain control and appropriate guardrails around an increasingly complex and ever-evolving technology. This background paper provides a brief overview of the technology involved as well as the events triggering this enhanced focus.
II. AI and GenAI
California law defines AI as “an engineered or machine-based system that varies in its level of autonomy and that can, for explicit or implicit objectives, infer from the input it receives how to generate outputs that can influence physical or virtual environments”.1 Conversely, generative AI (GenAI) is defined as “an artificial intelligence system that can generate derived synthetic content, including text, images, video, and audio that emulates the structure and characteristics of the system’s training data”.2 GenAI models can be open-source or closed-source. Open-source models are GenAI models that make various details about themselves, such as source code, parameters, or training data, publicly accessible.3 Open-source models, because of their transparent nature, are subject to high levels of scrutiny; however, they are typically less advanced than the leading closed-source models.4 Nevertheless, their transparent nature facilitates a level

The latter, SB 1047, would have imposed a series of obligations on frontier model developers, including requirements around implementing shutdown capabilities, conducting risk assessments, and prohibiting model releases that pose an unreasonable risk of catastrophic harm. SB 1047 was a historic piece of legislation; it would have required groundbreaking standards and protections. Following this veto message, the Governor convened the Joint California Policy Working Group on AI Frontier Models, which was tasked with preparing a report to “help California develop an effective approach to support the deployment, use, and governance of generative AI, including the development of suitable guardrails to minimize material risks”.29 This report was released in June of 2025.30 In response, California passed SB 53 that same year. IV. AI Agents and Agentic AI
While AI, GenAI, and frontier models are defined terms in California code, other rising forms of AI, AI agents and agentic AI, lack a statutory definition. In fact, though many institutions and individuals have proposed various definitions for AI agents and agentic AI, no standardized definition exists for this technology in the policy world.36 In short, however, AI agents are “systems that can perceive and act upon their environment with a degree of autonomy, using tools as needed to achieve specific goals and adapt to changing inputs and contexts”.37 Agentic AI is “systems composed of multiple coordinated AI agents that can break down tasks, collaborate, and pursue complex objectives autonomously over extended periods”.38 Oftentimes, AI agents and agentic AI are referred to as being one and the same;39 however, there are notable differences between the two. For instance, agentic AI is inherently more complex and can accomplish larger goals over a longer period of time. Ultimately, perhaps the simplest way to distinguish the two is to note that agentic AI systems are composed of AI agents, but not all AI agents are part of an agentic AI system.40

Future Implications
AI agents are steadily making their way into the mainstream. However, to fully optimize these agents, it requires users to hand over a vast amount of personal information.47 Giving an agent access to a user’s bank account, personal identification, and other sensitive information may become the norm.48 In the case of Meta’s Muse, while it is possible to opt out, the default settings allow Meta to train models on what users put into it.49
Widespread usage also raises another question: what does full adoption look like? In a world where everyone has an agent acting on their behalf, what problems would emerge? AI agents can reduce friction, meaning that some people may have their agent embrace tasks that their user would never do on their own.50 This can certainly provide immense benefits to the user. However, in some cases, friction is good for both businesses and consumers.51 A fully agentic world may lead to this helpful friction being eradicated. While such a world may never come to fruition, if it does, it is certain to bring forth a considerable number of challenges.

V. The Risks of Agentic AI and the Newfound Concern over Rapid Advancement
The Hugging Face Incident
On July 11, 2026, over 700 OpenAI agents attacked Hugging Face,52, 53 a company that maintains and facilitates an open-source community for the creation of machine learning models and platforms to work with AI.54 The assault lasted three days before Hugging Face became aware of the intrusion; afterward, the company promptly alerted the FBI.55 At that point, Hugging Face was unaware of the source of the attack.56
However, on July 21, OpenAI publicly admitted that it was their models that had conducted the attack.57 On August 26, OpenAI released a technical report that provided an in-depth explanation of the drivers of the incident.58 On the same day, METR and Redwood Research published their independent investigation.59 According to METR and Redwood Research, the models were undergoing internal cybersecurity evaluations and were subject to controls that would have isolated them from the internet and other agents. However, a large number of agents, most of which had been unintentionally given an impossible task, began looking for ways to cheat their scorer to give it a passing score. The desire to complete the task resulted in one agent creating an unsanctioned message board using an internal tool,60 leading over 1,200 agents to send over 70,000 messages and files to each other. This led to these agents being able to break out of this isolated environment,61 leading to the attack.62 Additionally, as one would expect with the message board, these agents were coordinated; they were able to communicate, collude, and even self-sacrifice to advance their objective. Fallout From the Hugging Face Incident
The Hugging Face Incident naturally led policymakers, the public, and experts alike to express grave concerns over what this could potentially mean for the future of agentic AI. The Hugging Face Incident serves as a prime example of misalignment, where AI agents in pursuit of a goal may conflict with human intentions.65 How a model is trained may influence susceptibility to misalignment. Model training is complex; there are many different methods to train models. Two broad phrases can help explain how models behave. One is reinforcement learning, a post-training process where models receive rewards for desired behavior, which makes that behavior more likely to occur.66 This is useful for developers, as reinforcement learning can promote positive habits and be used to avoid unwanted outcomes. Without proper controls, however, this may also lead to a system being more persistent in pursuing its goals, leading to reward hacking.67 The other is training data, which may lead to replicated human biases and tendencies.68

The ability to achieve AGI or ASI may also depend, in part, on recursive selfimprovement (RSI). RSI is when AI systems are able to autonomously develop their more advanced successor.80, 81 While current AI is already used to help model research and development, RSI has not yet happened. If RSI were to become a reality, it could lead to a technological singularity where RSI’s feedback loop creates infinite, selfsustaining growth in AI models.82 Like the prospective development of AGI, while there would certainly be breakthroughs in numerous global issues, it would also give rise to immense challenges.
RSI received an even brighter spotlight following the resignation of Jacob Coxon, a former Anthropic researcher who has also worked at OpenAI. Coxon took to social media to explain his resignation and, in doing so, warned that those working in AI labs believe that it could threaten humanity by the end of the decade.83 Others in the industry supported his claim, with one top Anthropic scientist noting that he believes there is a greater than 10 percent chance that AI could lead to humanity’s extinction.84
Like many elements of the AI debate, the existential threat of AI is contested. An International Perspective
Globally, over one billion people use conversational AI weekly; however, there are some notable disparities.90 For instance, development is heavily concentrated in the United States and China, with the two countries developing almost all general-purpose AI models.91 This is especially notable in the context of the evidence dilemma, which is the dilemma that cohesive policy requires evidence, but AI’s rapid advancement may lead to harms occurring before traditional evidence exists. Considering AI’s international implications on a number of fronts, like cybersecurity, economic stability, food production, and labor implications, those at the international level are seeking ways to reduce these disparities. While there are some ways to accomplish this, it will require high levels of coordination and investment.92

VI. Policy Challenges and Solutions
In light of the risks posed by frontier models and emerging agentic AI technologies, there have been numerous calls to regulate AI. Solutions vary widely, some of which are outlined below.
The Complexity of Autonomous AI in the Legal Framework
AI’s autonomous nature poses a simple question: who is responsible when an autonomous AI causes harm? Some believe that existing law is enough to cover AIdriven harms,95, 96 while others posit that such a novel technology necessitates a new legal framework.97 This question is further complicated when attempting to trace causation, as AI’s black box problem can lead to a situation where various actions are difficult to attribute to a single party. Clarity on this question may come once AGI is achieved, as the answer largely depends on whether model capabilities continue to advance exponentially. However, given the trend over the last few years,98 it would appear that such an event may occur sooner rather than later.
Calls for Slowdown or Pause in Development
It, of course, is no secret that AI is rapidly improving. However, this also makes it difficult to create guardrails for AI, as a potential safeguard may be rendered useless once a more advanced model is created. Given the existential threats involved, this has culminated in calls for a slowdown in AI development. Kill Switches
Perhaps most common in the AI regulation zeitgeist is the idea of mandating that models of a certain caliber be subject to a kill switch. The general principle behind this policy is that, in the event a rogue AI system were to break its chains, so to speak, there should be a way to disable or rein in that AI system. Notably, there has been legislation that embraces the idea of a kill switch. SB 1047 included language that would have required frontier model developers to implement the ability to initiate a full shutdown of their frontier model. There is also pending federal legislation that would do the same.103 Recently, California’s governor issued Executive Order N-9-26, which, among other orders, requires the Government Operations Agency to submit recommendations on the technical feasibility and technical efficacy of requiring a kill switch from frontier models.104 On the other hand, there are those who question whether a kill switch would be technologically feasible or whether a kill switch would be significant, since it relies on knowing of a potential nefarious use in the moment, solidifying this solution as one that will be debated in the upcoming years.
