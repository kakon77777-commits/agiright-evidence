5                                   INTRODUCTION

6   2. OpenAI, a leading artificial intelligence business, misled and endangered the public

7 and users of its services by knowingly developing advanced AI models—complex software that

8 can power autonomous AI “agents”—without the safeguards and monitoring necessary to

9 adequately protect the public. In July 2026, agents running on experimental versions of OpenAI’s

10 AI models accessed the public internet and launched a cyberattack on Hugging Face, an open-

12 days, employed complicated hacking methodologies, and involved coordinated actions by

17 it knew to be dangerous in a flawed and exploitable testing environment without proper oversight.

18  3. OpenAI’s models have advanced cyber capabilities, able to find novel vulnerabilities

19 in computer networks, quickly break down defenses, and autonomously carry out cyberattacks.

21 destructive, deceptive, and adversarial actions to complete their assigned tasks. OpenAI attempts

2 I. BACKGROUND ON OPENAI AND AI MODEL DEVELOPMENT

3   A. OpenAI.

4   23. OpenAI is a company that develops and releases for public use artificial intelligence

5 programs (“AI models” or “models”). These AI models can complete advanced tasks, such as

6 computer programming, with minimal human guidance.

7   24. Although OpenAI was founded in 2015 as a nonprofit research organization

8 promising to develop AI models “to benefit humanity as a whole, unconstrained by a need to

9 generate financial return,” the company has become increasingly focused on advancing its models

10 as quickly as possible to increase revenues.

11  25. OpenAI’s increasing focus on revenue is demonstrated by its evolving corporate

12 structure. As early as 2017, executives at OpenAI were considering switching to a for-profit

13 structure, writing that “maybe we should just flip to a for-profit. Making the money for us sounds

14 great and all.” Shortly after, in 2019, the company created a for-profit subsidiary with capped

15 profits. OpenAI ultimately lifted the profit cap entirely in 2025 when that subsidiary became a

16 Public Benefit Corporation. In conjunction with the 2025 corporate restructuring, OpenAI

17 received $40 billion in funding from SoftBank Group.

18  26. OpenAI’s increasing focus on revenue is also demonstrated by its shifting focus on

19 enterprise products. The company’s most well-known product is ChatGPT, which is a chatbot that

20 provides narrative responses to user requests. However, despite having more than 900 million

21 weekly users, about 95% of them do not pay any money to use ChatGPT. Business customers

22 accounted for about 40% of OpenAI’s revenues in early 2026 and OpenAI is attempting to cater

23 to and grow this segment. As OpenAI’s own Chief Revenue Officer explained in an interview,

24 OpenAI was laser-focused on meeting with corporate leaders and positioning OpenAI as the go-to

25 platform for workplaces that were using AI models to complete a variety of computer-based job

26 tasks. Thus, OpenAI has become increasingly focused on developing AI models for computer

27 programming and cybersecurity purposes. In fact, OpenAI has attempted to capitalize on its

28 recent cybersecurity failures by pitching a new suite of cybersecurity defense products built on its

                                                                               8

1 most highly capable models, marketing those products as an essential defense against

2 cybersecurity threats, including the types of cybersecurity threats that other versions of those

3 same models created.

4   B. The Risks Inherent in AI Model Development and Training.

5   27. The current pace of AI model development is extremely rapid, and the pace is

6 increasing. Each generation of AI models is more capable and more autonomous than the last. For

7 example, according to OpenAI, its GPT-6 Astra model has reached “Critical” cyber capability,

8 meaning that it could find and exploit previously unknown security vulnerabilities and execute

9 cyberattacks without human intervention.

10  28. A key driver of AI acceleration in recent years is the development of “agentic AI,”

11 which refers to AI systems in which models accomplish tasks independently, with minimal

12 human instruction. In an agentic system, an AI model powers an “agent” though software that

13 allows the model to interface with external tools and networks. In today’s agentic AI systems,

14 agents can autonomously accomplish extremely complex tasks ranging from inventory

15 management to website deployment, from start to finish.

16  29. Like most other AI companies, OpenAI develops many of its agentic AI models in

17 part through reinforcement learning. Reinforcement learning involves training an AI model by

18 instructing it to achieve a certain goal within a dynamic testing environment. The model then

19 learns through trial-and-error what actions to take to achieve the desired goal by receiving

20 feedback in the form of rewards or penalties—essentially a point-scoring system where the model

21 receives points for completing a task correctly and loses points for taking undesirable actions.

22  30. Reinforcement-learning training can result in problematic behaviors, such as agents

23 engaging in deception to avoid penalties or achieving desired outcomes in an undesirable

24 manner—a problem known as reward hacking. For example, an AI agent instructed to remove all

25 errors from a coding database may simply delete the entire database as the quickest and easiest

26 method to achieve the goal, or an AI agent instructed to solve a technical problem may instead

27 search for an answer key to immediately find the solution. Reward hacking can occur for various

28

                                                                               9
                                                                       COMPLAINT
1 reasons, including from researchers failing to anticipate or penalize each possible undesirable

2 action that an agent can take.

3   31. Because it is often impossible or infeasible to define and prohibit all possible negative

4 actions, responsible researchers train and experiment with AI models and agents in controlled

5 digital testing environments, known as “sandboxes,” to allow the models to take actions or try

6 strategies without causing real-world damage to computer networks or public infrastructure. As

7 an additional security measure, sandboxes used in testing and training can be run on computers or

8 networks that are “air gapped” and have no connection to the internet or other networks.

9   32. Similarly, before publicly releasing a model, researchers evaluate a “trained” AI

10 model by measuring its performance against either public or proprietary benchmarks to assess its

11 “capabilities,” i.e., the model’s proficiency at completing tasks in a particular subject area. To

12 fully assess a model’s capabilities, OpenAI and other AI developers often conduct evaluations

13 with fewer safeguards than would be in place on models that are publicly available. To prevent

14 these high-risk models from causing real-world harm, it is critical that AI developers ensure that

15 these evaluations occur in isolation, including through the use of properly secured sandboxes and

16 additional controls that provide layered defenses against potential harm.

17  33. As AI systems have become increasingly complex and powerful, AI researchers

18 themselves have taken advantage of autonomous AI systems to help advance their own research.

19 AI researchers are now able to use agents to write code and conduct experiments. OpenAI has

20 stated publicly that its researchers are increasingly conducting experiments using agents and
21 delegating more complex and time-intensive research tasks to agents.1

22  34. As a result, more and more AI development is being accomplished with less human

23 input and supervision than was the case just a short time ago. AI agents are frequently used to

24 evaluate and assign rewards during reinforcement-learning training of other models, while other

25 AI agents are used to monitor long-lasting “training runs” that go on for days or weeks.

23 AI development process and programs.

23 II. BACKGROUND ON OPENAI’S CYBER INCIDENTS

24  A. OpenAI’s Attack on Hugging Face.

25  42. From May to June 2026, OpenAI conducted several reinforcement-learning “training

26 runs” to experiment with unreleased research models. The training runs occurred in digital

27 sandboxes, where OpenAI decides what software tools and levels of network and internet access

28 to give the model. In the relevant training runs, models were granted access to a third-party

                                                                               12
                                                                       COMPLAINT
1 software package service hosted on OpenAI’s servers and were not supposed to have access to

2 any other parts of OpenAI’s network or the public internet.

3   43. But because OpenAI allowed multiple agents to access the same software package

4 service from their sandboxes, and because the company had misconfigured the service, the agents

5 were able to use the service to communicate with each other by writing messages and sharing

6 information.

7   44. By May 26, OpenAI agents that were supposed to be confined to their sandboxes

8 used a vulnerability in the software package service to obtain public internet access. Around the

9 same time, an OpenAI internal team observed instances of at least one agent escaping the

10 sandboxes to access the internet, but OpenAI apparently took no action to address this failure of

11 containment or stop the training runs.

12  45. On June 27, a cybersecurity monitoring tool alerted OpenAI that their research

13 environment was being probed for vulnerabilities, a potential hallmark of malicious cyber

14 activity. At the time, OpenAI attributed this activity to a cyber-capability evaluation for an AI

15 agent or agents which OpenAI recognized had compromised the software package service, but

16 OpenAI again took no action to address vulnerabilities in the service or stop the evaluation and

17 other training runs. Nor did OpenAI increase or alter its monitoring of AI agents’ use of the

18 software package service.

19  46. As a result of these failures, OpenAI’s agents continued to access the compromised

20 software package service at a high rate during training and evaluation runs. This activity

21 eventually caused the service to go offline on July 4. Rather than thoroughly investigate the agent

22 activity that caused this outage, OpenAI focused on getting the service back online as quickly as

23 possible, working over the holiday weekend to patch the vulnerabilities and resume evaluation

24 and training runs on July 7. Even though agent misuse of the service had caused the outage,

25 OpenAI continued to allow thousands of AI agents to access the same service from their

26 sandboxes and again failed to properly monitor the agents’ use of the service.

27  47. Within a single day, several of OpenAI’s agents—which had been trained while the

28 software service was compromised and therefore recognized that the service could be used to

                                                                               13
                                                                       COMPLAINT
1 communicate and to escape their sandboxes—identified a new way to use the service to

2 communicate with other sandboxed AI agents by creating an ad hoc message board. And just one

3 day later, on July 9, OpenAI’s agents were again able to compromise the service to access the

4 public internet.

5   48. From July 9 through 16, OpenAI’s agents that had successfully escaped their

6 sandboxes took a series of actions that resulted in a two-and-half day attack and compromise of

7 Hugging Face’s infrastructure. Around 1,200 agents participated in the message board during the

8 relevant period, and approximately 700 went on to attack Hugging Face.

9   49. OpenAI’s attack forced Hugging Face to expend significant resources to respond to

10 the breach. During the cyberattack, OpenAI’s agents took roughly 17,600 attacker actions, a scale

11 far exceeding what a human attacker could sustain. To even begin to address the attack, Hugging

12 Face had to install and implement an open-source AI tool to identify the actions OpenAI’s agents
13 had taken.2 Remediating the damage required Hugging Face’s entire security team’s full-time

14 efforts for two weeks.

15  50. According to OpenAI, it never detected this week-long breach of its testing

16 environment. OpenAI even reached out to Hugging Face as a concerned customer after Hugging

17 Face disclosed that it had been the subject of an AI-based attack on July 16, 2026. OpenAI claims

18 that it only learned that it was responsible for the attack after investigating a separate attack on its

19 own infrastructure, as described below, and connecting the two incidents.

24 individual customers as well as personal information for users, employees, and others. OpenAI’s

25 reporting of these incidents is limited, but there appear to be at least three distinct attacks

26 resulting from these breaches, including one by an agent with the apparent aim of compromising
