FINANCIAL STABILITY REPORT PRESS CONFERENCE

                                    Thursday 26 November 2026

       Opening remarks by Andrew Bailey, Governor of the Bank of England

Welcome to the press conference for the November 2026 Financial Stability Report. I
should start off by welcoming Katharine Braddick, Deputy Governor for Prudential
Regulation, to her first Financial Stability Report Press Conference.

I will highlight the key themes from today's publications by the Financial Policy
Committee (FPC). I will start with our view on the overall risk environment, before
turning to the FPC's work on operational resilience and the FPC's view on the
resilience of households, corporates, and the banking sector. I will then outline the
conclusion of the FPC's Review of Bank Capital, before turning finally the FPC's
work on supporting sustainable economic growth.

Starting with the overall risk environment, the continued conflict in the Middle
East has led to a protracted negative supply shock to the global economy.
Global growth has so far been resilient, but the outlook for inflation and interest rates
remains uncertain and sensitive to developments in the Middle East.

Beyond events in the Middle East, vulnerabilities previously highlighted by the
FPC in sovereign debt markets, risky credit markets, including in private
credit, and risky asset valuations remain, and in some cases have amplified
market volatility.

Turning first to sovereign debt markets. Sovereign debt-to-GDP ratios have
continued to move upwards globally. Government bond yields reached near two-
decade highs in several advanced economies, as shown in Chart 1. In the UK, the
increase in yields has mainly reflected higher expected policy rates. Elsewhere,
fiscal and political uncertainty has contributed more materially to the increases in
yields.
As we've highlighted previously there has been a shift in recent years as
investors in many sovereign bond markets are now underpinned by a
relatively high use of leverage, and we see hedge funds pursuing similar
trading strategies across jurisdictions. Market intelligence suggests that
deleveraging by hedge funds contributed to the increase in French sovereign bond
yields, although spillovers to other markets have been limited.

UK gilt and gilt repo markets have functioned well. As shown in chart 2, whilst
hedge fund leverage remains elevated by historical standards, net repo borrowing
has declined a little. Whilst the gilt repo market has remained resilient recently, past
episodes of market dysfunction have highlighted the need to pursue reforms to
enhance the resilience of that market. Today's publications provide an update on the
FPC's thinking on gilt repo market resilience. The Bank will publish a roadmap for
working with industry to develop its preferred package of policy measures in the first
half of 2027.
Turning to risky asset markets, equity valuations remain stretched on some
measures, despite recent falls. Hedge funds and retail investors have continued to
invest in AI stocks, with hedge funds obtaining leverage through significant prime
brokerage borrowing. Following the US midterm elections there was a sharp fall in
the value of AI-related equities. These moves were amplified by hedge fund
deleveraging. Volatility in Korean equity markets has also shown that procyclical
rebalancing from leveraged ETFs can amplify falls in equity prices. Whilst spillovers
to other markets were limited across these episodes, in a more severe shock, losses
on equity positions could lead globally active investors to delever in other markets,
including sovereign debt markets.

Meanwhile higher borrowing costs have increased refinancing pressures in
corporate debt markets, particularly for weaker borrowers. This is shown in the
sharp widening in spreads for US CCC-rated debt, as shown in Chart 3. We'll be
updating you on our private market SWES in the new year.
AI-related financing continues to grow rapidly and is increasingly spanning equity
markets, public bond markets, private credit and more complex financing structures.
This is increasing interconnections across the finance system. Some AI-related
companies are also providing finance to others in the sector in arrangements linked
to future revenue commitments. More complex structures and circular financing
arrangements can make it harder to identify concentrations of risk and where
exposures ultimately sit.

Turning to operational risks, the FSR sets out the need for firms to keep pace
with the growing scale and sophistication of cyber threats, to accelerate long-
term preparations for quantum-resistant cryptography, and to develop and
maintain credible arrangements to recover quickly from operational disruption,
as well as increasing their focus on deep cyber recovery options. The FPC
recognises that this will place competing demands on technology skills and
management capacity. However, a failure to take action could have system-wide
implications. Sector-wide collaboration is crucial, in that context the Bank intends to
establish a new Systemic Resilience Council which will bring together Bank, other
authorities, and senior industry representatives from banks, insurers and financial
market infrastructures to coordinate action on system-wide risks.

Taking the operational resilience themes in turn, frontier AI capabilities have
continued to grow since the July FSR. Recent incidents have further illustrated
the challenges in managing the risk of AI agents acting in ways which are misaligned
with the objectives intended by developers and users. Capabilities of open weight
models have also developed rapidly. These models can be run more cheaply than
closed weight models, and safeguards may be disabled or removed more easily.
This could make more advanced AI capabilities more readily available and useful to
malicious actors.

Recent outages with Google cloud services and Microsoft have illustrated how
operational dependencies mean disruption at a single provider can affect
many firms. This underlines the importance of the UK's Critical Third Party Regime,
under which nine firms have now been designated.
Quantum computing could compromise widely used cryptography and hence
cyber security. As the National Cyber Security Centre has been highlighting for
some time, this makes an orderly transition to quantum-resistant cryptography a
priority � which will have implications for the actions and decisions firms take today.
UK households and corporates remain resilient in aggregate, however higher
borrowing costs and energy prices are placing additional pressure on household
finances. Consistent with higher market interest rates, mortgage rates have
increased since the July FSR and more households are now projected to see
increases in mortgage repayments over the next three years, as shown in Chart 4.
However, the median increase is projected to be materially smaller than the
increases experienced during 2023 and 2024. Pressures will be greater for some
households, notably low-income households and renters.

The UK banking system remains well capitalised with high levels of liquidity.
Despite heightened uncertainty, banks have continued to support lending to the real
economy, particularly large corporates, shown in Chart 5. There is no evidence of
banks restricting lending to defend capital positions. The FPC has maintained the UK
countercyclical capital buffer at its neutral setting of 2%. This helps to ensure that
banks continue to have capacity to absorb unexpected future shocks without
restricting lending to households and businesses.
Today's publications mark the conclusion of the FPC's review of the bank
capital framework, the detail of which is set out in a Financial Stability in Focus
report.

The report sets out that the PRA will consult on lower minimum requirements
related to domestic lending. This is to reflect more fully the resilience provided by
maintaining a 2% neutral CCyB rate, and to better reflect the risks of geographic
concentration. After this reduction, systemwide capital requirements for the major UK
banks will be consistent with the FPC's benchmark of around 13%.

Changes announced as part of the FPC's bank capital review represent
meaningful reform, making capital requirements more proportionate, better
targeted, and � as a result � lower. Whilst overall impact varies by bank, in
aggregate the changes announced over this year result in Tier 1 capital requirements
for major UK banks reducing by an amount equivalent to around 50 basis points of
risk-weighted assets, which translates into at least �8bn. It is for banks to decide
how they respond to these changes. But to provide some illustration, if all of the
reduction in their capital requirements were to flow through to lending, it would
increase the major UK banks' lending capacity by around �175bn, which is
equivalent to around two years of net lending to the household and non-financial
corporate sectors based on last year's lending.

Reducing capital requirements much further would risk financial instability and
put long term growth at risk by undermining the banking system's ability to absorb
rather than amplify shocks.

Banks currently maintain levels of capital in excess of regulatory requirements. The
FPC and PRA intend to enhance banks' confidence in using their capital resources to
support the economy. The FPC has set out its vision of a single releasable buffer
which could be used without automatic distribution restrictions. Such reform
would ideally be achieved in coordination with international bodies, and we will
evaluate progress on this work by the end of next year. The FPC and PRC will also
consider how further action could reduce incentives for firms to maintain
capital in excess of requirements and bring forward proposals next year.

Finally, in response to its 2025 remit letter, the FPC's work on long-term capital
and high-growth firms has identified the actions which, from a macro-
economic perspective, would have the most significant impact on the supply
of long-term finance for high-growth firms seeking to scale up. Evidence shows
that the UK has a deep domestic pool of capital and a rich pipeline of innovative
firms, but material frictions affect how efficiently the two are matched. A range of
government and industry initiatives are already seeking to address these frictions,
but the framework for these initiatives is perceived to be fragmented. This makes
greater coordination and reducing complexity across the financing chain an
immediate priority.

Let me conclude.

The outlook for inflation and interest rates remains uncertain and sensitive to
developments in the Middle East, whilst vulnerabilities in markets previously
highlighted by the FPC remain, and in some cases have amplified market volatility.
Developments in AI, the deeper interconnections across the financial system from
common technologies and providers, and the development of quantum computing
make cyber and operational risks a more prominent risk to financial stability, and
we're working closely with other authorities and industry to address them. The
changes to the bank capital framework announced today, and earlier this year, make
requirements more proportionate, better targeted and � as a result � lower. And
finally, the FPC's work on long-term capital and high-growth firms has emphasised
the need for greater coordination and reducing complexity across the financing
chain.

And now, Sarah, Katharine and I will be happy to take your questions.
