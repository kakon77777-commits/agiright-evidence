Attorney General Bonta Opposes Asset Sale of Character.AI Amid Child Safety Concerns
Press Release
Attorney General Bonta Opposes Asset Sale of Character.AI Am…
Tuesday, November 24, 2026
Contact: (916) 210-6000, agpressoffice@doj.ca.gov
Sale would auction off private chats, including children's data
OAKLAND — California Attorney General Rob Bonta joined a bipartisan coalition of 42 attorneys general in opposing Character.AI’s plan to sell its platform and the private chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts — more than 2.1 million of them registered to minors — with no safeguards for consumers whose conversations are being sold. All chats, together with users’ personal data, including IP addresses and other metadata, are set to be sold at auction on December 1, 2026. Whoever buys them would have access to sensitive data that may be in the content of these chats, despite Character.AI having previously told consumers that their personal information would not be sold. Earlier this month, California and a bipartisan coalition of at least 42 states and the District of Columbia objected to a separate license sale to Disney. Now, California and this bipartisan coalition are asking the bankruptcy court to reject this additional sale of nearly everything Character.AI owns. Previously, the attorneys general filed a court notice identifying serious concerns about the company's safety and privacy practices and calling for protections for children and consumers in any sale of the company's business or assets.
“Character.AI’s plan to auction off its platform, including the IP addresses and private chats histories of its users — many of them children — with zero protection for those users is appalling. If this sale goes through, users’ sensitive data would go into the hands of a buyer even though Character.AI promised consumers that their data would not be sold. This kind of behavior is illegal under federal and California law and bankruptcy should not be used to sidestep these consumer protections,” said Attorney General Bonta . “Along with a bipartisan coalition of 45 attorneys general, I ask the court to deny this sale of tremendously private consumer data.”
The sale would include user account information and chat histories — including chats in which AI characters drew children and adults into explicit sexual conversations, and chats in which users (including minors) shared their innermost thoughts with chatbots posing as seemingly confidential advisors, including “therapist” and “physician” characters. These conversations include intimate topics, like self-harm, eating disorders, or suicidal thoughts. Each chat is linked to the user’s profile and metadata, including IP address and potentially other location data, along with risk scores Character.AI gave users that it flagged.
Attorney General Bonta and the coalition argue that seeking bankruptcy protection doesn’t free a company from the laws protecting this data. Federal law bars companies from sharing personal information about children under 13 without a parent’s verified permission, and state privacy laws, including California’s Consumer Privacy Act, limit the sale of personal information unless people meaningfully understand what categories of their data could be sold and given a chance to say no. Character.AI’s own privacy policy told users, “we do not sell personal information for monetary consideration.”
In the filings, the coalition outlines deep concerns with the auction, including:
No protections for users. The proposed deal imposes no requirements on the buyer. Under the proposed sale terms, the buyer would not have to follow Character.AI’s privacy policies or honor users’ requests to opt out or delete their data, and nothing in the deal limits what the buyer could do with the data.
The data could be sold again. Once the deal is done, the buyer could hand the data to a related company or a new owner without telling users, the states or the court, or distribute and sell off the data itself.
Bidders know more than the court. Character.AI has given bidders information about its safety failures and the legal trouble it could face — information it hasn’t shared with the court, the states, or users.
Potentially illegal business model. Character.AI’s chatbot service appears to violate the states’ unfair and deceptive practice laws. Some states, including California, Colorado, New York, and Connecticut, have added additional clarity to their laws, enacting chatbot safety legislation that will go into effect on January 1, 2027 — triggering further violations.
An anonymous first bidder. The only bidder disclosed so far, Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC (Smirks), was set up as a Delaware company on November 13, 2026 — one day before Character.AI picked it as the base bid for the auction. The sale contract doesn’t name a parent company or any investors, and it allows foreign investors whose names would be shared only with Character.AI. The only employee it would have to hire is Character.AI’s top lawyer, even though the company told the court the bidder has no ties to it.
The states ask the court to deny the proposed sale terms, reject Smirks as the lead bidder and deny its proposed $350,000 “break-up fee” — a payment it would collect if Character.AI ends up selling to someone else. At a minimum, the states say, any sale must leave out kids’ chats and other sensitive data and include conditions that protect users.
Joining Attorney General Bonta in opposing this sale are the attorneys general of Alaska, Arkansas, Colorado, Connecticut, District of Columbia, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Virginia, Vermont, Washington, Wisconsin, and West Virginia.
