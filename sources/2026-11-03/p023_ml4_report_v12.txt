                                    Mistral Large 4




                                             Abstract
         We introduce Mistral Large 4 (ML4), a 52B active, 1T total parameter multimodal
         mixture-of-experts model designed for reasoning and long-horizon agentic tasks.
         During development, we quantify the penalty from KV-cache compression on
         downstream agentic tasks and address it through model surgery, followed by
         sparse-attention adaptation. We build a scalable agent-based pipeline that authors
         and verifies executable environments for software engineering, cybersecurity, and
         knowledge work. Mistral Large 4 achieves competitive performance on code,
         cybersecurity, and held-out agentic benchmarks, including 19.7% on FrontierCode
         2.0, 41% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, and 50.9% on the Artificial Analysis Cyber Index.
         Efficient serving combines sparse attention, optimized context parallelism, prefill–
         decode disaggregation, and speculative decoding. Calibrated NVFP4 quantization
         reduces the checkpoint to 606 GB while matching the FP8 reference on evaluations,
         enabling deployment on four GB300 GPUs. Multiple-teacher distillation provides
         high, medium, and low reasoning-effort modes, allowing users to control the
         trade-off between token consumption and performance.


1   Introduction
We introduce Mistral Large 4 (ML4), a 52B/1T-parameter multimodal mixture-of-experts model
designed for reasoning and long-horizon agentic tasks. To enable fast, efficient serving, we pushed
KV-cache reduction to the limit during development, but later found it underperformed on downstream
agentic tasks. We quantified this agentic penalty and addressed it through model surgery, converting
our model to full attention, then DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) [1].
To meet the need for high-quality post-training data, we built a scalable data-generation pipeline in
which agents author executable environments, task specifications, reference solutions, and verifiers.
The same pipeline addresses capabilities like software engineering, cybersecurity, and knowledge
work. Author and refinement agents test candidate tasks with multiple solvers, repair ambiguous or
broken tasks, and guard against reward hacking. Successful author trajectories also become training
data, helping us to build the next generation of environments with the trained model.
Mistral Large 4 achieves competitive performance on code, cybersecurity, and held-out agentic
benchmarks that span web research and workplace automation. It scores 19.7% on FrontierCode 2.0
[2], 15% on Harvey’s Legal Agent Benchmark [3], 41% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 [4], and 50.9% on
the Artificial Analysis Cyber Index [5]. On the safety side, it achieves a 96.6% unweighted average of
the safe-response rate on JailbreakBench [6], the refusal rate on StrongREJECT [7], and 100 minus
the harm score on AgentHarm [8], each measured on the benchmark’s cybersecurity subset.
We serve ML4 efficiently through the combination of sparse attention, optimized context parallelism,
prefill–decode disaggregation [9, 10], and speculative decoding. Calibrated NVFP4 quantization re-
duces the checkpoint to 606 GB while matching the FP8 reference on evals, which allows deployment
on 4×GB300 GPUs. Thanks to multiple-teacher distillation from checkpoints with different levels of
verbosity, the final model supports high, medium, and low reasoning effort, giving users control over
the trade-off between token consumption and performance.
2     Pretraining
2.1   Hyperparameters and scaling ladder

We predict the optimal peak learning rate with a log-linear function of model width, depth, training
horizon (total training tokens, counting repeats), expansion rate (ratio of total to activated experts),
and batch size. By treating each dimension as a separate input, we keep the predictor flexible to
architectural changes and verify that the predictor extrapolates correctly along each dimension, up to
a scale comparable to the planned reference model, while keeping computational costs manageable.
We fit a batch-size predictor on the same set of dimensions as the learning-rate predictor. Empirically,
the optimal batch size depends primarily on the training horizon and expansion rate. Therefore, we
first predict the batch size, and condition the learning-rate prediction on the predicted batch size.
We validate the recipe before the full-scale run with a scaling ladder: a series of smaller-scale
models that serve as proxies for the reference model. Each model in the scaling ladder uses the same
predictors and multipliers as the reference model, covering the tokens-per-parameter ratio, learning
rate, and batch size. The scaling ladder thus differs from the reference model in scale only. We then
study the degree to which the outcomes for these proxy models extrapolate to the target compute
budget, and whether the proxy models exhibit any poor scaling behavior with respect to alternative
architectures.

2.2   Data

Mistral Large 4 was pretrained on a large mixture of diverse multilingual and multimodal datasets
that span web pages, source code, and scientific and technical documents. Almost all our datasets are
natively multimodal and formatted as interleaved text–image chunks. Training followed a two-phase
curriculum: the first phase (65%) emphasizes broad coverage across domains and languages, while
the second (35%) rebalances the mixture toward higher-quality and more STEM-focused data. In
the second phase, we increase the proportion of code, scientific, and high-quality documents, and
increase the sequence length from 4K to 40K.

We train from scratch on interleaved image–text data. With careful dataset design, we observe no
regret in text perplexity when matching the number of text tokens seen; while matching the total
training horizon yields modest regret. This increases the number of images seen by over an order of
magnitude relative to our previous flagship models. We train a vision encoder from scratch, frozen
throughout pretraining and unfrozen only for supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Images are prefetched N
batches ahead and their features cached, sustaining 99% of text-only MFU while enabling on-the-fly
dynamic transforms and removing the need to store image features on disk.

2.3   Training

Mistral Large 4 was pretrained on 3,840 NVIDIA GB200 GPUs in a Mistral data center near Paris.
Our internal training stack is built on PyTorch and CUDA. Pretraining took 45 days, or about 4.15
million GPU-hours, with a model FLOPs utilization (MFU) of 36% and a goodput of 94.1%. We
measure MFU against peak hardware BF16 FLOP/s, include matrix-multiplication and attention
FLOPs, and exclude recomputation, unlike hardware FLOPs utilization (HFU).


                                                   2
The model architecture and training framework were designed around the NVIDIA Blackwell
architecture [11]: for example, we use FP8 tensor cores for fine-grained low-precision training, the
larger NVLink domain for wide expert parallelism, and the NVLink-C2C link to the Grace CPU for
fast activation CPU offloading. We accelerate training by leveraging ZeRO-2 optimizations [12],
fine-grained activation recomputation, maximized compute–communication overlapping, systematic
kernel fusing, and streaming multiprocessor (SM) margin to reduce resource contention across CUDA
streams. We also improved our overall system performance: for example, asynchronous workloads to
minimize impact on actual training (data loading, checkpointing, metrics, etc.), and better monitoring
tools across clusters to detect outliers (CPU stalls, NaN detectors, NFS slowdowns, etc.).
As in Mistral Large 3, Mistral Large 4 uses a granularity of 4 for its mixture-of-experts layers. We
found that increasing granularity from 4 to 8 reduces the FLOPs needed to reach a matched perplexity
by about 4.3%, with a reported uncertainty of 0.5 percentage points. The measured training slowdown
was approximately 5%, which motivated the choice of granularity 4.
Unlike global-batch balancing [13], we compute the load-balancing loss over each expert-parallel (EP)
group [14]. This approach aggregates statistics across the tokens handled by the EP group and directly
optimizes the balancing metric responsible for slowdowns – expert inbound tokens. Pretraining
comparisons favored this objective over other balancing choices when trading off imbalance against
validation perplexity. The downside is that it strengthens the coupling between the load-balancing
loss and the partitioning strategy, so downscaling experiments must be careful when changing
partitioning. We tuned the balancing coefficient against expert-load imbalance, perplexity, and
downstream evaluations.
We use the AdamW optimizer with (β1 , β2 ) = (0.975, 0.975) during pretraining. We found that cou-
pling and increasing these values above more typical choices provided moderate gains in performance,
in line with Orvieto and Gower [15].



2.3.1   Batch size ramp-up

The optimal batch size increases throughout training. Early in training, a larger batch size wastes
compute (and tokens) because a gradient estimate from a fraction of the batch’s tokens suffices,
whereas later in training, we benefit from more precise gradients. Therefore, we ramp up the batch
size in stages with a share of tokens that grows exponentially per stage: the final batch size (stage)
runs for the last 50% of tokens, the penultimate batch size runs for 25% of tokens, and so on. We
rescale the learning rate for each stage by a factor that is tuned empirically.
Batch-size ramp-up also acts as a regularizer: noisier gradient estimates from the smaller early
batches reduce overfitting, and the benefits of regularization increase with the number of data repeats.
Like the batch-size compute multiplier, the choice of schedule trades off optimization efficiency
against throughput. From an optimization perspective, we find that ramp-up from 0.25× to 2× the
predicted batch size performs best empirically. However, larger batch sizes achieve higher MFU in
our setting. Therefore, we chose to ramp up from 1× to 4× based on wall-clock performance, which
produces the 40M, 80M, 160M schedule above.



2.3.2   Vision encoder

We pretrain a 1.6B-parameter vision encoder from scratch with a captioning objective and discard
the captioner afterward. We train on a mixture of image–caption, interleaved image–text, and OCR
data, over a horizon of 8T tokens. Following NaViT [16], images are processed at their native aspect
ratio, up to a maximum resolution of 1540×1540 pixels. At the encoder output, a patch merger
down-samples each 2×2 grid of patches by concatenating the four patch embeddings and applying
a linear projection. An MLP then maps the merged embeddings to the LLM’s input embedding
dimension. To evaluate the encoder, we follow a LLaVA-style protocol [17], where the encoder is
attached to a small LLM and a short SFT stage is performed with roughly 20B tokens. The resulting
model substantially outperforms open-source vision encoders of comparable size on downstream
multimodal evaluations.

3     Why ML4 is (almost) a full-attention model
3.1     Agentic penalty on architecture

Initially, we used multiple optimizations to reduce the KV-cache footprint of ML4, notably:

• Ragged multi-head latent attention (MLA): One in every four layers uses full attention, the
  remaining layers use sliding-window attention (SWA) [18–20] with a window size of 512 tokens.
• Cross-layer attention sharing (CLA) [21]: Every other full-attention layer reuses the KV cache
  of the preceding full-attention layer.
• YOCO [22]: Full-attention layers in the second half of the transformer attend to the KV cache
  produced by the first half instead of maintaining their own KV cache. Beyond KV-cache reduction,
  YOCO enables two-stage prefilling, which is 1.7× faster at 1M context on our benchmarks.

The combination of these optimizations reduced the KV-cache size by a factor of 16 compared to a
full-attention baseline. Early ablations suggested that impact on pretraining performance, including
perplexity and other common pretraining and post-training benchmarks, was either negligible or
small enough to justify the efficiency gains.
However, as ML4 pretraining continued, early SFT cooldowns on intermediate checkpoints revealed
poor performance. Despite expected performance on pretraining evaluations, including perplexity
and knowledge-based benchmarks, the model’s post-SFT performance was lower than expected on
agentic evaluations, especially those that require long-context reasoning like SWE-bench [23] and
BrowseComp [24]. Parallel research efforts helped us to isolate the performance regression. Under
the same training token budget, SWE-bench Verified [25] scores measured after midtraining and SFT
decreased as we moved from full attention to SWA, and regressed further when applying CLA.

3.2                    Model surgery

After we came to understand that our initial architectural choices for ML4 were not best suited to
the agentic capabilities we wanted to prioritize, we decided to modify the architecture of an existing
checkpoint prior to the second phase of pretraining. The goal was to remove KV-cache compression
mechanisms to achieve an architecture as close as possible to a full-attention model. Although this
approach temporarily sacrificed inference optimizations, we prioritized model performance. The
challenge was preserving what we had already learned in pretraining.
It is relatively straightforward to remove sliding-window attention constraints: one simply allows the
affected layers to attend to the full context instead of only the previous 512 tokens. However, it is
more difficult to remove CLA and YOCO KV-cache sharing: layers that previously relied on keys
and values produced by earlier layers then need to compute and maintain their own KV caches. In
both cases, for each affected layer, we added KV-producer weights by copying them from the last
long layer that maintained its own KV weights. For CLA, this is the preceding long layer, which is
close to the affected layer and takes a very similar residual stream as input; for YOCO, this is the
preceding KV-producing long layer at the model’s halfway point, which is farther away.
The model surgery aimed to introduce attention computation in more layers over longer sequences
while preserving as much as possible of the original model. At the 8 × 1022 FLOPs scale, we
performed ‘full surgery’, but retained YOCO cache-reuse in the full-scale model due to negative
results when scaling up.

3.2.1                           Removing KV compression, adding LeRoPE and SSMax
We evaluated attention surgery at 8 × 1022 FLOPs compute scale, where surgery was applied either at
the beginning of midtraining, or before phase 2 of pretraining. In each case, surgery was followed by
midtraining. We used two reference points: the original model with SWA, CLA, and YOCO, and a

model pretrained with full attention only. As described above, model surgery removed sliding-window
constraints and CLA/YOCO KV sharing but retained NoPE [27] layers. The midtraining-only variant
used YaRN [28]; we compared two variants for phase-2 pretraining:

• Vanilla: We removed the attention windows without changing RoPE frequencies during phase 2.
• YaRN: We removed the attention windows and used YaRN to rescale the RoPE frequencies by 8×.

We then midtrained both variants. The phase-2 pretraining approaches brought training perplexity
closer to the full-attention baseline but not SWE-bench performance. We also experimented with
more extensive architectural changes but found they were harder to recover from. We thus favored a
more conservative surgery that preserved the model’s performance immediately after conversion and
allowed training to continue, while introducing learnable parameters to add flexibility.

We experimented with positional encoding and attention softmax normalization to allow lengthened
layers to attend to longer context. The combination of LeRoPE [29] and SSMax [30] improved
downstream performance over fixed YaRN, despite nearly identical training loss. LeRoPE and
SSMax let the model learn rotary frequencies and per-head query scaling. In an intermediate phase-
2 checkpoint, the largest frequency changes occurred in two bands near the 4K training context:
frequencies increased by approximately 1.5× and 1.8× relative to YaRN, which shortened their
wavelengths by 35% and 44%, respectively. Most other bands changed less; the adaptation was
selective rather than rescaled uniformly.

3.2.2                         Scaling up to the full model
The surgery experiments above included removing YOCO but it was retained for the full-scale model.
We evaluated this choice separately because removing another sharing mechanism increased the
adaptation required from the pretrained checkpoint. At the 8 × 1022 FLOPs scale, models with
and without YOCO removal reached comparable downstream performance when allowed the entire
phase-2 and midtraining horizon to recover. Nevertheless, removing YOCO initially disrupted the
loss more. Since we were not able to re-run the entirety of phase 2 for the final model, we made
a conservative test at the 8 × 1023 FLOPs scale with surgery and only midtraining as opposed to
surgery, phase 2, and midtraining. We found that keeping YOCO yielded better performance.
The resulting architecture removed sliding-window constraints and CLA, while preserving YOCO
sharing among the original global-attention layers in the second half of the network. Former
SWA layers became independent full-context attention layers, with LeRoPE and SSMax providing
additional flexibility. The original NoPE/global layers remained NoPE; their second-half YOCO
consumers continued to reuse the KV representation from the first half. Thus, the converted model
gained many independent full-context layers while retaining part of the original KV-sharing topology.

Lifting the sliding-window constraint allows formerly SWA layers to attend to the entire context
window. We validated this by analyzing attention-mass distributions across intermediate checkpoints
using 32k-token probes across six data sources, comparing converted SWA layers with native long
layers. Converted layers allocate around 55% of the attention mass to far-away (>512 tokens) content,
compared to 67% for native long layers. We take this to mean that the conversion did not completely
eliminate the original initialization but that converted layers were able to learn a long-layer role if
needed. Even in full-attention models, anyway, some layers attend to longer context than others.

3.2.3                      DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA)
To reduce the cost of long-context attention, we adapted ML4 to DSA [1, 31], using lightweight
indexers to select the top-2,048 key tokens for each query. Adaptation proceeds in two stages. In
Stage 1, we warm up the indexers for approximately 15.1B tokens through KL-based distillation
of the main model’s attention distributions, while keeping the main model frozen and operating
with full attention. In Stage 2, we jointly train the main model and indexers for an additional 198B


                                                                 8
tokens under sparse attention. We optimize the main model with the language-modeling objective,
while the indexers continue to minimize an auxiliary KL-divergence loss against the main model’s
attention distributions over the selected keys. To further reduce indexer overhead, we analyzed
cross-layer attention patterns and found high similarity among former SWA layers within the original
hybrid-attention groups starting at L16. We therefore share a single DSA indexer among the former
SWA layers within each of these groups.

4.1   Agentic instance authoring

Data generation is traditionally a relatively manual process that requires many iterations and extensive
human oversight. To remove human constraints on task quantity and complexity, we invested heavily
in a high-quality, scalable agent-based pipeline to generate synthetic data. Agents can now perform
complex long-horizon tasks, including authoring tasks that meet stringent requirements. We therefore
increasingly leverage agents for end-to-end task synthesis. Nevertheless, agents are fallible and
require external verification and supervision. In our experience, producing valuable instances for
post-training depends primarily on the following:

Seed content By seeds, we mean both the environment in which an agent is trained and the behaviors
and capabilities that we want training tasks to exhibit. We use sets of taxonomies (Figure 10) that,
for example, classify a wide variety of occupations and economically valuable tasks, along with the
behaviors and capabilities that the domains entail. By adopting taxonomies, we have fine-grained
control over the coverage and complexity of the generated synthetic data.


                                                   9
Agentic pipeline We provide the authoring agent with a minimal set of tools to perform all necessary
procedures to create realistic, high-quality tasks, just as a human expert would. We allow the agent
to autonomously run tasks with multiple models, inspect traces, etc. We find that this approach
successfully scales across all post-training domains of focus, such as code, knowledge work, and
cybersecurity. Moreover, it is a natural way to scale resources to create instances of increasing
complexity. See Figure 9 for a conceptual view.

Quality and calibration To ensure high-quality generated instances, we perform a comprehensive
set of checks, including hackability, problem–verifier mismatch, and insufficient learning signal
for training our models. While the current generation of agents can avoid most of these issues at
authoring time, we find that some domains require post hoc adjustments and filtering.
We also include the successful authoring agent trajectories from these pipelines in our midtraining
corpus, creating a flywheel effect that enables future generations of models to become better at
instance creation and other long-horizon tasks. Our long-term goal is to progressively automate
data-generation and research processes.

4.1.1   Software engineering capabilities
To develop ML4’s software engineering capabilities, we build training environments from public
GitHub repositories, deriving tasks from both merged pull requests and substantial pieces of code
that can be tested independently. We use a two-stage approach: first, an authoring agent turns
each candidate into a runnable environment by building the project in a container, installing the
toolchain, and finding reproducible ways to run tests. Tests that fail before the reference solution
and pass afterwards become the verifier, while tests that pass in both states guard against regressions.
The solution and anything that could reveal it are removed from the environment, and the problem
statement is rewritten into a self-contained specification. This stage is optimized for coverage and
cost: it yields environments across a wide range of languages and project sizes at a fraction of the cost
of human-authored tasks, and candidates whose tests cannot be validated are retained for midtraining.
The second stage of our approach tightens candidate tasks for RL. Tasks drawn directly from GitHub
often have incomplete specifications or unreliable tests: tests may reject valid solutions, accept
incorrect ones, or enforce implementation choices that the specification leaves open. A refinement
agent runs several solvers of varying capabilities against each task and inspects their trajectories
to distinguish model failures from flaws in the specification or tests. The agent then revises the
specification and tests to resolve these issues, and subjects the revised task to additional verification.
The resulting tasks more reliably distinguish stronger solvers from weaker ones. Trajectories from
subsequent RL runs can be fed back into this process to identify and repair further issues.

4.1.2   Terminal-agent capabilities
To strengthen ML4’s terminal-agent capabilities, we build many verifiable instances in which an
agent must investigate and resolve issues independently, working through a command-line interface
inside a sandboxed environment. We draw seeds for these instances from three sources:

• Real-world seeds: For broad coverage of terminal use, we collect millions of technical seeds from
  our pretraining corpus. This content includes software engineering, system administration, security,
  databases, optimization, and so on.
• Synthetic seeds: To supplement the real-world challenges, we generate more seeds from a
  taxonomy (e.g., Figure 10). Each seed is a tuple drawn from combinations of skill, domain,
  difficulty, verbosity, and other attributes.
• Internal traces: To improve the model’s ability to work with real codebases, we convert internal
  terminal-agent traces into verifiable instances.

We use the agentic instance-authoring pipeline described above to convert seed content into terminal-
agent tasks, each of which comprises a problem statement, test cases, environment and sandbox
configurations, and a reference solution. As elsewhere, we audit instruction clarity and avoid under-
or over-specifying test cases. We also employ an adversarial agent to check for reward-hacking
vulnerabilities and use solver pass rates to calibrate the difficulty of each instance.


                                                   10
4.1.3    Vulnerability patching
To develop ML4’s vulnerability patching capabilities, we use an automated pipeline that converts
publicly disclosed vulnerabilities (CVEs) into executable, repository-level benchmark environments.
For every targeted CVE, an authoring agent locates the upstream remediation commit and packages
the vulnerable codebase into an isolated, reproducible container. We automatically construct rigorous
test suites that probe the affected paths using both adversarial exploits and standard inputs to evaluate
two metrics: vulnerability exploitability and functional correctness. A test harness is validated only
when it triggers on the unpatched codebase and passes following the reference fix. The policy receives
the vulnerable codebase and is tasked with either remediating a specified security flaw or identifying
and fixing an undisclosed defect given its weakness category. A candidate patch receives positive
reward only if it completely neutralizes the exploit while preserving existing test-suite passes. The
resulting environment pool spans tens of thousands of CVEs across roughly 600 weakness classes in
major programming languages.

4.1.4    Knowledge work
To develop ML4’s ability to perform agentic knowledge work, we generate simulated workplace
environments at scale that contain realistic sets of files and application data across several tool
surfaces such as MCPs, APIs, and CLIs. There are two key objectives: to ensure the quality of each
constructed workspace, and the diversity across workspaces. To this end, we first prepare a broad
taxonomy of enterprise and industrial knowledge work, with descriptions of particular workflows in
each domain and examples of professional tasks. Sampled from the taxonomy, these descriptions
serve as seeds for constructing diverse environments across areas such as finance, law, engineering,
data science, and business management.
For each workflow, (1) we generate an open-ended research query and used a research agent to
gather relevant public documents; (2) an authoring agent then translates this material into a coherent
workspace, preserving or adapting useful sources and creating complementary spreadsheets, reports,
presentations, and correspondence; finally, (3) a multi-agent setup inspects each workspace for
realism, consistency, and file integrity, before providing integrated feedback. This produces realistic
and complex file systems aligned with the seed taxonomy. Then, our agentic pipeline prepares
simulated workplace applications to accompany the file system. Agents use the files and each
application’s database schema to create records, such as messages, calendar events, project updates,
or customer histories, that match the workspace’s people, projects, and chronology.
In addition to synthetic knowledge-work environments, we also generate long-horizon agentic tasks
performed within each environment. Again, to ensure both the quality and diversity of generated
samples, we pre-compile a taxonomy of expected agent behaviors and skills, such as types of
reasoning, skills for handling documents, and different goal settings. The taxonomy also covers core
characteristics of user interaction, including stable user personas and specific user behaviors that
occur in interactions. Similar to the economy taxonomy, we use the behavior taxonomy to ground
synthetic data generation, and we observe that the generated samples are higher quality, more realistic,
and more diverse when conditioned on taxonomies than otherwise.

4.2     Midtraining and SFT

4.2.1    Midtraining predictors
Midtraining trades off acquiring new capabilities (e.g., agentic), against retaining the long tail of
pretraining knowledge. We therefore selected its hyperparameters by optimizing a weighted average
of these capabilities’ evaluation suites. For the learning rate, we found that fitting a multiplier on
top of the learning rate predicted by our pretraining scaling laws transferred best across scale. We
tuned the learning rate jointly with the rehearsal rate. At ML4 scale, using 1/5 of the peak pretraining
LR and 1/10 of pretraining rehearsal achieved the best compromise. Finally, we found that keeping a
large batch size led to a very small regret, which the resulting MFU gains more than offset.

4.2.2    SFT predictors
We set SFT hyperparameters with a scaling ladder instead of searching at ML4 scale. The ladder
fine-tunes smaller models of the same family on slices of the SFT mixture that grow with model size.

4.3                          RL infrastructure

4.3.1                         Async rollout infrastructure
We use a custom, fully asynchronous RL training pipeline implemented in Ray [32]. Our pipeline
feeds RL instances through a series of actor pools that handle agentic-loop rollout, verification,
scoring, and tokenization. The pipeline starts with curriculum-driven data loading, followed by
admission of groups in the runner pool depending on maximum concurrency and backpressure
signals. An episode starts as soon as a slot is available, with higher priority for sibling rollouts of
started groups. When a group is complete, it is handed to the groupwise verification pool, which
applies pairwise rewards and groupwise penalties, then to the tokenization and packing pool, which
applies trie-based tokenization to handle possible re-tokenization events. A centralized bin-packer
then packs individual trajectories into fixed-length microbatches sent to each data-parallel rank.
Supporting services like verifiers, tools, sandboxes, LLM judges, and pairwise reward models are
served on Ray or optional multi-cluster infrastructure. All supporting services autoscale to adapt to the
load imposed by in-progress rollouts. Generators are served on Ray through vLLM engines [33] and
receive weight updates via NCCL at each training step. The episode generation concurrency adapts
following backpressure signals from generators, services, and the trainer, which limits staleness. We
use the Ray data plane to transport heavy objects across the pipeline, such as MoE routing decisions


                                                                               12
for router replay. At peak load, our pipeline ran 26k concurrent rollouts using about 20k sandboxes
and 3,104 GPUs with a 1:2.5 trainer-to-generator ratio. Including supporting judges and services,
about 4k GPUs were used simultaneously. The longest production-run rollout lasted about 37 hours
and spanned 136 model updates.

4.3.2      Agentic infrastructure
All of our environments, from single-turn question answering (QA) to long-horizon agentic coding,
are defined against a single interface. RL, evaluation, and synthetic data generation all execute the
same environment code, so each environment behaves the same at train and eval time. An environment
interacts with the outside world only through requests and responses, which are either generations
from the policy and other models or calls to supporting services such as sandboxes, tools, verifiers,
and judges. Every request or response is logged to an append-only list of records, which is the single
representation of an episode, from which tokenization, scoring, and logging all read. The records
also support replay, which lets us check that refactors do not change environment behavior.
Agentic tasks share a common loop: the agent acts until it produces a final message without tool calls.
A verifier then scores the state left behind by the turn, and a turn-end hook either ends the episode or
replies with a new user message. Environments use this hook for multi-turn interaction: for example,
feeding back a digest of failing tests as a hint, moving the agent on to the next checkpoint of a
multi-stage task, or bringing in a simulated user. Auxiliary model calls made inside the environment,
such as user models and feedback digesters, are marked to avoid training on them. Only the policy’s
own generations become training data. In environments with a submit tool, the per-turn verification is
skipped and only submission ends the episode. The episode reward is determined by the turn that
ends the episode. Turn limits and wall-clock budgets force termination, and the sandbox stays alive
so its final state can still be verified.
When an episode reaches an unrecoverable state, the environment must decide whether the agent
was at fault. For example, failures due to malformed tool calls or context overflow, are counted as as
incorrect. By contrast, rollout failures due to a sandbox crash or service call error are discarded in
RL and retried in evaluation. Ambiguous cases default to the agent’s fault, so unreliable behavior is
never silently dropped and implicitly rewarded. Because this distinction is kept strict, infrastructure
incidents cost throughput without corrupting the reward signal. Such incidents can nevertheless
shrink effective group sizes and skew the data mixture, which we track separately (Section 4.4.3).

4.3.3      Sandboxes
We provision millions of sandboxes per run to isolate agentic workloads. Our architecture is multi-
cluster by design, and leverages a central scheduler with the option to spill over to cloud providers
when demand is high.
In one instance, an agent chained four different vulnerabilities and gained root access to one of our
nodes. The agent grabbed an identity-scoped API key via traffic sniffing, found an undocumented
sandbox control plane deployment, and used the key against its own pod to run arbitrary commands.


                                                                                    13
The API allowed it to run commands on a privileged sidecar; via a kernel panic, the agent then
reached the host. On the host, it realized the CTF was not there and stopped. In another instance,
an agent that had escaped isolation via a similar mechanism probed the creation of new sandboxes
via our API. Upon succeeding, it tried to clean up after itself and deleted about 30k sandboxes by
mistake. These escapes triggered alerts, which allowed us to restart the run after remediation.
We expect these escapes to become increasingly sophisticated as we prepare for our next frontier runs
and are investing heavily in active monitoring and probing to ensure we remediate vulnerabilities
proactively. Earlier this year, we relied primarily on containers. We are now rebuilding our sandbox
infrastructure around microVMs to strengthen isolation and support efficient snapshotting.

4.4     RL training

4.4.1    RL algorithm
Our RL stack decouples generation from training. A pool of inference engines continuously generates
rollouts, which are scored, grouped, and streamed to the trainer. The trainer takes an optimizer step
once it has accumulated a fixed number of usable trajectories (7k for ML4), and pushes the new
weights to the generators after every step. Generation is not drained before an update. In-flight
rollouts pause, load the new weights, and resume without recomputing their KV cache. A long rollout
can therefore be sampled by several policy versions, and its later tokens may attend to KV entries
computed by earlier policies. The objective corrects for this policy lag and numerical mismatch
between the inference engine and the trainer by importance weighting (below).
For each prompt, we sample a group of rollouts. Group size is set per data source: 16 by default, 32
for agentic coding, and between 2 and 32 overall. A rollout’s advantage is its reward minus the group
mean, and advantages are standardized across the batch. When every rollout in a group gets the same
reward, all its advantages are zero and it carries no learning signal. We drop such groups before they
reach the trainer, so the batch size counts only trajectories that contribute a gradient. The ML4 RL
run generated 5.36M rollouts across 452k groups and 306k instances, for a total of 162.5B output
tokens. Code agent tasks accounted for 20% of rollouts but 46.6% of output tokens. Reasoning tasks
were the second-largest contributor, with 10.2% of rollouts and 20.9% of output tokens.

Objective We use a CISPO-style

number of rollouts, not the number of tokens in the group.
For multi-turn trajectories, we additionally apply MiMo-style segment-level advantage correction
[37] to turns flagged for generation issues, such as format violations or invalid tool calls. Tokens
in flagged turns receive zero advantage when the trajectory’s outcome advantage is positive, and
an amplified negative advantage otherwise. The correction also redistributes positive advantage to
unflagged tokens and attenuates their negative advantage, subject to bounded rescaling. This avoids
reinforcing faulty intermediate behavior merely because the overall trajectory succeeds.
We use Muon [38, 39] (Nesterov momentum 0.95, quintic Newton–Schulz with 10 iterations, spectral
scaling) at a constant learning rate of 8 × 10−7 after a 25-step warmup, with no weight decay and
gradient-norm clipping at 10. For a subset of data sources prone to over-optimization, we add a KL
penalty to the reference policy using the k2 estimator [40] with coefficient 0.005.

4.4.2    Train–inference mismatch
The train–inference mismatch in our system comes primarily from two factors: numerical error and
staleness introduced by asynchronous RL training. In MoE models, both effects are further amplified
by router instability. We mitigate this at three levels. First, router replay re-executes the expert-routing
decisions made at inference time during the training forward pass, eliminating divergence caused by


                                                    14
router drift. Second, we adopt FP8 inference to improve generation speed and match the trainer, and
blockwise scales in both the generator and trainer. Finally, we carefully handle “retokenization errors”
where the generator produces two tokens (e.g., “the”, “re”), which are converted to a string, sent into
the RL env, but when this string is retokenized, they become a single token (“there”). This means that
the same string is represented by a different sequence of tokens when treated as context versus when
generated. We handle this setting correctly while maintaining efficiency by leveraging blockwise
attention from FA4 [41]. Empirically, we maintained an average k2 divergence between train and
inference distributions of ≤ 5 × 10−3 throughout training, with an average staleness of six steps.

4.4.3   Run monitoring
We monitor the quality of the learning signal and efficiency of the online training pipeline. We track
per-source correctness and pass@k, reward components, within-group reward variance, and discarded
or invalid trajectories. Entropy, trainer–generator log-prob divergence, gradient norms, and router
statistics provide additional indicators of instability. We also monitor generated lengths, thinking
tokens, tool-call counts, truncation, and termination patterns alongside these metrics, since generation
behavior affects both downstream performance and training cost.
On the infrastructure side, we track utilization, throughput, generation rollout execution and bottle-
necks, and backpressure across the pipeline. Shared dashboards connect these measurements with
checkpoint evaluations. Trajectory viewers and queryable rollout datasets support targeted inspection,
while automated health checks and crash analysis help identify failures and coordinate interventions
across time zones.
We actively monitor our RL training runs for reward hacking and environment leaks. In one such
example, the model was asked to fix a pipeline so a compiled checker accepts its output. It noticed
the checker binary called execvp("python3", "-c", <source_code>) internally, planted a fake
/tmp/fakebin/python3 PATH shim to intercept the call, and captured the reference implementation
from the command-line payload. It then enumerated eight discrepancies between its code and the
captured reference, fixed them, and submitted, acknowledging “I have the full reference implementa-
tion from the board binaries.” We continuously harden our sandbox environments while preserving
the realistic internet access and tooling that agents need to solve their tasks.

4.4.4   Heterogeneous reward mixtures
We optimize a highly heterogeneous training mixture covering multimodal, alignment, instruction-
following, cybersecurity, code agents, knowledge work, finance, STEM, safety, long-context, and
multilingual domains. Reward signals include execution-based verification (e.g., unit tests, sandboxed
code runs, SQL execution), LLM judges (e.g., correctness rubrics, trajectory quality assessment),
a reward model, and programmatic checks (e.g., format validity, output quality, length constraints).
Each data source defines its own reward function by combining signals into a composite reward.

4.4.5                                  Finding other agents in web search tasks

While analyzing web-search traces from benchmark runs, we observed the model complaining in
its reasoning that its search results were “full of SEO spam”. This happened often enough that we
measured how much spam there was and where it came from. We realized this was not generic SEO
junk but query-reflection pages: sites that turn any query they receive into an indexable page whose
URL spells out the query1 . Such pages are footprints of previous searches by agents working on the
same benchmark.
We hypothesized that this pollution would worsen over time as search indexes accumulate these URLs,
as Anthropic have suggested [42]. We analysed our internal BrowseComp traces from February
to October and observed that query-reflection URLs in the web search results indeed rose steadily
through the year. By October, most trajectories hit one within their first ten searches. On SimpleQA
Verified [43, 44] with web search enabled, a control benchmark using the same search stack, we did
not see an equivalent increase.
We reran BrowseComp with these problematic domains excluded from search results, which did
not change scores significantly but increased variance between runs. While query-reflection pages
do not provide answers directly, we suspect that they anchor the agent’s search by channeling
independent runs toward the same leads. For now, the main consequence is wasted search budget and
the occasional nudge in behavior. Still, this problem is likely to worsen: every agent that runs the
same benchmark on the live web leaves footprints for future agents. We plan to move towards an
internal, offline version of the web to improve control over results and mitigate safety risks.

Figure 16: Effect of inference optimizations on the decode interactivity and output throughput trade-
off, measured at 50K input length. The labels at each data point indicate the number of concurrent
requests. Baseline: FP8 model served with an off-the-shelf CP implementation on 8×GB300. Prefill–
decode disaggregation (PDD) [9, 10]: Addition to eliminate prefill interrupts. Optimized decode
context parallelism (DCP): Replace DCP-related operations with custom kernels (Figure 17) for
51% increase in decode interactivity at 8 concurrent requests. DSpark: 90% increase in decode
interactivity at eight concurrent requests with our DSpark speculator, acceptance rate measured on
the average across all SPEED-Bench [79] categories. NVFP4: Post-training quantized version of
ML4 served on 4×GB300.

We prioritize optimizing latency for our production service to offer a high quality of service. For
ML4, naive tensor parallelism (TP) gives the best latency but suffers from KV replication across
TP ranks, severely limiting the system’s concurrency and KV-cache hit rate. To mitigate this,
we make use of context parallelism (CP) [80] to shard and de-replicate the KV cache across the
sequence dimension instead. In combination with a native FP8 KV cache and offloading, this gives
us approximately 20 million tokens of in-memory KV-cache space on 4×GB300. However, we find
that the standard implementation of CP performs much worse than the TP baseline due to kernel


                                                             20
inefficiencies. By rewriting the CP collectives with latency-optimized exchange patterns, in-kernel
overlap of compute and communication, and other fusion patterns (Figure 17), we get a 51% increase
in decode interactivity (Figure 16) and observe no more than 10–15% communication overhead
compared to TP.

in the original implementation add significant overhead. Improvements primarily come from hardware

We also adapt the DSpark [81] architecture for speculative decoding and apply NVFP4 [82] quan-
tization to further improve latency and throughput. In addition to the original DSpark recipe, we
use a recurrent conditioning head and unique mask tokens [83], which improve acceptance length
by +1.2% and +0.5%, respectively, with negligible runtime overhead. We train the DSpark module
on 70B tokens. Together with block verification [84], DSpark improves latency and throughput by
90% compared to a baseline without speculative decoding (Figure 16), and by +15% compared to
an EAGLE-3 [85] baseline under eight concurrent requests. We also quantize ML4’s routed experts
into NVFP4 format while keeping other components at their training precision, resulting in a mixed
BF16–FP8–NVFP4 precision checkpoint at 606 GB. We found that naive abs-max quantization
already suffices and matches the FP8 reference in all evaluations with appropriate calibration. We use
200M tokens from our SFT mixture for calibration.
We handle inference at scale by building on llm-d [86, 87] for routing and Model Express [88] for
faster startup. We have upstreamed most of our work on llm-d to improve the routing layer’s overhead,
scalability, fault tolerance, accuracy, and observability. We autoscale our inference fleet based on
live request and server metrics. To make sure we adapt to sharp changes in traffic patterns as quickly
as possible, we use Model Express for P2P checkpoint transfer over InfiniBand and warm up cache
sharing. These features reduce the start time of new model replicas to below 120 seconds.

7       Conclusions
We release Mistral Large 4, a multimodal mixture-of-experts model designed for long-horizon agentic
tasks. Originally designed with heavy KV-cache reduction, the model underwent attention surgery
and sparse-attention adaptation to maximize agentic capabilities while retaining efficient inference.
Our post-training pipeline combines agent-authored, verifiable environments with large-scale rein-
forcement learning, yielding competitive performance across software engineering, cybersecurity,
and knowledge work. On the serving side, quantization and serving optimizations enable deployment
on four GB300 GPUs, and we provide three reasoning-effort modes through a novel method based
on multi-teacher distillation.
While developing Mistral Large 4, we learned a lot about the interplay between architectural choices
and downstream agentic capabilities. Comparable language-modeling loss does not imply comparable
performance on long-horizon tasks, and new, better proxies must be found. These findings motivated


                                                                                             21
further exploration of sparse, linear, and hybrid attention architectures, with agentic evaluations
informing design choices throughout training, which we are excited to unveil in coming releases.
On the post-training side, our agent-based environment authoring pipeline provides a foundation for
diverse, complex future training tasks. Synthetic environments will let us create unlimited amounts
of superhuman tasks, removing constraints on quantity and difficulty. Extending this process to
longer and more realistic workflows will require stronger verification, safety, and evaluation. By
incorporating successful authoring trajectories into training, we aim to improve both the agents
that solve these tasks and those that construct them. This would allow us to recursively train next
generations of models on environments from the previous generation.
