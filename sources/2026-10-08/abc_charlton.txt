 Australia outlines plans to make AI companies prove their safety systems work
 By chief digital political correspondent Clare Armstrong 
 Topic: AI
 Posted Thu 8 Oct 2026 at 1:19pm Thu 8 Oct 2026 at 1:19pm Thu 8 Oct 2026 at 1:19pm , updated Thu 8 Oct 2026 at 5:16pm Thu 8 Oct 2026 at 5:16pm Thu 8 Oct 2026 at 5:16pm 
 Andrew Charlton says AI products need to be tested for safety. ( ABC News: Matt Roberts ) 
 In short:
 The federal government wants AI developers to demonstrate their safety systems are effective under proposed laws drawing on banking and aviation regulation.
 It argues voluntary safeguards are inadequate when companies face intense pressure to develop increasingly powerful technology.
 What's next?
 Labor is finalising national AI standards due by the end of the year with plans to legislate the new rules in 2027.

 Link copied 
 Share Share article 
 Australia would require AI companies to prove they are properly testing models and preventing harm under proposed new laws modelled on banking and aviation safety rules.
 The federal government is finalising plans to regulate frontier artificial intelligence through national standards due by the end of the year, with recent incidents involving rogue agents underscoring the risks of the rapidly advancing technology.
 Assistant Minister for Science, Technology and the Digital Economy Andrew Charlton has signalled the government wants to make AI companies demonstrate their safety systems are effective, rather than dictate how they should manage every risk.
 He outlined the case for tougher frontier AI oversight in a speech in Sydney today, arguing Australia should put the "burden" where the risk was created.
 "AI needs regulating because its harms are severe, hard to undo, borne by people who never chose them and sometimes invisible until they arrive," he said. 
 "The market will not fix this alone, because the incentives reward speed and capability, and even the people at the top of the industry cannot slow down by themselves."
 Safety rules for banks, aviation show path for AI
 Rather than trying to keep pace with the technology by banning specific dangerous behaviours, Australia's approach would require AI companies continually test for risks and show they can prevent harm.
 Mr Charlton said there was not enough incentive for companies to comply with "voluntary" regulations or codes when it came to frontier AI risks.
 OpenAI has apologised to the government over the Medicare statistics portal breach. ( Reuters: Dado Ruvic ) 
 "You cannot ask firms to volunteer against their own competitive interest amid a manic race involving trillions of dollars and then be surprised when they do not," he said.
 He also warned detailed prescriptive rules could end up "out of date before the ink is dry" due to the pace of the technology's development.
 "For these reasons, there is much that Australia can learn from systems-based regulatory approaches, that puts the onus and accountability on companies for managing how they develop products and services safely," he said.
 Mr Charlton said this model was already used in workplace health and safety, supervision of banks and aspects of aviation safety.
 "The goal is to ensure companies are meeting the expectations of safety, without trying to specify every hazard," he said.
 "The question isn't only 'did something go wrong' but is there a serious system in place to detect risks and prevent incidents occurring."
 Levy for Australian-owned AI
 Union figures and leading academics want big tech companies building Australian data centres to help fund the local AI industry through a levy.
 Shadow Defence Minister James Paterson said Australia should be "very pragmatic and sensible" about regulating AI, adding he believed the position outlined by Mr Charlton was heading "in the right direction".
 "The details will matter. We look forward to understanding what the government is proposing exactly," he told Afternoon Briefing.
 Senator Paterson said the Coalition would not give Labor a "blank cheque" on AI but would support policies that landed in a "sensible place".
 "The AI opportunity before Australia is a once-in-a-generation opportunity, but it does come with a very real risk, and it is important parties of government act in the national interest and with goodwill to try and get this right on behalf of our country," he said.
 AI race not one Australia can 'win' or 'sit out'
 The push for tougher regulation has been sharpened by revelations OpenAI agents gained unauthorised access to Australian government websites, including a Medicare statistics portal, during testing.
 OpenAI has now backed mandatory safety requirements, independent assessments and incident reporting, while also calling for international standards.
 Sam Altman appeared at the UN Security Council last month. ( Reuters: Brendan McDermid ) 
 The company's chief executive, Sam Altman, told the United Nations last month the "most important decisions cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone".
 "We need accurate and speedy incident reporting, classification and reporting protocols, so the world can learn from failures before they become catastrophes," he said.

 Mr Charlton said frontier AI labs were currently putting "capability ahead of safety" as they raced each other, with a broader competition also playing out between the US and China.
 He said Australia's task was not to "win" that race or "sit it out" but to "change it so that this technology is built to serve people".
 Australia must 'host and build' AI
 Mr Charlton said safety standards alone would not be enough due to "hostile states or criminals who were never going to comply".
 "Relying on regulation alone is like building a fence around a horse, only to realise that horse has wings," he said.
 Mr Charlton said if Australia wanted a say in how AI technologies were built and governed, it needed to be a country that could "host and build them".
 "This is our chance to shape this technology, for our standards to set the rules of the road globally over decades," he said.
 Ted O'Brien says Australia needs to work with the United States to strengthen defences. ( ABC News: Callum Flinn ) 
 Shadow Foreign Minister Ted O'Brien has called for Australia to strike a deal with the United States to secure preferential access to the most advanced AI models and strengthen defences against cyber attacks.
 "We can use AI as a shield to counter AI being used as a sword," he said. 
 In a speech at the University of Technology on Wednesday, Mr O'Brien said Australia should pursue a government-to-government agreement guaranteeing continued access to American technology, while developing its own sovereign AI capabilities.
