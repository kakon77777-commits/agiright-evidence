AHM Statement on OpenAI’s October 6 Release of Mathematical Documents

7 October, 2026 in guest blog, opinion | Tags: AI, Association for Human Mathematics | by Terence Tao

[This is a guest post by the Association for Human Mathematics, reposted from their statements page. This blog post was initially written in a different file format and converted using AI. — T.]

Yesterday, on October 6th, 2026, OpenAI — which is currently defending lawsuits against accusations of illegal plagiarism, copyright infringement, and trademark dilution — released a repository of manuscripts purporting to contain solutions to a number of high-profile problems in mathematics.

Mathematicians did not ask for this work to be done. The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, from whom OpenAI has claimed to derive its legitimacy, opened their initial advisory statement by saying that frontier AI corporations should not test advanced mathematical problems on internal models. In ignoring the central premise of the Advisory Group’s position, OpenAI has indicated total disregard for the norms of scientific research — norms that guarantee that mathematics remains trustworthy, ethically researched, and in the public interest.

Mathematicians have a particular vision of progress that is informed by history and field-specific considerations. We reject OpenAI’s assertion that this release advances our subject, and we urge mathematicians and the public to view the value of this publication model with due skepticism.

Releasing over 700 files at once is not a demonstration of scholarship, but a demonstration of power. We urge mathematicians to discontinue their work with OpenAI and to return to a vision of science that centers human understanding.

Association for Human Mathematics Communications Working Group

Maths is one of the few fields where you can actually check the work, by hand or by computer. A proof is either right or it’s wrong. If one of these proofs holds up, does it become wrong because of a copyright case? Of course not. Leading with this is just attacking the messenger.

Secondly, since when do mathematicians get to decide who works on which problems? These were open problems. Some of them have literal prize money attached to get people to solve them. And nobody is forcing anyone to review these papers. They exist now. Read them, check them, ignore them, that’s entirely up to you.

Thirdly, “the norms of scientific research”. What does that actually mean here? The work was published openly, for free, with full write-ups and in a lot of cases machine-checked Lean proofs. That sounds pretty scientific to me. If the real issue is attribution, or speed, or who has to do the checking, then say that. Right now I genuinely can’t tell what rule was broken.

Fourthly, “a particular vision of progress”. Wasn’t the vision to get these problems solved? That’s the whole point of putting them out there and attaching prizes to them. So why is progress being published on a public GitHub suddenly bad for the field?

And “a demonstration of power”? If a single person had solved even one of these problems, we’d be calling it the scholarship of the century. The fact that it came from an AI company doesn’t change whether the maths is right.

To be fair, I think there probably is a real concern buried in here because someone has to check 700+ papers, and that work usually falls on mathematicians who aren’t being paid for it. That might be a fair point but it’s not the point this statement makes. Instead it just tells everyone what to think without explaining why.

Honestly, I think statements like this do more damage to how people see mathematics than anything an AI company has done. If mathematicians genuinely feel they’re being hard done by, explain it properly and bring people along. I’d absolutely listen. But this doesn’t make the case.

7 October, 2026 at 7:39 pm

Anonymous

First off, I’m not some great mathematician with some great ethos. But I’m a major fan of this blog, and I want to make one point

Never in human history have we considered a conjecture solved when no one, not even the author of the proof, understands how it was solved. But now, all these (great) conjectures are being solved at an alarming rate, yet our collective knowledge about these conjectures has not moved at all. 

Thus, a simple question: are these problems really solved? For example, take Navier-Stokes: We have virtually no idea what allowed the transition from the rough forcing in the work of Coroba/Martinez-Zorora (on IPM) to smooth forcing in the Navier–Stokes construction. (I think the jump from rough to smooth forcing in IPM is a major result in and of itself). As far as I can tell, our best understanding is that “Open”-AI’s construction requires the forcing to be smooth, and that this requirement likely presents an obstruction to extending the techniques to the unforced problem. Yet surely this would be a trivial, almost obvious observation to someone who had actually proved the Navier–Stokes result with smooth forcing. So I ask again: Do we really understand fluids any better than we did before?

The discourse on this blog, and among the general public, has largely been the same old song and dance: “AI has enormous potential to help mathematics, if only it were done right,” or “AI should be used to spin the wheel faster, not reinvent it.”

But why, in any world, would “Open”-AI care about any of this? Their objective is to make money, not to do right by mathematicians. Every time we repeat these same things over and over again about AI and mathematics, we reinforce that their models are becoming more powerful, generating more attention, more publicity, and ultimately more value for their (likely already historic) IPO. It is the best possible positive feedback loop for them, while creating precisely the opposite effect for the rest of us.

I think it is no secret that all of us want something to give. But change has never come from simply continuing to ride the same bus over and over again while complaining that it should be driven differently. You got to hop off at some point. 

So what if we simply stopped caring about “Open”-AI’s proposed solutions? What if we went about mathematics as usual, refusing to recognize these results as actual solutions unless they have a manuscript where their authors could explain every single little detail on every single page, just as mathematicians have been expected to do throughout history?

Continuing as we are, simply writing blog posts about how AI should be used and how much potential it has does little more than give these companies additional attention. It does nothing to address the underlying problem. And, most certain of all: it does not advance our collective understanding of mathematics.

Maybe the change we need is much simpler than we think. Perhaps we need to reconsider when a problem is actually solved. If a proof is generated entirely by an LLM whose prompters/authors cannot account for every single step of the argument, then we should simply refuse to recognize it as a solution. Not because we are opposed to AI, or because we are afraid of progress, but because no progress has actually been made.  

And perhaps, more importantly, we need to actually stick to the standard, just as mathematicians have throughout time.
