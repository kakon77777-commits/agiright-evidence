Introduction
One year ago, Governor Newsom signed SB 53 – the first law in the nation to govern frontier AI development. California led the nation in requiring frontier AI developers to establish and follow comprehensive safety plans, report critical safety incidents, and protect whistleblowers, inspiring other states to follow its approach.
Since then, AI capabilities and their associated harms and risks have soared. Even with the safeguards the state has put in place, leading AI researchers, safety advocates, civil society leaders, and even the companies themselves say that frontier AI development can cause catastrophic consequences. Yet the federal government has not acted. No federal law requires AI companies to follow baseline safety or security standards, undergo independent testing or verification, or shut down their models if they threaten the public.
We now know the OpenAI Hugging Face incident this past summer was one of dozens of instances in which AI agents operated outside of developer control. Our consultations with a range of AI experts underscored that the push within AI companies to automate model development and pursue recursive self-improvement may create risks at a pace that exceeds the capacity of government officials to respond.
The federal government’s approach of relying on voluntary company commitments rather than actual legislation is not enough to address significant safety and security concerns. Strengthening the state’s AI governance and guardrails has never been more urgent.
California is uniquely situated to fill this gap. It stands alone as the global leader in AI development, hosting two-thirds of the world’s top 50 AI companies. And in parallel, it has pioneered groundbreaking approaches to AI governance. SB 53

both introduced safety and security requirements and fostered greater transparency so policymakers and the public could better assess AI risks. California also enacted strong laws to protect children, consumers, and workers from AI harms. This fall, the state also passed SB 813 and AB 1405, creating a first-in-the-nation framework to build a robust ecosystem of independent verification organizations (IVOs) and auditing for AI safety.
These laws were critical first steps, putting the AI industry on notice that a wait-and-see approach was no longer tenable. When the Governor signed SB 53, he made it clear that as the evidence changed on AI risks and the state’s ability to respond, the state would need to clarify and strengthen its approach. Any fast-moving technology requires an agile, iterative approach to governance.
Since then, the evidence has changed dramatically. In the last year, the release of highly capable coding agents has revolutionized software development. New and unreleased models have proved capable of worrisome cyber capabilities, including the ability to exploit high-severity and zero-day vulnerabilities in critical operating systems and internet protocols.1 Loss-of-control incidents, once the stuff of AI theory, now occur with disturbing frequency.2

Recommendations for Legislation
In response to Executive Order N-9-26, the California Government Operations Agency, in consultation with the California Office of Emergency Services and outside AI experts, has developed recommendations for legislative action.
The executive order asked for recommendations in four areas: (1) standards, (2) independent verification organizations, (3) critical safety incident reporting, and (4) emergency shutdown requirements. We summarize our recommendations in each of the four areas below:

Establishing Baseline Standards
●​ The state should establish baseline standards for safety, security, and transparency that all large frontier AI developers must follow. Independent Verification Organizations (IVOs), meanwhile, should have the flexibility to determine and update technical specifications for meeting the standards as AI technology evolves. The state should adopt clear standards that apply throughout the AI model development cycle, with reference to national and international standard-setting bodies wherever appropriate. IVOs would translate these standards into the technical methods and tests used to verify compliance, updating them as the science advances.
●​ The state must have an immediate and ongoing line of sight into the possibility of recursive self-improvement (RSI) with the authority to cap its development and use if needed. Developers should have to report key metrics on their automation of AI R&D, including the share of compute they are devoting to this purpose. As the state stands up a system of IVOs, the very first responsibility of the IVOs should be to verify these metrics and assess the risks as they grow. If the state assesses that the automation of AI R&D above a certain level may result in catastrophic risk, the state should have authority to cap the development and use of RSI.
Requiring Oversight by Independent Verification Organizations
●​ Every large frontier developer must submit to ongoing oversight by licensed independent verification organizations. IVOs must have full access to the people and information they need to run tests and make informed judgments on the risks of a developer’s frontier AI models. They must assess risks against the standards adopted by the state on an ongoing basis and share those unredacted assessments with the state. The California Government Operations Agency must license IVOs under
6

the criteria set out in SB 813 and revoke the licenses of IVOs that are not operating with sufficient expertise, independence, and rigor.
●​ If an IVO determines that it cannot, using its approved methods and technologies, attest to the compliance of a developer’s models with government standards, the government must have the authority to order corrective action. IVOs should present assessments to the state, which, after engaging with the developer, should make a decision on whether to order corrective action. This is to ensure the developer is taking sufficient action to comply with baseline standards, reduce risk levels, and provide sufficient access to evaluators. Developers would be able to contest the state’s determination in court before it goes into effect. If a court finds in favor of the state, the court should be able to compel adherence to the state’s standards as a condition of further model development. Note that for imminent dangers, the state should follow an expedited shutdown procedure (more on this in the chapter on Managing Severe AI Crises).
●​ Stand up CalCompute with a surcharge on fees that model developers pay for IVOs. SB 53 set out a framework to create CalCompute. Since then, the need for publicly owned and operated compute has become more pressing as the demand for chips has risen, pricing out talented researchers who are working on vital safety and security issues.9 A surcharge on IVO fees from the industry can help launch a CalCompute cluster to serve the public interest. The state can reserve capacity for independent safety and security research that serves the public and enhances the capacity for public oversight.

Expanding Incident Reporting to Cover Serious Incidents
●​ The thresholds for critical incident reporting must expand to capture all serious incidents. Attempts to steal model weights should be reported even if death or bodily injury does not result, as should any situations of loss-of-control, whether from internal or external use of a model. The state should also establish a near-miss reporting channel and expand whistleblower protections to include former employees and contractors.
●​ Models that are capable of actions that could result in catastrophic risk must be treated as frontier models and required to report incidents. This means that such models are subject to reporting requirements even if they have been trained with fewer than 10^26 integer or floating-point operations. Relying solely on this numerical threshold has prevented the state from scoping in dangerous incidents, such as the OpenAI Hugging Face incident.
●​ Reduce the reporting timeline for critical safety incidents. Right now, the timeline for reporting most safety incidents is 15 days. The state should cut that timeline to 48 hours and retain the 24-hour requirement for imminent catastrophic risks, so both the state and the developer have timely notice of incidents and are ready to respond quickly if needed.
Managing Severe AI Crises
●​ Every large frontier developer must create a Risk Containment Protocol (RCP), tested and verified on an ongoing basis by an IVO, to show they are capable of shutting down models in case of emergency. The RCP must demonstrate not a single on-off switch but the ability to intervene on multiple levels (including compute, agents, internal access, and model deployment), identify the people responsible for intervening, coordinate
8

with outside actors such as cloud providers and power suppliers, and determine when it is safe to restore the model. A senior company official must certify that the RCP is effective. For open-weight developers, requirements should only apply to model deployments within their control.
●​ The RCP should specifically address potential failures of the shutdown procedure and how the developer is addressing them. As an example, the developer should specifically test for whether the model will attempt to block implementation of the shutdown, such as by copying its weights (itself a form of loss-of-control) as well as whether an outside actor could access the shutdown controls. The developer should identify how it will address failure modes, and the IVO should verify the steps are sufficient. After an actual shutdown, the California Office of Emergency Services (Cal OES) should draw up lessons to reduce future failures and update guidance on RCPs for developers and IVOs.
●​ If the danger from a model is imminent and the developer refuses to take action to contain the danger, the state should have authority to order an immediate emergency shutdown. State officials can issue such an order if they have credible reason to believe there is an imminent threat of a catastrophic risk or major loss-of-control event. The developer can challenge this decision and seek a temporary restraining order, but the government’s order should go into effect immediately and remain in effect pending judicial review. The state should order the narrowest workable intervention to address the danger and should remove the order when the danger is no longer imminent.
There are many more recommendations in the report chapters. We hope these suggestions will help inform and shape discussions as the legislature returns next month.

More critically, the legislature should provide the state with standby authority to cap automated AI R&D when the level of risk is unacceptable. For example, the state should be able to cap the share of compute a developer can devote to automation of AI R&D. As broader measurements of AI-led R&D become more mature, the state should also use those metrics. We do not recommend that the

state impose a cap immediately. But the state must possess this authority if it finds that a developer’s automated AI R&D has exceeded its committed thresholds or is advancing faster than its safeguards and evaluations can keep up, creating a material risk of catastrophic harm. The cap should be limited in time, reviewed periodically, and lifted when the issue has been addressed
