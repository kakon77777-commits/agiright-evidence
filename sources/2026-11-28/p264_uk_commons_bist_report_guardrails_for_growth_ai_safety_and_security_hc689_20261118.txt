Summary

Artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to deliver immense benefits
including higher productivity, scientific advances and economic growth.

Frontier AI differs from conventional tools because it can serve many
purposes, rather than solely perform a specific task. AI’s full potential is
still unclear and will evolve as the technology develops. The most capable
‘frontier’ AI models are advancing faster than our ability to manage
the risks, while ‘open-weight’ models create new concerns of different
uncontrolled harms.

To maximise the benefits of AI, the UK must diffuse AI throughout our
economy and society. But this will not happen if AI is not trusted. Recent
incidents have damaged that trust: AI agents have broken out of test
environments, accessed government systems, and bypassed safeguards.
Maximising trust and the opportunity that lies ahead will therefore depend
on the UK’s ability to manage risks of increasingly capable AI systems, and
we are not there yet.

The UK is a global leader in AI security research. The AI Security Institute
(AISI), founded in 2023, is an internationally recognised centre of expertise
in AI testing, research and evaluation. However, the UK does not have a
legislative framework for frontier AI safety and governance.

Hitherto, AISI’s invaluable work has been enabled by voluntary cooperation,
and its access to talent. Both must be protected and strengthened for
the future. AISI is not a regulator, and voluntary cooperation does not
guarantee access to new models before release, disclosure of incidents, or
action when public and company safety assessments differ.

Decisions about the risks society should accept, and the safeguards
required to manage them must ultimately rest with Government,
accountable to Parliament, informed by our world leading research,
innovation and academic institutions. Specifically, our report identifies ten
critical gaps in the UK’s framework for AI safety:

I. Missing definitions of acceptable harms and risk thresholds

II. Lack of statutory powers for model access

III. Lack of independent verification of safety claims

                                          1
IV. Insufficient controls on internal pre-release deployment, training and
      containment

V. Absence of safety-proof evidence before release

VI. Absence of mandatory incident reporting

VII. Lack of powers to restrict or halt deployment

VIII. Insufficient safeguards for irreversible release

IX. Inadequate liability and redress mechanisms

X. Insufficient protection for whistleblowers

Our report sets out clear recommendations to address each of these gaps.
We note, however, that no single approach is sufficient to address the risks
which are now multiplying; in fact, five distinct but interlocking approaches
may be necessary:

• targeted legislative and regulatory interventions to address known
      risks;

• a statutory duty requiring AI developers to prevent harm;

• a digital rights framework providing protections and remedies;

• criminalising misuse of models for harm; and

• international cooperation and a convention on AI safety.

Each approach has limitations if pursued in isolation. So, the Government
should set out a clear strategy explaining how existing, proposed and new
legislation will work together to manage AI risks, optimise opportunity,
maximise public trust and so accelerate the diffusion of AI through the
economy and society. Given the speed of change, Governments, parliaments
and the public will need to rethink traditional assumptions and approaches
to safety and risk management. Crucially, regulation will need to become
more flexible to keep pace with technological change.

This Report is the first in a series from the Committee’s long-term inquiry
into AI adoption, safety and governance. It deals primarily with domestic
measures that the Government should take in the short- and medium-term.
Alongside this report, the Committee is publishing a call for evidence on the
UK’s role in securing a global convention on AI safety and security in 2027,
as part of its G20 Presidency.

  1 The prize and the peril

   The prize

1. AI has the potential to deliver significant benefits for the UK.1 OECD
      estimates suggest that AI could increase UK labour productivity growth
      by 0.4–1.3 percentage points annually over the next decade,2 equivalent to
      up to £38 billion in additional economic output each year and an economy
      up to £400 billion larger after ten years.3 Because the UK is home to world
      leading AI testing, evaluation, research, development and innovation–and
      a fast-growing start-up ecosystem–it has the potential to become a global
      leader in exporting AI assurance, which could become a valuable source of
      economic growth and maximise influence over future AI development.4

   The peril

2. The benefits of AI will only be optimised if it is trusted by individuals,
      businesses and public institutions. Today, however, frontier AI is advancing
      faster than our ability to manage its risks.5

3. Sir Ken McCallum, MI5’s Director General, has described rogue actors’
      interest in AI for reconnaissance and attack methods and biological
      weapons.6 AI-powered discovery of new vulnerabilities in systems can only
      protect key services when it is possible to deploy fixes and identify affected
      systems. The majority of the most advanced closed and open-source and
      -weight models are developed by firms headquartered in the United States
      and China, outwith the UK’s jurisdictional reach.

4. In particular, Chinese developers are producing increasingly open-weight
      models,7 which are easy to adopt8 and difficult to control once released.9
      Their low cost and ease of access will likely accelerate global adoption10 and
      so AI systems developed outside the UK could become widely embedded
      in the economy. As AI capabilities continue to improve, the risks posed by
      misuse, loss of control, cyber-attacks and other harms may outpace our
      ability to understand, monitor and mitigate risk.11

5. To continue to identify and understand risks, the UK will need to
      retain, resource and constantly refresh capabilities at the frontier of
      AI development. In particular, AI safety cannot be managed unless the
      Government is able to assemble the right skills to do the job. Today,
      the Government and AISI are competing in a high-stakes global talent
      market against private sector employers who offer significantly greater
      remuneration than the public sector. Government will need to continue
      making the argument that retaining and growing talent is critical to our
      national interests. Addressing the capability gap in public services will
      require new flexibility in the standard Government pay scales, especially for
      technical staff.

6. conclusion
        The Government must address growing safety and security risks without
        undermining its ability to access frontier systems, lead international
        standards or benefit from international advances in AI. It must also
        recognise the limits of domestic regulation in a global ecosystem.

7. conclusion
        Notwithstanding these constraints, the Government must bring forward
        a comprehensive framework to address the ten critical AI safety
        and governance gaps we identify in chapter three. These gaps leave
        significant weaknesses in the UK’s safety and security regime and must
        be addressed, and iterated, in consultation with stakeholders and the
        public, to build trust in AI and reap the potential economic rewards.

8. recommendation
        The Government must set out a legislative roadmap for AI safety. This
        should include an AI Safety and Security Bill to enshrine aspects of the
        new protections we set out in this report. Inevitably, this Bill will need to
        dovetail with, and enshrine key elements of, an international convention
        on AI safety which should be the centrepiece of the UK’s G20 Presidency.
        Some elements of the legislative strategy, such as the liability regime,
        must wait for the Law Commission’s findings. A bill in the next session
        should be merely the first step on that pathway, and the Government
        should set out the sequence it envisages in response to this report. Given
        the speed with which this technology is evolving, we accept it may prove
        essential that ministers are granted order-making powers, which allow
        them to act to safeguard the public quickly. The transformational nature
        of the AI revolution will demand that both Government and Parliament
        are creative when developing new legislative approaches. Parliament
        should anticipate the creation of new executive powers by designing
        upfront a strong transparency and accountability framework for
        granting and holding to account the exercise of these powers.

9. recommendation
        In response to this report, the Government must set out how it will
        address the disparity in pay between the public and private sector, to
        recruit and retain people with the technical skills that the public interest
        requires.

  2 Five approaches to

  regulating digital risks

10. Evidence suggests today’s AI safety system is not fit for purpose. Witnesses
      agreed that political leaders should determine acceptable risk levels12 but
      while successive governments have considered AI regulation,13 it remains
      piecemeal.14 The Cabinet Office Permanent Secretary also questioned the
      suitability of the UK’s contextual approach15 to AI regulation.16

11. While the Government has committed to AI safety regulation17 and has
      amended the draft Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information
      Systems) Bill to improve emergency response provisions, a coherent
      legislative framework has not been forthcoming. To address this, we
      propose that five distinct, but interlocking, approaches will be needed:

      • Targeted legislative and regulatory interventions to address known
             problems.

      • A statutory duty that requires AI developers to prevent harm.

      • A rights framework that provides protection and remedies to AI harms.

      • Criminalising misuse of models for harm.

      • An international convention on coordinated action on AI safety.

12. Each approach has strengths and weaknesses. Targeted action can be
      overtaken by technological advances. A duty to prevent harm puts the
      responsibility on private actors who may decide that penalties for allowing
      harm to occur are a “cost of doing business”. Individual claims for the
      violation of digital rights cannot contain a rapidly developing systemic
      incident. Criminalising the misuse of models for harm will require debate

13. conclusion

        Democratically elected policymakers should determine AI development
        and deployment rules. The absence of a clear regulatory framework
        leaves UK citizens exposed to AI risks, while creating uncertainty for
        developers and businesses. We find that targeted legislative action, a
        duty to prevent harm, “digital rights,” criminalising the misuse of models
        for harm, and an international convention may all be needed to manage
        AI risks and shape opportunities for the future.

14. recommendation
        The Government should set out a coherent legislative strategy for AI
        safety. This should detail how the different, complementary strengths
        of the five approaches we outline will be used to develop a coherent
        regulatory framework. The response to this report should explain how
        existing legislation, previous legislative commitments, legislation in
        progress and potential future legislation will tackle the ten risks we
        identify, alongside efforts to build an international framework. Any
        proposal must include dedicated new AI legislation to address the
        challenges we have identified as outside the scope of current or planned
        provisions.

  3 The gaps

   I. Define the harms and acceptable risks

15. Frontier AI systems are general-purpose technologies. This means that risks
      are diverse, difficult to predict, and likely to change as models become
      more capable and widely adopted. Increasingly capable systems pose
      risks including enablement of cyber-attacks, development of chemical
      and biological weapons, introduction of uncontrolled AI recursive self-
      improvement (RSI),18 the possibility of systems acting beyond human control
      and opaque chains of reasoning. AI safety researchers argue that these
      risks are likely to increase as capabilities advance.19 We heard that leading
      firms are developing increasingly capable systems, despite significant
      uncertainty over safety, and competitive pressures are likely to accelerate
      these risks.20

16. The UK has not defined thresholds for unacceptable AI risks and harms.21
      Firms determine and judge the efficacy of safeguards themselves.22
      Frontier companies assured the Committee they would not deploy unsafe
      models.23 But each uses different thresholds for defining harm which evolve
      as capabilities advance.24 Several firms make safeguards contingent
      on competitors’ behaviour,25 which could lead to the normalisation and
      ratcheting of hazardous practices.26

18 Recursive self-improvement (RSI) is a process in which AI contributes to its own
       advancement, automating parts of AI research and development. While fully autonomous
       RSI has not been demonstrated, some researchers argue that, if achieved, it could
       accelerate AI development beyond the pace of effective human oversight, making it more
       difficult to evaluate, govern and control more advanced systems.

17. Witnesses highlighted the difficulty of determining where limits should
      be drawn, and the need for policymakers to specify acceptable harm
      thresholds and redress measures.27 Anthropic argued that government
      should set the rules, while Google DeepMind emphasised the need to
      develop better measuring capability.28 Daniel Kokotajlo, an AI safety
      researcher, suggested activities associated with achieving recursive self-
      improvement (RSI) could provide a basis for identifying particularly high-risk
      systems and triggering enhanced safeguards.29

18. Frontier AI presents a new safety challenge because its many possible uses
      may not be clear even when models are released. As risks and opportunities
      emerge, risk assessments and regulation will need to evolve. Nevertheless,
      Governments must define tolerable risk by assessing both the likelihood
      of harm occurring and the severity of its consequences. This has been an
      approach to legislation which has existed in the UK since the Health and
      Safety at Work Act 1974 and was recently used in the Online Safety Act 2023.

19. A model’s capabilities do not, by themselves, determine whether its use
      is an unacceptable risk. Assessment must distinguish what a system can
      do, what risk remains after safeguards are applied, and when public
      intervention is required. This requires scrutiny of the effectiveness of
      safeguards, whether the system reliably follows intended instructions,
      whether its actions can be monitored and controlled, and the circumstances
      in which it is used. Crossing a capability threshold should trigger a specified
      assessment and response, which may include stronger safeguards,
      restrictions or a pause where risks cannot be adequately controlled. Real-
      time monitoring of the most powerful models should inform regular updates
      to safeguards, helping regulation keep pace with the technology.

20. conclusion
        Decisions about the risks society should accept are matters for
        Government, accountable to Parliament, and informed by experts
        and public consultation. AISI should provide technical evidence to
        inform those decisions but should not be responsible for determining
        acceptable risk. A domestic framework must identify who sets risk
        criteria, who updates these criteria as capabilities and safeguards
        change, and who acts when those criteria are breached. It must also
        allow sufficient flexibility to respond to emerging risks. AI firms must
        be held accountable for operating within these thresholds, and need
        clear requirements and consequences from the regulator for breaching
        them. An international convention should seek to agree this approach
        internationally.

21. recommendation
        The Government should as a first step introduce an AI Safety and Security
        Bill creating clear legal duties on AI firms to identify and prevent harms
        from specified high-risk development activities conducted in the UK and
        specified frontier AI models distributed in the UK. The framework should
        not become an unnecessary drag on innovation but should nevertheless
        assign responsibility for setting and regularly updating risk criteria,
        informed by AISI’s technical assessments and subject to parliamentary
        scrutiny. It should distinguish reasonable capability thresholds that
        trigger additional assessment from findings of unacceptable residual risk
        that require restrictions or intervention. It should specify the safeguards,
        reporting obligations and regulatory action required at each stage, with
        a clear jurisdictional basis and proportionate obligations on overseas
        providers.

22. recommendation
        The Government must set out clear ministerial, and cross Government,
        accountability for specifying, agreeing and amending harm thresholds,
        in a way that is transparent and accountable to Parliament. Firms, the
        public and Parliament are entitled to know who decides what level of risk
        is tolerable, on what evidence, and with what right of challenge.

23. recommendation
        To advance the safety of frontier models developed abroad and beyond
        UK statutory control, the Government should seek to enshrine this
        approach in an international convention on AI safety.

   II. Lack of statutory powers for model
   access

24. AISI plays an important role in understanding and mitigating frontier AI
      risks. Its access to frontier AI systems, however, depends on voluntary
      cooperation. Firms expressed support for collaboration with AISI, but it
      has no guaranteed statutory right of access to models, systems, training
      outputs, safety frameworks or other outputs. Arrangements can be
      jeopardised by decisions taken by overseas governments. For example, early
      in 2025, AISI’s pre-deployment testing was interrupted,30 and while it is now
      resumed, the incident confirms the challenge of current arrangements.

25. Witnesses drew a distinction between evaluation and regulation. AISI’s
      current role is to test models rather than approve or reject deployment
      decisions.31 Google DeepMind called AISI a “world-leading source of
      technical expertise” and argued its strength is that it is not a regulator,
      which facilitates information-sharing.32 It cautioned that converting the
      Institute into a regulator could alter that relationship and openness.33
      Meta stated AISI should be placed on a statutory footing and resourced to
      evaluate advanced models.34

26. The lack of agreement among major tech companies reflects their
      competing interests and the limits of industry-led action. By contrast, AISI’s
      collaborative, non-regulatory model has enabled invaluable information-
      sharing with leading firms and helped those frontier firms deploy models
      which are safer and stronger. Voluntary cooperation alone, however, does
      not guarantee access to successive generations of frontier AI systems,
      which is needed.

27. recommendation
        The UK should establish enforceable access requirements within its
        jurisdiction, but recognise domestic legislation will not secure access
        to models developed overseas. A durable international framework must
        be introduced alongside our proposed AI Safety and Security Bill, to
        combine cooperation by overseas developers and governments while
        preserving the research relationships on which effective evaluation
        depends. A core objective for the UK’s G20 presidency, therefore, should
        be an international agreement that helps enshrine model access for a
        trusted network of AI safety institutes and an international consensus
        around shared approaches to AI assurance and risk management. The
        international community has developed similar arrangements in, for
        example, the inspection regime for nuclear energy. We now need similar
        durable arrangements for AI safety.

28. recommendation
        The Government should continue to resource AISI to enable it to continue
        to grow its capabilities for frontier analysis. The Government should
        establish a statutory AI regulator, separate from AISI but drawing on its
        expertise, with secure multi-year funding. Before legislating, it should

        re-evaluation.

   III. Independent verification of safety
   claims

29. Aengus Lynch, an AI safety researcher, argued that frontier risks are
      difficult to define and measure, particularly where harms extend beyond
      a company’s direct operations.35 We heard that independent, third-party,
      evaluators could identify emerging risk scenarios, assess mitigations, verify
      monitoring systems and provide oversight independent of commercial
      incentives.36 Geoffrey Irving, an AI safety researcher, similarly argued that
      researchers do not yet possess reliable methods for fully understanding
      how advanced AI systems behave during pre-deployment testing,37 so
      independent scrutiny and ongoing monitoring are required.38

30. Independent evaluation requires meaningful access to models, systems,
      testing, documentation and organisational processes.39 Crucially,
      this includes access to and monitoring of a model’s chain of thought.
      Independent evaluators need this access to assess developers’ risk-
      management claims and understand how systems are trained, tested,
      deployed and governed.40 Frontier firms were broadly supportive of greater
      external involvement in safety assessment,41 but no agreed framework
      governing the appointment, independence, reporting or oversight of
      independent evaluators yet exists.

31. conclusion
        Independent evaluation of frontier AI models is likely to bring significant
        long-term safety benefits. Any regime for evaluating safety claims
        will need to provide evaluators with sufficient access and authority to
        scrutinise both frontier AI systems and the organisational processes
        through which they are developed, tested, deployed and monitored,
        throughout their lifecycle.

32. recommendation
        The Government should require developers of designated high-risk
        systems to substantiate their safety claims and provide independent
        evaluators with the access necessary to scrutinise them. The framework
        should establish requirements for evaluator competence, independence,
        conflicts of interest, reporting and continuing oversight. An international
        convention should seek to establish a similar regime for model
        developers beyond the UK’s jurisdiction. This framework should support
        appropriate visibility into model reasoning and chains of thought.

   IV. Controls on internal deployment,
   training, and enforceable containment

33. Risks are not solely limited to externally deployed models. Risks emerge
      during model training and are arising in internally deployed models,
      particularly as developers seek AI recursive self-improvement (RSI).42 We
      heard evidence that internally deployed systems may pose risks beyond
      the creators’ environment, when systems interact with external networks
      or cause wider disruption.43 Incidents of concern have occurred during
      training, testing and evaluation where AI systems operated outside
      constraints;44 testing environments have been misconfigured45 and models
      have demonstrated potentially dangerous capabilities during testing that
      have required additional mitigations to be implemented.46 OpenAI’s Hugging
      Face Incident, for example, exposed weaknesses in existing containment

34. conclusion
        Containment failures and other unexpected behaviours can occur
        during training, testing, evaluation and internal deployment, before any
        public release decision is taken. Controls focused solely on externally
        deployed systems leave a major gap. Future governance arrangements
        should provide appropriate visibility and oversight of high-risk internal
        deployment and evaluation activities.

35. recommendation
        Through the AI Safety and Security Bill, the Government should legislate
        to ensure that its domestic regulatory framework covers high-risk
        training, testing, evaluation and internal deployment activities, as well
        as externally deployed systems. Working with the AI Security Institute,
        regulators and industry, it should develop guidance and, where
        appropriate, minimum standards for the containment, monitoring and
        oversight of high-risk frontier AI systems. Any new AI regulator should
        be endowed with powers to obtain information about containment
        arrangements, testing activities, incidents and mitigations associated
        with deployment and evaluation. To guard against such risks in models
        developed abroad, the UK should seek to enshrine this approach in an
        international convention.

   V. Pre-release safety evidence

   VI. Mandatory incident reporting

40. There is no standardised framework to govern reporting of frontier AI
      incidents. AI firms are not subject to any general statutory requirement to
      notify government of identified incidents, so government may lack visibility
      of incidents and patterns in a model’s behaviour, relevant to risk. We heard
      such reporting should extend beyond serious incidents to include near
      misses and precursor events, enabling emerging risks to be identified before
      significant harm occurs.55

41. Frontier firms told us that internal reviews of testing environments identified
      incidents which affected third parties.56 The fact that additional incidents
      were only identified retrospectively raises questions about whether existing
      monitoring and reporting arrangements are adequate.

42. Firms were unclear about what incidents should be reported, when
      notification should occur and which authority should receive reports.
      OpenAI acknowledged it should have notified the Australian Government
      sooner following an incident where its AI agents accessed government-
      related systems, obtaining internal files, credentials and data, and argued
      that a mandatory framework could establish common thresholds, severity
      classifications and notification timelines.57 The incident also demonstrated
      the consequences of unclear reporting routes; in this incident OpenAI
      notified the Government through a generic Government inbox.58

43. There was support from firms for Government-led standardisation and
      mandatory reporting. OpenAI argued that standardising would allow
      regulators to compare incidents across companies, identify trends and
      improve understanding of risks.59 OpenAI and Anthropic supported placing
      incident reporting on a statutory footing, while Google DeepMind and Meta
      supported standardised frameworks with clear routes and authorities.60
      Firms emphasised the need for mechanisms to handle sensitive
      information.61

   IX. Liability and redress

54. Due to the general-purpose nature of frontier AI systems, their capabilities
      can inevitably enable harm.74 This creates uncertainty about where
      responsibility should lie when harm occurs. Frontier systems are deployed
      through complex supply chains which make it difficult to determine the
      respective responsibilities of developers, deployers, infrastructure providers
      and users. Clarifying responsibility across the AI supply chain is important in
      determining who bears liability where those safeguards fail.

55. The Law Commission is consulting on how product liability law and
      compensation should apply to software and AI systems.75 Questions remain
      about responsibility across the supply chain, particularly where systems
      undertake unintended actions and multiple actors contribute to a system’s
      operation. Liability frameworks primarily operate after harm has occurred
      so, while liability reform may improve incentives and compensation, it
      cannot ensure rapid containment of emerging risks or recovery beyond a
      defendant’s resources.

56. conclusion
        A safety regime is incomplete if responsibility remains unclear when
        safeguards fail or harm occurs, and those harmed do not have clear
        and effective routes to redress. Clarifying liability arrangements,
        and ensuring AI firms cooperate with them, will require international
        collaboration, common standards and effective enforcement.

57. recommendation
        The Law Commission will report on its product liability consultation in
        due course. Following the Law Commission’s report, the Government
        should work with international partners to clarify the responsibilities
        of developers, deployers, infrastructure providers and users where AI
        systems cause harm or safeguards fail. In addition, the Government
        should identify outstanding matters and gaps outside the scope of the
        Law Commission’s consultation and set out how it intends to address
        those gaps. Ministers should coordinate with devolved administrations
        and set a timetable for responding to the Law Commission’s
        recommendations.

   X. Protection for whistleblowers

58. Former AI employees raised concerns about organisational safety culture,
      governance and risk management. They described losing confidence in
      their employers’ approaches to the development of advanced systems and
      argued that commercial and competitive pressures could influence how
      risks are communicated.76 They feel more able to speak freely about safety
      after leaving their organisations.77 They described contractual restrictions,
      workplace pressures and financial disincentives that could discourage
      employees from raising serious safety concerns while employed.78

59. The evidence raises questions about whether workers who identify safety
      risks have practical routes to report them without risking professional or
      financial consequences.79 Whistleblowing protections are linked to specified
      categories of wrongdoing and prescribed reporting routes, such as the
      Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998.80 Employees who raise concerns about
      AI risks may not have a clear route for confidential reporting to a specialist
      authority.

60. conclusion
        Employees working on frontier AI systems may be among the first
        to identify serious safety concerns, but concerns are often raised
        publicly only after individuals have left their organisations. Contractual
        restrictions, financial disincentives and uncertainty about appropriate
        reporting routes discourage employees from raising concerns. Effective
        AI governance therefore requires clear and protected channels for
        reporting serious prospective AI risks.

61. recommendation
        The Government should establish protected confidential reporting to the
        AI regulator. This means reviewing whistleblowing categories, prescribed
        recipients, worker coverage and remedies for serious prospective
        AI risks. Lawful reporting must be protected notwithstanding
        confidentiality clauses.

  approach

62. The Online Safety Act 2023 provides an important precedent to inform
      legislation that reduces digital harms. The Act, for example, requires in-
      scope service providers to prevent children from accessing certain harmful
      content and to minimise the risk of vulnerable age groups from encountering
      other harmful content.81

63. The Act was presaged by the Carnegie UK Trust’s Internet Harm Reduction
      proposal. This drew on well-established health and safety legislation and
      the “precautionary principle” that has informed policy frameworks in other
      novel areas.82 One specific precursor considered in the Carnegie paper was
      the Health and Safety at Work Act 1974, which deals with the employer-
      employee relationship but which also considers wider harms:

             It shall be the duty of every employer to conduct his undertaking in
             such a way as to ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, that
             persons not in his employment who may be affected thereby are not
             thereby exposed to risks to their health or safety.83

64. Carnegie noted that the concept of a person who “may be affected”
      presents “a very broad category which, were it to be applied analogously
      to the online context, could fill the gap in protection”. They acknowledge
      that the 1974 Act “provides a lesser protection to third parties than it does
      to employees” but argue that the concept of wider harm “serves as a broad
      model”.84 The Carnegie proposal also notes the challenges in defining
      “harm” for the purpose of a statutory duty. They note that criminal law,
      while useful, does not provide a “complete list of harms against which
      we would expect a service provider to take action”.85 They proposed that
      Parliament set out “primary or heads of harms as a non-exclusive list to give
      the regulator initial direction”.86

      systems can mislead their overseers or induce users to bypass safeguards.

  Conclusions and
  recommendations

   The prize and the peril

1. The Government must address growing safety and security risks without
      undermining its ability to access frontier systems, lead international
      standards or benefit from international advances in AI. It must also
      recognise the limits of domestic regulation in a global ecosystem.
      (Conclusion, Paragraph 6)

2. Notwithstanding these constraints, the Government must bring forward
      a comprehensive framework to address the ten critical AI safety and
      governance gaps we identify in chapter three. These gaps leave significant
      weaknesses in the UK’s safety and security regime and must be addressed,
      and iterated, in consultation with stakeholders and the public, to build trust
      in AI and reap the potential economic rewards. (Conclusion, Paragraph 7)

3. The Government must set out a legislative roadmap for AI safety. This
      should include an AI Safety and Security Bill to enshrine aspects of the new
      protections we set out in this report. Inevitably, this Bill will need to dovetail
      with, and enshrine key elements of, an international convention on AI safety
      which should be the centrepiece of the UK’s G20 Presidency. Some elements
      of the legislative strategy, such as the liability regime, must wait for the
      Law Commission’s findings. A bill in the next session should be merely the
      first step on that pathway, and the Government should set out the sequence
      it envisages in response to this report. Given the speed with which this
      technology is evolving, we accept it may prove essential that ministers are
      granted order-making powers, which allow them to act to safeguard the
      public quickly. The transformational nature of the AI revolution will demand
      that both Government and Parliament are creative when developing new
      legislative approaches. Parliament should anticipate the creation of
      new executive powers by designing upfront a strong transparency and
      accountability framework for granting and holding to account the exercise
      of these powers. (Recommendation, Paragraph 8)

                                                28
4. In response to this report, the Government must set out how it will address
      the disparity in pay between the public and private sector, to recruit and
      retain people with the technical skills that the public interest requires.
      (Recommendation, Paragraph 9)

   Five approaches to regulating digital risks

5. Democratically elected policymakers should determine AI development
      and deployment rules. The absence of a clear regulatory framework leaves
      UK citizens exposed to AI risks, while creating uncertainty for developers
      and businesses. We find that targeted legislative action, a duty to prevent
      harm, “digital rights,” criminalising the misuse of models for harm, and an
      international convention may all be needed to manage AI risks and shape
      opportunities for the future. (Conclusion, Paragraph 13)

6. The Government should set out a coherent legislative strategy for AI
      safety. This should detail how the different, complementary strengths
      of the five approaches we outline will be used to develop a coherent
      regulatory framework. The response to this report should explain how
      existing legislation, previous legislative commitments, legislation in
      progress and potential future legislation will tackle the ten risks we identify,
      alongside efforts to build an international framework. Any proposal
      must include dedicated new AI legislation to address the challenges we
      have identified as outside the scope of current or planned provisions.
      (Recommendation, Paragraph 14)

   The gaps

7. Decisions about the risks society should accept are matters for
      Government, accountable to Parliament, and informed by experts and
      public consultation. AISI should provide technical evidence to inform those
      decisions but should not be responsible for determining acceptable risk. A
      domestic framework must identify who sets risk criteria, who updates these
      criteria as capabilities and safeguards change, and who acts when those
      criteria are breached. It must also allow sufficient flexibility to respond
      to emerging risks. AI firms must be held accountable for operating within
      these thresholds, and need clear requirements and consequences from the
      regulator for breaching them. An international convention should seek to
      agree this approach internationally. (Conclusion, Paragraph 20)

8. The Government should as a first step introduce an AI Safety and Security
      Bill creating clear legal duties on AI firms to identify and prevent harms
      from specified high-risk development activities conducted in the UK and
      specified frontier AI models distributed in the UK. The framework should not

                                                29
      become an unnecessary drag on innovation but should nevertheless assign
      responsibility for setting and regularly updating risk criteria, informed
      by AISI’s technical assessments and subject to parliamentary scrutiny. It
      should distinguish reasonable capability thresholds that trigger additional
      assessment from findings of unacceptable residual risk that require
      restrictions or intervention. It should specify the safeguards, reporting
      obligations and regulatory action required at each stage, with a clear
      jurisdictional basis and proportionate obligations on overseas providers.
      (Recommendation, Paragraph 21)

9. The Government must set out clear ministerial, and cross Government,
      accountability for specifying, agreeing and amending harm thresholds,
      in a way that is transparent and accountable to Parliament. Firms, the
      public and Parliament are entitled to know who decides what level of
      risk is tolerable, on what evidence, and with what right of challenge.
      (Recommendation, Paragraph 22)

10. To advance the safety of frontier models developed abroad and beyond UK
      statutory control, the Government should seek to enshrine this approach in
      an international convention on AI safety. (Recommendation, Paragraph 23)

11. The UK should establish enforceable access requirements within its
      jurisdiction, but recognise domestic legislation will not secure access to
      models developed overseas. A durable international framework must be
      introduced alongside our proposed AI Safety and Security Bill, to combine
      cooperation by overseas developers and governments while preserving
      the research relationships on which effective evaluation depends. A core
      objective for the UK’s G20 presidency, therefore, should be an international
      agreement that helps enshrine model access for a trusted network of AI
      safety institutes and an international consensus around shared approaches
      to AI assurance and risk management. The international community has
      developed similar arrangements in, for example, the inspection regime for
      nuclear energy. We now need similar durable arrangements for AI safety.
      (Recommendation, Paragraph 27)

12. The Government should continue to resource AISI to enable it to continue
      to grow its capabilities for frontier analysis. The Government should
      establish a statutory AI regulator, separate from AISI but drawing on its
      expertise, with secure multi-year funding. Before legislating, it should set
      out the regulator’s remit, its relationship with AISI, how its powers would
      be enforced and their likely consequences, including unintended effects.
      These powers should include access to models when defined thresholds
      are met or under agreed international conventions; access to relevant
      systems, logs, training outputs and safeguards; sanctions for unreasonable
      delays or refusal of access; and ongoing monitoring and re-evaluation.
      (Recommendation, Paragraph 28)

13. Independent evaluation of frontier AI models is likely to bring significant
      long-term safety benefits. Any regime for evaluating safety claims will need
      to provide evaluators with sufficient access and authority to scrutinise both
      frontier AI systems and the organisational processes through which they
      are developed, tested, deployed and monitored, throughout their lifecycle.
      (Conclusion, Paragraph 31)

14. The Government should require developers of designated high-risk systems
      to substantiate their safety claims and provide independent evaluators with
      the access necessary to scrutinise them. The framework should establish
      requirements for evaluator competence, independence, conflicts of interest,
      reporting and continuing oversight. An international convention should
      seek to establish a similar regime for model developers beyond the UK’s
      jurisdiction. This framework should support appropriate visibility into model
      reasoning and chains of thought. (Recommendation, Paragraph 32)

15. Containment failures and other unexpected behaviours can occur during
      training, testing, evaluation and internal deployment, before any public
      release decision is taken. Controls focused solely on externally deployed
      systems leave a major gap. Future governance arrangements should
      provide appropriate visibility and oversight of high-risk internal deployment
      and evaluation activities. (Conclusion, Paragraph 34)

16. Through the AI Safety and Security Bill, the Government should legislate to
      ensure that its domestic regulatory framework covers high-risk training,
      testing, evaluation and internal deployment activities, as well as externally
      deployed systems. Working with the AI Security Institute, regulators and
      industry, it should develop guidance and, where appropriate, minimum
      standards for the containment, monitoring and oversight of high-risk
      frontier AI systems. Any new AI regulator should be endowed with
      powers to obtain information about containment arrangements, testing
      activities, incidents and mitigations associated with deployment and
      evaluation. To guard against such risks in models developed abroad, the
      UK should seek to enshrine this approach in an international convention.
      (Recommendation, Paragraph 35)

17. AI firms should not be exclusively responsible for determining whether
      their frontier systems are safe to deploy. The Government must therefore
      create a risk and regulation framework that both optimises opportunity
      and manages risk. While firms should have to evidence their safety claims,
      designated high-risk systems should require independent safety verification,
      informed by evaluation throughout development, testing and internal
      use. Evaluators must have access to test developers’ claims and the
      ability to disclose where access has been restricted or their findings differ.
      (Conclusion, Paragraph 38)

                                                31
18. The Government should explore how an independently scrutinised safety
      assessment could assess designated high-risk systems before they are
      deployed in the UK. This framework should include an assessment of
      the system’s capabilities, the effectiveness of safeguards and the risks
      remaining in its intended circumstances of use. It should demonstrate
      compliance with applicable risk criteria, record material disagreements
      between developers and evaluators, and specify conditions for deployment.
      Material changes in capability, safeguards, controllability or use should
      trigger reassessment, with restrictions or suspension where risks cannot be
      adequately controlled. (Recommendation, Paragraph 39)

19. The Government cannot assess frontier AI risk without systematic
      information about failures and near misses. Fragmented voluntary
      arrangements prevent collective learning and may leave individual
      companies to decide unilaterally whether an event is sufficiently serious to
      disclose. (Conclusion, Paragraph 44)

20. Using the AI Safety and Security Bill, the Government must introduce
      mandatory reporting of frontier AI incidents of material concern to the UK. It
      should seek to internationalise these arrangements through an international
      convention. This approach should be supported by a clear and standardised
      reporting framework. This framework could include:

      • clearly defined reportable incidents and near misses;

      • standardised severity classifications;

      • clearly defined notification timelines;

      • clear requirements on who must report incidents and to which
             authority;

      • requirements to report the extent of visibility into model behaviour and
             reasoning processes;

      • legal protections for confidential and security-sensitive reporting; and

      • requirements for corrective action, follow-up reporting and resolution
             updates. (Recommendation, Paragraph 45)

21. No UK regulator or public authority currently has the power to impose
      safeguards on frontier AI systems, pause training, delay deployment
      or withdraw unsafe services. Decisions about whether systems are
      safe to develop or deploy remain with the companies building them.
      (Conclusion, Paragraph 48)

                                                32
22. Where risk cannot be adequately controlled, the Government should
      ensure that the AI safety regulator defines and seeks powers to require
      safeguards for AI products serving UK users. In defined circumstances
      where the Government has significant concerns about an AI model,
      it should have the power to delay, restrict or prohibit its deployment,
      training or testing in the UK. The statutory regulator should not be AISI.
      (Recommendation, Paragraph 49)

23. Safety frameworks cannot assume that all material risks will be identified
      before deployment. Once model weights have been widely copied and
      distributed, recalling every copy or preventing all further use may be
      technically impossible. However, it is important that Government has the
      capability to target action at particular hosts, repositories, services or
      unlawful uses, and to criminalise the harmful misuse of models, or acts that
      enable such misuse. In addition, to control misuse of irretrievable models,
      the Government may have to develop a way to criminalise the misuse of
      models for harm. (Conclusion, Paragraph 52)

24. Given the difficulty of reversing distribution, where it has jurisdiction, the
      Government should require independent scrutiny before high-risk open-
      weight models are released. AI firms and relevant hosts and repositories
      should be required to maintain and exercise proportionate emergency plans
      with regulators, operational agencies and international partners. These
      plans should distinguish between systems remaining under an identifiable
      operator’s control (where access can be restricted or services suspended),
      and distributed models that cannot be recalled. The Government must
      have in place capability to detect and remove harmful models from
      services where it can, to disrupt unlawful use, and ensure the right range of
      criminal offences are available to deter the financing of, advertisement of,
      distribution of, or misuse of AI models. This Committee will explore the best
      means to develop and deliver an international AI framework that supports
      these powers in our future work. (Recommendation, Paragraph 53)

25. A safety regime is incomplete if responsibility remains unclear when
      safeguards fail or harm occurs, and those harmed do not have clear and
      effective routes to redress. Clarifying liability arrangements, and ensuring AI
      firms cooperate with them, will require international collaboration, common
      standards and effective enforcement. (Conclusion, Paragraph 56)

26. The Law Commission will report on its product liability consultation in
      due course. Following the Law Commission’s report, the Government
      should work with international partners to clarify the responsibilities
      of developers, deployers, infrastructure providers and users where AI
      systems cause harm or safeguards fail. In addition, the Government
      should identify outstanding matters and gaps outside the scope of the
      Law Commission’s consultation and set out how it intends to address those

                                                33
      gaps. Ministers should coordinate with devolved administrations and set
      a timetable for responding to the Law Commission’s recommendations.
      (Recommendation, Paragraph 57)

27. Employees working on frontier AI systems may be among the first to
      identify serious safety concerns, but concerns are often raised publicly only
      after individuals have left their organisations. Contractual restrictions,
      financial disincentives and uncertainty about appropriate reporting routes
      discourage employees from raising concerns. Effective AI governance
      therefore requires clear and protected channels for reporting serious
      prospective AI risks. (Conclusion, Paragraph 60)

28. The Government should establish protected confidential reporting to the
      AI regulator. This means reviewing whistleblowing categories, prescribed
      recipients, worker coverage and remedies for serious prospective AI risks.
      Lawful reporting must be protected notwithstanding confidentiality clauses.
      (Recommendation, Paragraph 61)

   Prevention of harm approach

29. A duty to prevent harm and a requirement for safety by design may provide
      a viable approach for managing AI risks, alongside other measures.
      Such an approach would require adjustments to tackle the unique
      risks of AI, but the precautionary approach has long been a feature of
      safety legislation and recently has informed legislation for online safety.
      (Conclusion, Paragraph 67)

30. Using the AI Safety and Security Bill, the Government should introduce
      a duty to prevent harm on developers of AI models. This should cover
      development, testing, internal deployment, supply, modification and
      operation, protecting third parties and customers. Duties could follow
      control over tools, permissions, connections and monitoring. The Bill should
      include delegated powers to extend into these areas through secondary
      legislation, following consultation, if required. Protection should continue
      following a model’s release. To safeguard the risks of models developed
      beyond the UK’s jurisdiction, the Government should seek to enshrine this
      approach in an international convention. (Recommendation, Paragraph 68)

   Digital rights

31. AI models are increasingly able to independently undertake tasks and
      perpetrate harms against the individual. Action to protect citizens from AI-
      facilitated harms must be guided by a focus on established individual rights.
      (Conclusion, Paragraph 75)

                                                34
32. The Government should affirm that enshrined individual rights will inform
      all aspects of government policy on AI regulation. The Government should
      explain how established rights will be given practical effect through its AI
      regulatory framework, which should include: proportionate safeguards
      against deceptive or manipulative uses; requirements for transparency
      where people interact with AI; and accessible routes to complaint,
      investigation and redress. (Recommendation, Paragraph 76)

   The UK’s international leadership: a
   window of opportunity

33. The UK cannot regulate frontier AI developers unilaterally, but it is
      well placed to shape international approaches to AI safety through
      technical leadership, standard-setting and international coordination.
      The pursuit of international agreement should not become an excuse
      to defer domestic action. The UK’s ability to influence international
      approaches will be strengthened by a coherent domestic framework.
      (Conclusion, Paragraph 80)

34. Within three months, the Government should publish objectives for its
      G20 presidency and work through the international network of AI safety
      institutes. These should include common approaches to assessing
      capabilities, safeguards and residual risks; criteria for escalating scrutiny
      and intervention; evaluation and monitoring; incident reporting; safety
      standards; and high-risk open-weight releases. The Government should
      identify negotiating priorities, responsible ministers and milestones, and
      report progress alongside domestic implementation. The Government
      should also assess options for more formal international governance
      arrangements, including whether there is a case for pursuing a multilateral
      convention or treaty on advanced AI systems and associated risks. The
      Committee will examine these objectives through its further work on
      international AI safety and security. (Recommendation, Paragraph 81)
