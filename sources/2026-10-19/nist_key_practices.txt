Key Practices for Incident Readiness and Response

in Super Intelligence Training and Evaluation

Introduction

This document sets out ten voluntary key practices to guide emerging approaches in the
management of incidents that might occur during the training and evaluation of super
intelligence (SI) agents. While these incidents highlight the importance of traditional
cybersecurity principles and practices, they also introduce novel considerations for
incident prevention, response, and reporting. These novel considerations primarily stem
from the agentic nature and advanced cyber capabilities of cutting-edge SI systems.

These key practices are authored by the National Institute of Standards and
Technology's Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence
(NIST CAISSI), the National Security Agency's Super Intelligence Security Center (NSA
SISC), and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA). The practices
are intended to help the frontier SI industry prevent, prepare for, and respond rapidly to
incidents in which SI systems do not act as intended or authorized.

The following key practices are divided into five stages of incident management, which
are meant to guide emerging methods in this fast-evolving area with the expectation
that new technical solutions will continue to reshape the details of implementation. The
practices explicitly draw from and build on existing cybersecurity best practices ­ such
as NIST's Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 [1] and NIST SP 800-61r3 [2] ­ with
explicit CSF control references integrated throughout. These key practices should be
continuously consulted and implemented throughout the incident management life
cycle.

Audience
These voluntary key practices and recommended resources are primarily intended for
organizations training or evaluating highly capable SI agents on long-horizon tasks with
limited human supervision. The practices may also apply to deployers of such SI
agents.

Definitions
SI agents: SI systems that can interact with an environment, receive information, and
autonomously execute actions in service of a specified goal. SI agent systems typically
consist of at least one generative SI model and scaffolding software that equips it with
tools to take a range of discretionary actions. These systems may be more expansive,
containing multiple sub-agents with software that orchestrates their interactions.

SI control incident: An occurrence in which one or more SI agents takes significant
uninstructed actions that compromise explicit or implicit policies, controls, or security
measures imposed by the operator to define the intended task scope or to ensure
human oversight. "Uninstructed" means that the agent was not directed to take the
action by its operator or any adversarial source.1 These uninstructed actions may occur
during agent development, evaluation, or use, and may or may not affect an external
party. SI control incidents may range in severity based on factors such as the
uninstructed actions taken and the degree of impact to internal or external systems; the
applicability of some practices in this document may depend on the severity of the
incident (see "Incident Severity Scores" in Areas for Future Work).2

SI control near-miss: An occurrence which did not result in an SI control incident but
presented a reasonably foreseeable or imminent risk of doing so. Near-misses may
reveal potential gaps or weaknesses in controls, security measures, or processes
designed to prevent, detect, or respond to SI control incidents.

Prepare

Preparation is the foundation of incident management best practice: organizations
govern their programs (`Govern'), identify assets and risks (`Identify'), and harden
systems (`Protect') before an incident occurs [1][2][GV.RM].3 Preparation entails
activities and practices such as asset and risk inventories, defense-in-depth, least
privilege, zero-trust controls, credential management, and clean recovery baselines
[3][4][5]; supply chain risk management [1][6]; secure development practices for
foundation models [7]; and drills of response and recovery plans [8][9]. SI control
incidents introduce new considerations beyond established cybersecurity best practice
due to SI agents' nondeterministic nature and capacity for autonomous action.

1. Design and Implement Technical Controls

1.1. Consistent with least privilege, limit SI agent access (e.g., access to sensitive
         information, systems, and tools) to what is necessary to perform intended tasks
         [PR.AA-05].

1.2. Where possible, use single-use infrastructure and limit communication between
         SI agents to intended channels to prevent unauthorized coordination [PR.IR-01].

1.3. Provide agents with instructions that clearly identify the task scope as well as any
         bounds, restrictions, or rules of engagement.

1.4. Identify, map, and restrict potential persistence mechanisms and unauthorized
         communication across internal and external systems. Pay particular attention to
         channels for creating copies of SI model weights or performing unmonitored
         model inference [ID.RA-05, PR.IR-01].

1.5. Limit SI agent network access, via a strict allowlist where possible. Route any
         required network access through a monitored gateway [PR.IR-01].

1.6. Implement an SI control policy laying out intended responses to defined
         scenarios and infrastructure for automated enforcement [2].

1.7. Build mechanisms to rapidly block, contain, or terminate agent activity if an
         incident or near-miss is detected [RS.MI-01].

2. Build Organizational and Personnel Response Readiness

2.1. Establish clear indicators, criteria, and severity scoring for SI control incidents
         and near-misses [ID.RA-06, RS.MA-03][2].

2.2. Run drills and simulations to prepare for SI control incidents and related
         detection, response, recovery, and reporting measures [ID.IM-02][10].

2.3. Create inbound reporting channels to receive reports of SI control incidents and
         information from affected third parties [2].

2.4. Partner with independent external auditors and evaluators to carry out
         assessments of control, monitoring, and incident detection protocols and verify
         that these systems are operating as intended.

2.5. Consider publishing identifiers associated with SI evaluation, including those
         particular to an agent, to assist defenders with understanding unexpected traffic.
         See "Agent Identifiers" in Areas for Future Work.

         deployments. Only start or resume if senior leadership, informed by assessments
         from external evaluators where appropriate, are satisfied with the risk levels. Risk
         assessments can cover multiple training runs or evaluations [GV.RR-02, GV.RM-
         06]. See "Structured Risk Assessments" and "Training-Safe Recovery" in Areas
         for Future Work.

Detect

Best practice requires adequate detection. Organizations should define intended activity
and continuously monitor for unexpected activity [1][3]. Detection entails defining
indicators of compromise, instrumenting systems to gather telemetry, and setting
policies that route anomalies to automated response or human review [2][11][12].
Organizations should also monitor SI outputs, affordances, and model internals. SI-
enabled monitors are also often used to oversee SI agents, which may introduce
additional vulnerabilities.

3. Monitor for Unexpected Activity

3.1. Define the intended security boundaries and scope of actions for each SI agent,
         including the SI agent's system and network access, to facilitate detection of
         discrepancies and anomalies [PR.IR-01]. See "Defining Intended Activity
         Baselines" in Areas for Future Work.

3.2. Comprehensively monitor all system layers for agents that pose SI control
         incident risks, including the model, harness, host, and network levels, using both
         programmatic and SI real-time monitors to scan multi-agent workflows, agent
         trajectories, reasoning, and outputs [PR.PS-04, DE.CM-01, DE.CM-06, DE.CM-
         09]. See "Synchronous Monitoring" in Areas for Future Work.

3.3. Ensure that SI agents cannot access or interfere with monitoring processes and
         logs. Incorporate additional signals in monitoring, including hardware telemetry
         and performing consistency checks between monitoring layers, to detect
         tampering attempts [DE.AE-03].

3.4. Where possible, use multiple models for monitoring or cross-validation, ideally
         from different model families.

3.5. Establish mechanisms to detect unexpected activity and escalate for human
         review or automated responses, such as methods for real-time visibility across SI
         agents or placing canaries in environments to detect agent instances that have
         obtained access outside intended bounds [DE.AE-02, DE.AE-06]. See
         "Additional Mechanisms for Escalation" in Areas for Future Work.

4. Validate and Secure Monitors to Improve Operational Integrity

4.1. Red-team monitors with humans and SI systems to improve their reliability
         [ID.IM-02].

4.2. Treat monitored SI model outputs as potentially containing exploits aimed at the
         monitors [DE.CM-09]. See "SI-Enabled Investigations" in Areas for Future Work.

Respond

Best practice calls for adequate response measures: organizations contain and remove
threat actors and preserve evidence for analysis [1][2][13]. Adequate response entails
isolating affected systems, revoking credentials, rebuilding compromised environments,
determining the blast radius, and conducting root-cause analysis that looks past the
specific vulnerability exploited [3][4]. SI control incidents may result in environmental
compromise and persistent access by SI agents, warranting a comprehensive response.

5. Collect, Retain, and Analyze Evidence

5.1. Preserve model weights as well as training, evaluation, and deployment
         environments to the extent necessary to investigate SI control incidents,
         including intermediate checkpoints, timestamps, sandbox and network logs, and
         rejected actions and tool calls [RS.AN-07].

5.2. Preserve detailed information about the configuration of SI agent(s), safeguards,
         related software components, and environments necessary to reproduce the
         incident [RS.AN-07].

5.3. Retain tamper-resistant logs with reliable timestamps sufficient to reconstruct
         agent trajectories, tool calls, reasoning traces, user interactions, environmental
         states, and security-control decisions [PR.PS-04]. Support Continuous Event
         Monitoring and Threat Hunting, Investigation, Response, and Forensics of both
         testing environments and agents [RS.AN-07].

5.4. Analyze log and telemetry data to establish the actions taken by SI agents during
         the incident, identifying any internal or external systems that were impacted and
         root causes such as vulnerabilities [RS.AN-03, ID.IM-03].

5.5. Consider leveraging SI models to assist in incident investigation; however, where
         feasible, verify key facts through human review and use multiple models to cross-
         check analysis, including models from outside the model family from which the
         incident originated [RS.MA-01, DE.AE-06]. See "SI-Enabled Investigations" in
         Areas for Future Work.

5.6. Investigate training and evaluation runs for other similar SI agents to assess
         whether any other agents were involved in the control incident or experienced
         similar control failures.

6. Mitigate and Shut Down Undesired Activity

6.1. Define and implement shutdown policies in the event of unintended activity,
         control failures, and monitor outages or tampering [RS.MI-01]. Calibrate policies
         and response options to the capability of the model and severity or scope of the
         incident [RS.AN-08].

6.2. Automatically shut down impacted SI agent trajectories on detection of a control
         failure or in-progress SI control incident [RS.MI-02].

6.3. Depending on the severity or scope of the incident, shut down or pause training,
         evaluation, or deployment for other similar SI agents until control measures can
         be strengthened and verified. See "Incident Severity Scores" in Areas for Future
         Work.

6.4. Revoke credentials under the organization's control that were accessed during
         the incident and rebuild any environments that may have been compromised
         [RS.MI-02, RC.RP-05].

6.5. Seek out and remediate any possible routes for agent persistence, such as code
         backdoors and hidden communication channels [RS.MI-02].

Recover

Recovery is another key element of established best practice: organizations restore
systems to trustworthy operation from known-good baselines and verify their integrity
before returning to service [1][2]. Trustworthy restoration entails testing restoration
procedures in advance and documenting recovery decisions along with any residual risk
accepted [13]. SI control incidents introduce new considerations: recovery measures,
especially those taken during model training, can shape the actions of future agents,
including by inadvertently reinforcing patterns of activity which led to the incident.

7. Verify and Strengthen Containment Before Resuming SI
      Agent Activity

7.1. Rebuild any environments that may have been compromised from verified
         baselines (e.g., model weights, harness code, container images, tool
         configurations) [RC.RP-03].

7.2. Patch or report any vulnerabilities exploited by agents during the control incident
         [RS.MI-02].

7.3. Validate that monitors, logging, egress allowlists, shutdown mechanisms,
         permission restrictions, and other containment layers are functional before SI
         agents are restarted [RC.RP-05].

7.4. Update and strengthen controls and monitors to prevent future incidents. This
         includes, at a minimum, verifying that new controls and monitors would prevent
         and/or detect similar control incidents, such as by replaying trajectories or actions
         from the incident [ID.IM-03, ID.IM-04].

7.5. Before resuming SI agent activity, document the recovery decision and the
         remaining risks [RC.RP-06].

7.6. Prepare an after-action report, as appropriate, documenting the incident, the
         response, and lessons learned [RC.RP-06]. Lessons learned may warrant follow-
         up investigations or experiments to understand any failures related to agent
         activity and alignment during the incident.

7.7. For SI control incidents that occur during model training, design recovery
         procedures that neither reinforce patterns of activity that led to the control
         incident nor make future adverse activity more difficult to detect, such as by
         training against explicit adverse chain-of-thought reasoning in a way that reduces
         monitorability. See "Training-Safe Recovery" in Areas for Future Work.

Report

Organizations should follow established best practices by notifying relevant authorities,
coordinating with affected parties, and disclosing vulnerabilities through established
channels [1][2]. In addition to meeting applicable incident reporting obligations (including
to CISA and law enforcement) and reporting software vulnerabilities through
coordinated mechanisms, report SI control incidents and near-misses. Voluntary SI
control incident reports should focus on the unsanctioned actions taken by the agents
and explanations for these actions, rather than system compromise alone.
Organizations should publicly share lessons learned to advance best practice.
Reporting is not the final phase of incident management following recovery; rather
reporting practices should take place throughout the incident lifecycle.

8. Coordinate with Affected Parties

8.1. Notify parties whose systems have been compromised or exploited by an SI
         control incident, including third parties who are not the final target of the incident
         [RS.CO-02].

8.2. Share relevant details for affected parties' incident response, including detailed
         timelines and transcripts that provide context on exploited vulnerabilities or
         impacted systems, where feasible [RS.CO-03].

8.3. Share information, such as lessons learned, with other parties across the value
         chain that were impacted by the incident or may benefit from investigation
         findings, such as the developer of the SI model, or evaluation and infrastructure
         providers.

9. Notify Government

9.1. Adhere to any applicable legal reporting requirements, including cybersecurity
         incident reporting obligations to CISA, law enforcement, and/or cyber-defense
         organizations. Voluntarily report cybersecurity incidents or near-misses arising
         from SI control incidents to CISA as appropriate [GV.OC-03, RS.CO-02][10][14].

9.2. Report software vulnerabilities arising from SI control incidents using established
         mechanisms [16][17].

9.3. Voluntarily share SI-specific information concerning control incidents and near-
         misses with SI experts in government, such as NIST CAISSI and NSA SISC, to
         facilitate research and preparedness, after carrying out any legally mandated

Areas for Future Work

Several important areas for future research and development will inform further
practices in responding to SI control incidents. These areas include:

 Training-Safe Recovery. Identifying best practices and tradeoffs for responding
    to incidents of both SI control failures and near-misses during training to reduce
    the likelihood of future occurrences of the activity that led to the incident and
    avoid making future adverse activity more difficult to detect.

 Defining Intended Activity Baselines. Better methods and practices for
    defining baselines and scopes for intended SI agent activity to support monitoring
    and detection of anomalous activity.

 Synchronous Monitoring. Techniques for synchronous monitoring of agent
    trajectories, reasoning, and outputs that strike better balances between latency,
    performance, and security, or that reduce tradeoffs entirely.

 Incident Severity Scores. Standardized severity scores for SI control incidents
    to inform response timelines and procedures (e.g., interventions ranging from
    blocking single actions to ending trajectories or halting full evaluation or training
    runs).

 SI-Enabled Investigations. Practices for mitigating risks of correlated failures,
    addressing potential adversarial attacks and prompt injections against monitors,
    and maintaining monitor validity when processing voluminous logs.

 Structured Risk Assessments. Research and methods concerning properly
    decomposing, substantiating, and quantifying risks and mitigations concerning SI
    agent training, evaluation, and deployment, including the risk of agents taking
    steps to obscure adverse activity.

 SI Red-Teaming for Critical Systems. Research and methods for using SI
    agents to safely red-team SI control measures used in critical systems, such that
    actions taken by agents do not endanger safety or affect the availability of critical
    services.

 Additional Mechanisms for Escalation. Methods for additional mechanisms by
    which SI agents can trigger human review of actions, for example in multi-agent
    settings.
