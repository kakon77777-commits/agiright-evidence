Section-by-Section Summary

                                   FRONTIER Act 2.0 (discussion draft)

Sec. 1. Short title.

This section provides that the Act may be cited as the Frontier Risk Oversight, National
Transparency, Independent Evaluation, and Reporting Act of 2027 -- the FRONTIER Act of
2027.

Sec. 2. Definitions.

This section defines the key terms used throughout the Act, including the following:

Frontier model / frontier developer -- The term "frontier model" means a foundation model
trained using more than 1025 operations, counting the original training run and any subsequent
modification. The term "frontier developer" means any entity operating in or affecting interstate
or foreign commerce that has trained, or initiated training of, such a model. Once a developer is
covered, its obligations reach all of its foundation models, except that model cards remain
limited to frontier models.

Large / very large frontier developer -- These terms establish the higher coverage tiers, keyed
to AI-related development expenditures and measured with affiliates over the preceding 36
months: at least $1B in such expenditures for the large tier, and at least $5B for the very large
tier. Each higher tier carries every lower tier's duties.

Catastrophic risk -- The term "catastrophic risk" means a foreseeable, material risk that a
frontier developer's foundation model materially contributes to 50+ deaths or serious injuries, or
$1B+ in property damage, in a single incident involving the model (i) providing expert-level
assistance in developing or releasing a CBRN weapon or in conducting offensive cyber
operations, (ii) engaging in conduct without meaningful human oversight that is a cyberattack or
would constitute murder, assault, extortion, or theft, (iii) evading the control of its developer or
user, or (iv) automating AI research and development in a way that substantially accelerates any
of those risks. Because automated AI research and development, including recursive self-
improvement (RSI), is its own category of catastrophic risk, each of the Act's obligations applies
to it: frameworks must address it, large developers report quarterly on AI R&D capabilities and
internal use, and very large developers must retain an IVO dedicated to assessing it.

Imminent catastrophic risk -- The term "imminent catastrophic risk" means a present or
impending catastrophic risk, and serves as the predicate for emergency orders and mandatory
IVO referral.

Acceptable levels of catastrophic risk mitigation -- The term means risk mitigation adequate to
ensure that a foundation model's anticipated benefits outweigh its catastrophic risk, taking into
account the probability and magnitude of each.

Critical safety incident -- The term "critical safety incident" means unauthorized exfiltration or
malicious modification of model weights; a cyberattack on the developer; harm from a
materialized catastrophic risk; loss of control, including the model deceiving its developer to
subvert controls, outside a designed evaluation; a near miss; developer-identified incidents
bearing on catastrophic risk or public safety; incidents affecting critical infrastructure; or
incidents the Secretary designates by rule.

Near miss -- The term "near miss" means an event in which a material safeguard, control, or
monitoring system applied to a foundation model, including during testing or internal use, fails,
is bypassed, or is materially degraded, and, but for timely intervention or luck, would likely have
led to the compromise of model weights, harm from a materialized catastrophic risk, or loss of
control, but no such harm resulted.

Secretary -- The term refers to the Secretary of Commerce, acting through a new Office of AI
Security (Sec. 3), who holds the Act's rulemaking authority, receives incident reports and
registrations, and licenses and oversees IVOs.

Sec. 3. Office of AI Security.

This section establishes an Office of AI Security within the Department of Commerce, headed by
a Senate-confirmed Assistant Secretary of Commerce for AI Security, through which the
Secretary carries out the Act, provides hiring authority, and authorizes $100 million a year for
the Office.

Sec. 4. Rulemaking and guidance.

This section vests the Secretary with enumerated notice-and-comment rulemaking authorities to
carry out the Act. The Secretary must issue rules governing IVO licensing and oversight,
including time-limited, category-specific licenses, graduated sanctions, and scaled fees, and
setting criteria for substantial (model) and material (framework) modifications. The Secretary
may also issue rules adjusting the compute and expenditure coverage thresholds and adding
capability-based criteria for frontier models, calibrated to reach the developers whose activities
present catastrophic risk, and governing incident reporting, without narrowing the statutory
definition of a critical safety incident. It also directs the Secretary to issue nonbinding guidance,
after public comment, on approaches large frontier developers may use to meet the Act's
mitigation standard, along with competence standards for interim assessors and a model access
plan for IVOs, reviewed annually in light of corrective orders.

Sec. 5. Cumulative obligations.

This section provides that each higher tier complies with every requirement applicable to the
tiers below it.

Sec. 6. Registration and disclosure.

This section requires large frontier developers to register with the Secretary, as a condition of
developing, deploying, or operating any foundation model, by filing and annually renewing a
disclosure statement identifying the developer, its significant owners, and its points of contact. It
directs the Secretary to collect fees to offset administrative costs and maintain the public registry
of filed developers.

Sec. 7. Model cards.
This section requires all frontier developers to publish, before or when deploying a new or
substantially modified frontier model, a model card on its capabilities, intended uses, and
restrictions. Large frontier developers must also include their catastrophic-risk assessments and
results.

Sec. 8. Incident reporting.

This section requires reporting of critical safety incidents to the Secretary within 72 hours of
when the developer knew or reasonably should have known of them (near misses and other
developer-identified incidents, once identified), or within 24 hours where an incident poses an
imminent threat, with follow-up reports on the most serious incidents. Large frontier developers
must also report quarterly on catastrophic risk arising from their foundation models, including
internal use and capabilities that could automate or substantially accelerate AI research and
development; file a rapid report within 72 hours of a substantial increase in that risk; and certify
in each quarterly report that they have reported every critical safety incident known to them. A
governor may designate a State emergency management agency to receive incident information
affecting the State under a confidentiality agreement.

Sec. 9. Frontier AI frameworks.

This section requires large frontier developers to write, implement, comply with, and publish a
frontier AI framework -- covering catastrophic-risk thresholds and assessment, model-weight
cybersecurity, incident response, and deployment and internal-use decisions, and incorporating
widely-accepted national, international, and industry approaches, best practices, frameworks, and
methods -- that is reasonably designed to achieve acceptable levels of catastrophic risk
mitigation for each of their foundation models. It requires annual review of the framework and
an annual independent compliance audit, with publication of the redacted audit report and its
transmittal to the Secretary.

Sec. 10. Developer redactions and protection of submitted information.

This section permits redactions in published material only as necessary to protect trade secrets,
risk-prevention mechanisms, cybersecurity, public safety, national security, or privacy, with a
justification for each redaction and an unredacted copy to the Secretary, who may require an
overbroad redaction to be narrowed. Information submitted to the government is exempt from
FOIA and similar State and local disclosure laws.

Sec. 11. Independent verification.

This section directs the Secretary to license and oversee IVOs. It requires each very large frontier
developer to retain, for each category of catastrophic risk, an IVO for ongoing assessment of
whether its framework, governance, risk-monitoring, and mitigations achieve acceptable levels
of catastrophic risk mitigation. IVO selection phases in by category: the developer first chooses
its own assessor that meets industry competence standards and the Act's independence
requirements; once licensed IVOs have capacity for a category, the Secretary assigns the IVO;
and as the market matures, Secretary assignment is combined with developer choice. Assigned
IVOs are paid from a fee-funded pool; developer-selected IVOs are paid by the developer.
Access is governed by a single access plan for each developer, proposed by its lead IVO and
approved by the Secretary, covering unredacted materials, model weights, internal-use models,
and personnel, with a resident IVO team on site; the developer may object only on limited
grounds before an administrative law judge.

The IVO determines the scope and frequency of its assessments, reporting at least every 6
months and out of cycle when risk changes, and recommends corrective actions. If the
developer's response is inadequate and it does not cure the deficiency, the Secretary may, after
notice and a hearing, order the developer to take necessary actions, subject to judicial review;
compliance with an order satisfies the framework standard for that deficiency. It requires an IVO
that determines a model poses an imminent catastrophic risk to refer the matter to the Secretary
within 72 hours for consideration of a Sec. 15 emergency order, and to notify the Secretary of
unreported critical safety incidents.

It sets statutory independence standards for IVOs, provides for regular inspection of IVOs, and
voids engagement terms that tie payment to results or penalize adverse findings. It permits IVOs
to subcontract specialized portions of an assessment while remaining fully responsible, prohibits
material misrepresentations in any report or opinion, and protects IVOs from suit over good-faith
findings and reports, other than for bad faith or willful misconduct.

Sec. 12. IVO capacity-building grants.

This section authorizes the Secretary to award grants and cooperative agreements to build IVO
capacity, with priority for categories of catastrophic risk that have too few licensed IVOs and for
risks from automated AI research and development, but not to pay for assessing any particular
developer.

Sec. 13. Enforcement.

This section authorizes the Attorney General to enforce the Act through civil penalties and
injunctions. Violations carry a civil penalty of up to the greater of $1M per violation or a
percentage of global annual revenue, with higher caps for repeat violations, and claims that a
framework is not reasonably designed require prior notice and an opportunity to cure. It
penalizes knowing IVO misrepresentations, prohibits materially false or misleading statements
about catastrophic risk or compliance, and requires the Secretary to refer IVO-reported violations
to the Attorney General or explain in writing why not. Violations of a Sec. 11 corrective order
are enforceable on the same terms, and the Attorney General may not bring a framework-
reasonableness claim over an IVO-identified deficiency while the corrective-action process is
pending or an order addressing it is in effect.

Sec. 14. Near-miss penalties.

This section authorizes the Secretary to assess a civil penalty against a frontier developer for
each near miss involving its models. The Secretary sets penalty amounts by rule so that each
developer's expected penalties equal the expected catastrophic harm its models create beyond
what it could pay (its assets after such harm, plus insurance). Each near miss bears the expected
harm on the pathway it reveals, such as loss of control or weapons misuse, divided by the
number of near misses that pathway is expected to produce, so that, for a given level of risk,
rarer near misses carry larger penalties. If the developer failed to report a near miss as Sec. 8
requires, the penalty is multiplied by 10, unless the developer reasonably concluded in good faith
that the event was not reportable. Near misses in designed evaluations carry no penalty. A
developer may contest a penalty in district court, which reviews it de novo. Penalties are in
addition to those under Sec. 13 and apply only to near misses involving models deployed or used
internally after the rule takes effect.

Sec. 15. Emergency orders.

This section authorizes the Secretary of Commerce to suspend or restrict a frontier developer's
development, deployment, or internal use of a foundation model upon a written finding that it
presents an imminent catastrophic risk. A provisional order may issue on a preliminary
determination -- with notice and an opportunity to cure unless the imminence of the risk
forecloses them -- and expires after 45 days; a final order requires the written finding and may
be renewed only on a fresh finding. Each order must state its basis, why a narrower measure
would not suffice, the restrictions imposed, and the conditions for rescission, and the Secretary
must rescind the order once those conditions are met or the risk has passed. The developer may
seek an expedited administrative hearing at which the Secretary bears the burden of justifying the
order, and expedited judicial review in the D.D.C., with appeal to the D.C. Circuit; to keep a
longer order in place, the Secretary must prove its necessity in court. Congress receives each
order and semiannual reports on the use of the authority. Violations carry a civil penalty of up to
$10M per violation per day, and willful violations are criminal.

Sec. 16. Reports to Congress.

This section directs the Secretary to report annually to Congress on critical safety incidents and
IVO-reported violations, and directs the Comptroller General to report annually to House Energy
& Commerce and Senate Commerce on the state of the IVO market, IVOs' independence from
the AI industry, and recommendations to preserve that independence.

Sec. 17. Relationship to State law.

This section provides that nothing in the Act preempts any State law at least as protective of
persons or property as the Act, except for the confidentiality of federal materials shared with
States and the protection for IVOs' good-faith performance.

Sec. 18. General provisions.

This section provides that the Act takes effect on enactment and includes a severability clause.

Sec. 19. Sunset.

This section provides that the Act ceases to have effect 10 years after enactment.
