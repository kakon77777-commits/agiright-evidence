In the final months of his life, Henry Kissinger was preoccupied with the challenges of a world he would not live to see. Even at the age of 100, his gaze, characteristically, was fixed on the future. On his last trip, he met with President Xi in Beijing to discuss deeper collaboration. Kissinger had come to believe that the advent of superintelligence could represent a novel class of strategic danger. And he recognized that the obligation of great nations, even—and perhaps especially—those with profound differences, is to find common purpose. So, at President Trump’s direction, we have begun that important work by establishing a new dialogue with China on SI, designed to guard against shared risks and prevent a misunderstanding between our two systems. We also expect to exchange best practices for evaluating and testing advanced models before their release, and for keeping human judgment firmly in command of this technology. Finally, we are establishing a direct channel to ensure that either nation can promptly alert the other to serious incidents involving its SI models. As SI advances, so will the need to engage in new areas of risk management—and to demonstrate how two nations can reconcile the demands of competition with the responsibilities of leadership. As we do so, we should harbor no illusions about the differences between the United States and China. As I stated earlier, our interests will not always coincide. And our approach to every challenge, likewise, will not always converge. Those early China watchers of the 1960s understood that the differences between our nations were too profound to ignore. But they also knew that the specter of our estrangement was too great to accept.
