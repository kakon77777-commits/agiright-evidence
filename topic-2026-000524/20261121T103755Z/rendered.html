Overview

This post presents evidence from our capability evaluations of public models relevant to AI R&D acceleration, up to Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra. We remain significantly uncertain about central questions, including (1) what capabilities are in practice necessary for drastic acceleration, and (2) how quickly relevant model capabilities are improving.

We think that current public evidence is insufficient to rule out drastic AI R&D acceleration in the next couple of years. By drastic AI R&D acceleration we mean a situation where capabilities improve so quickly that humans are no longer able to meaningfully understand, assess, and react to catastrophic risks posed by new AI systems.

This overview summarizes our main takeaways and their relevant sections within the post.

1 Background describes our motivation and aims, and how our thinking about capability evaluations has changed since the release of the original time horizons paper.

2 Methodology describes the different task sets used throughout the post, changes to human baselining methodology, and introduces our use of multiple score thresholds per task.

3 Results contains the evidence for our main takeaways, which are:

  • Models usually match or outperform skilled humans on our coding and optimization tasks (Section 3.A.1). When we recruited our own human baseliners, we typically failed to find humans that outperform the current frontier models. In a couple of cases where we have existing sources of many (10+) domain specialists attempting a task, models tend to place at a very high percentile but not the absolute top (Section 3.A.2).
  • On our dataset, models perform somewhat worse on tasks that we estimate are costlier to turn into RL training environments, even after controlling for how long these tasks would take humans. However, models appear to be improving at similar rates on our lower and higher cost tasks (Section 3.B.1). We observe that various properties that can make an eval more realistic also make the eval costlier to make, run, and score. We use a model estimated measure we call ‘Lifecycle Cost’ to try to quantify this observed costliness. In our data, a doubling of lifecycle cost for a task of a given length imposes a difficulty penalty that is very roughly equivalent to halving the time horizon of the agent. Despite this, we see similar rates of progress on high and low cost subsets of our tasks, and we also see AI performance improving over time even on our most costly tasks.
  • On our tasks which require choices in the absence of clear empirical feedback, AI agents appear to have worse judgment than human experts (Section 3.B.2). This matters especially when feedback is slow or the available tests provide limited evidence that a solution will generalize. For instance, in one task we found that compared to an expert scorer, model judges overestimate an output’s quality when the output is made by a similarly strong or stronger model.
  • On the combined suite of TH1.1 and TH2, time horizons are doubling roughly every 3.6 months (Section 3.C.1). Since TH2 is more difficult, any given model has a lower 50% time horizon on this combined suite compared to on TH1.1 alone.⁠ 1 We find that horizon doubling trends are shorter on a subset of TH2 (the verifiable subset) when excluding TH1.1. This could be consistent with a recent acceleration in time horizon that is hidden in the combined trend (which includes both TH1.1 and TH2) due to TH2 tasks being harder for a given time horizon, but there are also other plausible explanations. The doubling time on the combined suite is similar to the trend recently reported by OpenAI on internal research tasks.⁠2
  • When tracking public model capabilities on a composite metric of model ability over time, we do not see strong acceleration or deceleration in model ability over the public frontier with the models we tested. However, we do see uncertain evidence of developer-specific trend changes in some conditions (Section 3.C.2). We use an index (METR Capability Index, or MCI) to track frontier AI capabilities over the past few years based on IRT, a statistical modelling technique similar to the one underlying the Epoch Capabilities Index. Similar to other indices of this kind⁠3, we do not observe slope changes in the overall public frontier trend (on the public models we tested) but we do observe some developer-specific slope changes.⁠4 However, we are unsure of the validity of these findings due to their sensitivity to methodological choices.

4 Robustness details various checks we did on our results, such as checking for reward hacking and refusals. We also investigate the effectiveness of agent swarms as an elicitation technique and find that they seem helpful on verifiable tasks with a clear metric and fast feedback (compared to best-of-k⁠5), and have unclear or negative effects on our other tasks.

5 Discussion describes open questions for assessing AI R&D acceleration.

The appendices give more detail on our methodology, report robustness checks for our analyses, and highlight individual tasks with notable model behavior.

1 Background

In this post we focus on the measurement of capabilities relevant to accelerating AI R&D. A particularly salient uncertainty for AI R&D relevant capability evaluation is whether strong performance on ‘well-specified’ tasks alone is sufficient for drastic AI R&D acceleration, or whether other capabilities are needed. We aim to present evidence that is informative regardless of the reader’s view on this question.

Historically, METR used our ‘time horizon’ metric as a way to measure the SWE-related agentic capabilities of AI systems.⁠6 Time horizon measurements were useful for risk assessments because (1) We considered the capabilities we were measuring with our time horizon suite to be necessary to pose catastrophic risk, (2) Our time horizon measurements were substantially grounded in human reference data, which made it easier to relate to risk relevant thresholds, and (3) measured capabilities were far from possible danger, which allowed us to argue these systems posed negligible catastrophic risk with reasonable confidence. For example, see METR’s evaluation of GPT-5.

Much has changed since the original release of time horizon, and this has led to various changes in our approach and thinking:

  • Public models have gotten a lot better at many tasks: When time horizon was first released in March 2025, the best models had a measured time horizon of around 1 hour (Sonnet 3.7). Within a year, models had saturated the TH1.1 suite, which includes tasks up to 30 hours. In many domains, it is now challenging and expensive to find human baseliners who outperform the models.⁠7
  • Public models have strong enough capabilities in areas like coding and hacking that we no longer think we can construct an argument that risk is very low due to incapability on verifiable SWE-based tasks alone: For instance, public frontier models can hack services,⁠ 8⁠ 9⁠10 reason about the mechanics of how they are graded⁠11, and bypass restrictions set by sandboxes.⁠12 As a result, in order to make claims about risk from capabilities, we need to either (1) engage more with the specifics of the capability profile of the agent, and the capabilities required for a specific threat model, or (2) find other necessary broad agentic capability limitations that bound risk.
  • We are increasingly concerned that our easy-to-run, containerized evals could overestimate real-world capabilities, due to similar RL environments becoming common in training. Both AI developers and evaluators have a shared desire for quantitative, cheap signal about AI capabilities (developers for training, and evaluators for measurements). As a result, we think evaluations often share much in common with the RL environments used to train models. This is a problem for evaluators because we often want to use our evaluations as a proxy for some other, harder-to-measure, capability of interest.⁠ 13 When these proxies (or nearby tasks) are trained on, they are liable to overestimate capabilities in these areas of interest.⁠14 We are somewhat concerned that AI developers' efforts to train on available cheap, quantitative capability signal mean that evaluators have relatively less uncontaminated signal to use for generalizable measurement of capabilities of interest.

These changes in the landscape have led to various modifications in our approach to capability evaluation. In summary:

  • Human baseline and time estimation changes: Because our new tasks are harder and we aim to assess model capabilities relative to AI R&D lab employees, we recruited more experienced human baseliners than the STEM graduates in our previous work. The required expertise and time commitment made recruitment difficult, so we collected substantially fewer human baselines. We also allowed human baseliners to use Sonnet 5 for implementation. We did this to help with recruitment and try to avoid slowdowns associated with baseliners being used to coding with AI assistance.⁠15 Human baselines are directly used for only 9% of our estimates for how long it takes a human to complete the task. For tasks without successful human baselines, we use an estimation procedure informed by public records, experts, and extrapolation that we detail in Appendix A: Difficulty Estimation and Baselining.
  • Task coverage and multiple score thresholds per task: To use our limited resources well we attempted to make more specific evals that targeted capabilities that we considered relevant to AI R&D relevant strengths and weaknesses. For instance, optimizing metrics, logic puzzles, and judgment-loaded tasks. As a result, the new tasks we have developed don’t cover as broad a range of general SWE work as the TH1.1 suite. Additionally, many of our new tasks now have multiple score thresholds, instead of just one. This allows us to extract more information from each task, at the cost of some of our analysis becoming more complicated,⁠16 see Section 2 for more information.
  • Investigation and handling of suspected systematic measuring issues: As mentioned above, we are concerned about systematic measurement issues related to similarity of our evaluations and RL environments. We attempt to mitigate this problem by using more manually scored tasks and qualitative evidence, or otherwise developing evaluations that we expect to be less similar to the environments AI developers train on, while still being related to the capabilities we’re interested in.

2 Methodology

We discuss results from several tasks and experiments, many of which have their own specific methods, budgets, and affordances (e.g., GPUs, internet access, etc.). This section describes shared methodology.

Agent Setup. For all tasks, we evaluated models using our standard ReAct-style scaffold which amongst other tools, has bash, python and image-viewing tools.⁠17 We run models with xhigh or higher reasoning effort (a setting that controls how much the model reasons before acting), and the highest available reasoning setting for models older than Feb 2026. Token and time limits were chosen per task with the aim that agent performance reached a plateau in further inference scaling, though this aim wasn’t always achieved, see Appendix I: Inference Scaling for token scaling curves.

Task Suites. Our results cover three distinct task suites:

  1. Time Horizon 1.1: 222 tasks including tasks from SWAA, HCAST, and RE-Bench.
  2. MirrorCode: 75⁠18 CLI software replication tasks.
  3. TH2: 36 tasks grouped into 12 task families⁠19 with two broad subsets:
    1. TH2 verifiable: 8 families covering optimization, ML, puzzles, and games.
    2. TH2 hard-to-verify & judgment: 4 families covering conceptual reasoning, judgment, and hard-to-verify research.

New tasks sets. The tasks we created sets span two kinds:

  • Verifiable: In these tasks, agents work towards a well-defined objective that is scored automatically, such as an optimization target, score, or accuracy on held-out inputs. They usually measure their progress against the objective while working, although in some tasks they are required to build proxy metrics.
  • Hard-to-verify & judgment: In our hard-to-verify tasks, agents work on an open-ended research problem with no cheap check for success or progress, and typically humans or a model given a human-written rubric judge final outputs. These research tasks are mostly based on our own work at METR. We also created more judgment-loaded tasks. By judgment, we’re broadly referring to the ability to make good choices in the absence of clear and fast empirical feedback.⁠20 In these tasks, agents are typically given an artifact (e.g., another agent’s transcripts on a task) and asked to judge its quality for some purpose.

Scoring. Unlike our earlier suites, TH2 includes some tasks that are manually graded by humans (Making METR Tasks) or model judges (Investigate Wiki Incident).⁠21 In Time Horizon 1.1, continuously scored tasks are binarized via a task-specific threshold chosen to represent human performance. For our new tasks, where possible, we instead use multiple score thresholds based on intermediate human performance on the task. We define these thresholds as (score, time) points: each point is the time taken by an expert human to reach a corresponding score.

We defined (score, time) points for 34 of the 36 new tasks using a mix of successful human baselines (3 tasks) and researcher estimates of score given time (31 tasks). Despite our best efforts to recruit strong human baseliners, sometimes our baselines fell short of what we estimated an AI R&D expert (e.g., lab employee) could achieve. In such cases, we used our estimates when we could make reasonable arguments for their feasibility. Our estimates usually draw on records of real human work, e.g., NanoGPT speedrun records, and in some cases experts or our own experience. Where a task had more than one baseline, we used the points of the highest-scoring baseline. See Appendix A: Difficulty Estimation and Baselining for more details. We include robustness checks on how sensitive the time horizon trends are to our estimates in Appendix B: Time Horizon Diagnostics.

Run coverage. On both TH1.1 and our new suite TH2 we typically ran 15 models: GPT-4o, o1, GPT-5, Opus 4.5, Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4, Opus 4.7, GPT-5.5, Opus 4.8, Fable 5, GPT-5.6 Sol, Opus 5, Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5.5. Where we refer to using the MirrorCode suite of tasks, we use older runs that do not cover the latest models.⁠22

Lastly, in this post we use an IRT methodology to summarize agent performance on a single scale and deal with uneven run coverage. We describe this methodology next.

METR Capability Index (MCI) Methodology

In some parts of our analyses, we want a common scale for model capabilities and task difficulties. Even though the capability profile of models is often ‘jagged’ compared to the human capability profile, model capability profiles are usually quite similar between models. This presents us with an opportunity to roughly summarize their capabilities with a single latent factor. Item Response Theory (IRT) models are one way to fit data to find these latent ‘strength’ factors. The METR Capability Index (MCI) is an IRT model that is very similar in spirit to the Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI). However, unlike the ECI, which aggregates model performance across public benchmarks, our model treats the individual task as its unit of analysis and uses tasks we developed ourselves.

Notably, unlike a ‘time horizon’-like axis, IRT models do not require calibration against human performance. The ability axis reflects models’ patterns of strengths and weaknesses across tasks, rather than those of humans.

For each of our tasks, we define a scale in [0,1], where 0 corresponds to failure and 1 corresponds to full score⁠23, and fit a two parameter logistic for the score an agent m will get on a task t⁠:

pmt=σ(αt(θm−βt)),σ(x)=11+e−x

where pmt is the expected score agent m will get on task t⁠, αt is the task discrimination, βt is the task difficulty, and θm is the agent ability. When an agent’s ability θm is equal to a task’s difficulty βt⁠, the agent is expected to achieve 50% of the maximum possible score. The slope parameter αt captures how quickly the expected score changes around that 50% point.

To fit this two-parameter logistic, we maximize the fractional-logit log-likelihood function. We also apply weightings to, for instance, upweight based on the number of runs we have on a task, downweight large numbers of similar tasks, and downweight ‘noisy’ tasks where our observed data is not well fit by our model. We generate 95% confidence intervals using hierarchical bootstrap across task families and runs. For full IRT model and fitting details, see Appendix C: MCI Modelling Methodology. We also do various robustness checks of our model, which can be found in Appendix E: MCI Robustness Checks.

3 Results

In this section we’ll cover our results and other collected evidence. Section 3.A covers AI R&D relevant model capability strengths, Section 3.B covers model limitations, and Section 3.C covers capability trends.

3.A AI R&D Acceleration Relevant Capabilities

We find that current public frontier models have extremely strong capabilities on optimization tasks, often outperforming skilled humans. Generally, these tasks have a clearly defined goal that is cheaply checkable. Usually the solution has few required criteria or constraints other than those expressed by some score, and often this score is observable to the agent.

Section 3.A.1 describes evidence that models outperform our humans on our hard but well-defined coding and optimization tasks. Section 3.A.2 describes model performance relative to top experts in places where we have the data to do so.

Appendix O: Qualitative and External Evidence on Models’ Strengths and Limitations also describes external evidence that model strengths in this area extend beyond just benchmarks.

3.A.1 Models often match or outperform skilled humans on coding problems and metric optimization with strong feedback signals

We see strong performance from models on TH1.1, our old (primarily verifiable) sets of tasks, as well as on the verifiable portion of our TH2 tasks. Current public frontier models have an estimated time horizon of up to 27 hours on the verifiable subset of our TH2 tasks.

We also found that current public frontier models were able to beat the human baseliners that we recruited on the 3 tasks we attempted to acquire human baselines for. Since we want to measure whether models are able to replace AI developer employees, we aimed to acquire baseliners of a similar skill level. We recruited people with specialist knowledge in the task domain. For instance, for Train a Program our best external baseliner is a former International Grandmaster on Codeforces and won the 2020 Kaggle ARC-AGI competition. For Minecraft Bot and Reverse Conway, our best-performing baseliners were somewhat less credentialed, but had compensatory specialist knowledge or experience. We paid $200-350/hr and spent a total of roughly $90k for 7 attempted baselines.

Overall, we are uncertain about the comparability of our baseliners to AI R&D lab employees. We would guess that our baseliners’ skill within the task domain probably didn’t match the level of skill that typical frontier AI researchers have within their own domain. On the one hand, our baseliners were experienced, and often had specific experience relevant to the task. On the other hand, AI developers offer very high compensation, and have recruiting teams optimizing for finding highly talented individuals. Additionally, our tasks required dozens of hours of commitment, which plausibly selected against potential participants with full-time employment, and may have led to some adverse selection effects. For more details on human baselines see Appendix A: Difficulty Estimation and Baselining.

Various other work also observes high capability on these kinds of tasks. For instance, on MirrorCode, where agents reimplement software by checking against a full test suite, Opus 5.5 solves 77% of medium to large programs that would take human experts weeks to months to do on their own. GPT-6 Astra recently scored 98% on FrontierMath Tier 4, a benchmark of research-level math problems designed to take a strong mathematician specializing in that domain 1-2 weeks.

3.A.2 Where we have more human data, models outperform most humans on verifiable optimization tasks, but do not always dominate top experts.

For two of our TH2 verifiable task families we have a larger pool of pre-existing human data to compare model performance against. On these two task families, we observe AI agents had high percentile performance but were generally unable to beat the very top performing humans.

The first of these two task families was 15 Al Zimmermann contests, multi-month long optimization competitions. We find the median placement of the strongest models (Fable 5.1, Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra) is the 96th to 98th percentile of human participants (97 to 413 participants per contest). We estimate that a dedicated human participant might spend between 90 hr and 180 hr over the course of the competition. We think the top of the human participant pool is quite strong.⁠24 In the median contest, roughly 4 humans beat the strongest models we tested, see Figure 3(a).

The second of these two task families was Budget NanoGPT speedrun, where we run the AI agent with a comparable amount of compute⁠25 to what human speedrunners typically use. Agent solutions (all starting from record #78) and human records aren't perfectly comparable, since each human record builds on previous records. However, interviews with contributors suggest that each human record took a similar amount of labor and resources, comparable to one of our agent runs. So we compare each agent run’s speedup to the speedup of individual human records, and believe this comparison is rough but still informative. Also note that recent "human records" can be AI-assisted (sometimes heavily so), and that we only include human records set up to Aug 2026.

On Budget NanoGPT speedrun, we find that fully autonomous AI agents outperform many competent speedrunners, achieving better speedups whilst costing less. However, top human contributors still can reach much larger speedups than AI agents, see Figure 3(b). The best AI agent run (of 2 to 5 runs per model) reached a 15.7% speedup over our starting record in 48 hours. The author of a recent unusually strong record (#92), with AI assistance for coding, estimated spending about 60 hours for a 41% speedup over the previous record.

For more detail on each of the results see Appendix K: Al Zimmermann's Programming Contests and Appendix L: Budget NanoGPT.

3.B AI R&D Acceleration Relevant Capabilities Limitations

In this section we present evidence that current public frontier models have capability limitations that may prevent them from drastically accelerating AI R&D via automating research work.

In Section 3.B.1, we introduce Lifecycle Cost, a rough proxy for how costly a task would be to build and use as an RL training environment. We find that on our dataset, models perform worse on tasks with higher Lifecycle Cost, even when controlling for task length.

Section 3.B.2 describes evidence of limitations related to performing well on tasks where it is difficult to get clear empirical feedback about the quality of the solution. In these tasks, agents need to create their own metrics of quality, handle tradeoffs, or exercise judgment.

Appendix O: Qualitative and External Evidence on Models’ Strengths and Limitations also describes external evidence on weaknesses in the context of AI-related research.

3.B.1 On our dataset, models perform worse on tasks that we estimate are costlier to turn into RL training environments, even after controlling for task length.

As discussed in Section 1, the evals that are METR’s current primary way of measuring model capabilities are likely to have much in common with the RL environments that companies use to train their models. This is because both METR and AI developers want to be able to cheaply, easily capture signals about model capabilities. As RL has become a larger share of training, we have become more concerned that this overlap causes systematic measurement issues.

To try to quantify how difficult a task is to make into an RL environment, we created a very rough proxy measure which we call Lifecycle Cost. This is the cost for us to develop a containerized environment for the task, and then to run and score the task 100 times.⁠26 The goal with this quantity is not to reflect the ‘real cost’ of RL environments per se, but rather to have some rough quantitative metric of relative costliness.

Table 1 gives examples of properties that we have found make it more expensive to create highly realistic tasks, which Lifecycle Cost aims to capture.

Table 1: Lifecycle cost factors
Cost factorExample
Manual scoringIn many cases, programmatic scoring would overly constrain the task, and models may be unable to properly judge the quality of the output. In these cases, we can use humans to score outputs. This is especially costly when the human needs to be an expert. Examples include Making METR Tasks and Sunlight.⁠27
Compute-per-runSome of our tasks involve using GPUs or other compute infrastructure, which has an associated cost. In TH2, both Budget NanoGPT and Train a Program use GPUs.
Serial timeEvaluations that take a long time to complete are generally more difficult to construct and run. Long tasks tend to be more prone to technical issues and length often compounds other cost factors, such as monitoring cost.⁠28 Our longest evaluations in TH2 verifiable tasks are run for 48 hours.
Token costsTasks where models use more tokens are costlier to run. The token cost of an evaluation is often (but not always) correlated with the serial time needed to run the task.⁠29
Action monitoringSometimes models behave in ways that can cause real world harm. METR monitors certain high-risk evaluations to prevent this. This increases token costs, and often requires human researcher time for approvals. Further details about METR’s monitoring setup are available in this post.
Environment complexityEnvironments which offer more components or opportunities for interaction are generally harder to make. It is therefore often desirable to ‘import’ a complex environment from some existing artifact (e.g. a game, or a dataset). The process of importing the task whilst ensuring it is fair is generally harder for more complex artifacts as there are more opportunities for bugs, and increased labor cost of QA. Another obvious route for introducing environment complexity is allowing the evaluation to interact with the ‘real world’, which we discuss below.
Interaction with the real worldAllowing internet access, and particularly interacting with the real world (e.g. making money on some prediction market) is a potential way to incorporate lots of realistic complexity into a task. However, real world interaction can increase Lifecycle Cost by increasing monitoring requirements and QA costs associated with guarding against unintended solutions. These tasks also typically have more issues with noise (lack of reproducibility), have shorter lifetimes (the internet and world change over time), and involve resources that require some evaluator overhead to manage (e.g. cryptocurrency, credit cards, accounts).
Implicit generalizability requirementsIn real work, there is often some gap between what measures are readily available to iterate against, and the ultimate requirements applicable to the solution. For example, it is easier to iterate against the currently known feature requirements of an application, and its related tests, than to choose an architecture which will be easy to adapt to future requirements and preclude harder-to-notice failure modes. Introducing these generalization requirements in a fair way usually requires giving agents more context on the wider situation in which its solution will be used, which takes additional development time. Additionally, the ultimate requirements on a solution are often most naturally exercised on longer timescales, which can make scoring harder.⁠30
Making the task robust to reward hacking, and scoring sensitive to the capabilities of interestIt is often the case that when we first make a task, oversights in task design mean that agents can score highly via methods that do not test the capability of interest. Despite many of our tasks containing instructions to follow the intended spirit of the evaluation, we still find that we need to put in a lot of ‘hardening’ effort to prevent reward hacking or otherwise ‘cheesy’ solutions. This increases implementation and QA costs. This also increases running costs, since we typically check our runs for reward hacking each time they are run.⁠31
Use of expertsSome tasks require specialist knowledge and time to create, run or score. For example, datasets created by experts (LMCA), and evaluations that require expert scoring (Making METR Tasks, Sunlight).
Human interaction during the runTasks which require human participation (e.g. in a game, or in an adversarial cyber challenge). Wall clock, coordination, and labor costs are significant for such tasks.⁠32 (None of our TH2 tasks include this).

We estimate Lifecycle Cost for our tasks with an LLM judge (Opus 5.5). This judge is given the task code (including the README, instructions, and scoring), and asked to estimate various costs associated with the task like e.g. development, QA, and scoring costs. We also amortize shared development costs over families of similar tasks. We iterated on the prompt for the judge such that the judge’s estimates roughly matched our own for about 10 tasks across domains and cost ranges.⁠33 While we expect our prompt to generalize reasonably well to tasks outside ours, some choices like which phases we break cost into, how much we assume AI speeds up task development, labor rates, etc. reflect our own experience. For full details of how we estimate Lifecycle Cost see Appendix F: Estimating Lifecycle Cost for Tasks.

We think there are various limitations to Lifecycle Cost as a proxy for ‘difficulty to make a suitable RL environment’, and also to using ‘difficulty to make a suitable RL environment’ as a predictor of model performance broadly:

  • RL environment creation difficulty doesn’t account for differing demand in different capability domains. For example, we might assume that AI developers are more interested in creating models that are good at coding, but much less interested in making models good at deception and sabotage.
  • RL environment creation difficulty doesn’t account for cross-task generalization gains, or the amount / degree of task coverage needed for generalization within a domain.
  • Our LLM judge is calibrated to our own estimates of Lifecycle Cost, which are informed by METR’s constraints. Labs are likely to have different constraints to us.
  • Costs are likely to change as model capabilities improve. What is costly today may not be costly in a year, or a few months. For instance, judging according to a rubric may have been costly to do in 2023, but model graded rubrics are common today. In Table 1 we give examples of factors that have, up to now, increased Lifecycle Cost for us. We are not claiming that these are fundamental barriers, or even that these will be relatively costly in the future.

Figure 5 shows a scatter plot of human time-to-complete vs Lifecycle Cost for our tasks. For TH2 tasks, Lifecycle Cost is estimated only for the highest score threshold. On our dataset, human time-to-complete and Lifecycle Cost are quite correlated (R2 = 0.82).⁠34

When we control for this correlation between time-to-complete and Lifecycle Cost we find that (for a given length) tasks with higher Lifecycle Cost tend to be somewhat more difficult than what would be predicted by their time-to-complete alone (R2 = 0.14), see Figure 6.⁠ 35⁠36

However, our data is noisy and could be affected by a few things. For example, many of our task times are estimates, and if they are systematically wrong, part of this effect could come from error in task time. That said, it’s somewhat reassuring that when we use models to estimate task time instead of human estimates/baseline, we also observe a similar trend. Another reason could be that this effect is driven primarily by a few high cost tasks. In the plot above, we account for this by filtering such outlier tasks. More ablations are in Appendix F: Estimating Lifecycle Cost for Tasks.

When we subset our tasks by high and low Lifecycle Cost, controlling for task length, and plot time horizons over release dates, we find that models perform worse on the higher Lifecycle Cost subset of our tasks, see Figure 7. On average, the higher Lifecycle Cost subset is 2.9x as expensive as the lower Lifecycle Cost subset, and models perform 2.9x worse in terms of time horizon, equivalent to 1.5 doublings.

Interestingly, compared to the lower lifecycle cost subset, model performance on the higher lifecycle cost subset is closer to performance reported by OpenAI on their own internal research usage.⁠37 We don’t have many task distributions to compare here, but we think this is at least an encouraging sign about our ability to create evaluations that are directionally more predictive of performance on ‘actual’ AI R&D tasks. It also seems like some (weak) evidence in favor of lifecycle cost being correlated with factors of difficulty that make ‘real’ AI R&D internal research tasks more difficult for models than some kinds of evaluations.

On our data, doubling times on the higher cost subset are similar to (and very slightly shorter than) doubling times on the lower cost subset. However, our task set spans a restricted range of relative lifecycle cost so it is possible that for sufficiently high relative lifecycle cost doubling times are longer (or alternatively, are shorter).

If we were to take seriously that lifecycle cost was a good proxy for realism, then differences in doubling times would be consequential for forecasting the impact of AI systems:

If higher lifecycle cost tasks actually have a slower growth rate, and relatively high lifecycle cost tasks are required, then naive extrapolation could predict that drastic AI R&D acceleration happens much later.

On the other hand, if there was no difference in growth rate at different (relative) lifecycle costs, then this would imply that each doubling in relative lifecycle cost simply delays trends by some number of months. On our dataset, a doubling in lifecycle cost for a given length of tasks results in model performance reaching 50% success rate approximately 4.6 months later.⁠ 38 Note that we are highly uncertain about the lifecycle cost of tasks required to drastically accelerate AI R&D. They could potentially be many doublings more expensive than the typical task in our dataset.⁠39

Overall, we remain uncertain about the relative growth rates of high lifecycle cost vs low lifecycle cost tasks. For more information see Appendix F: Estimating Lifecycle Cost for Tasks.

Using a similar methodology to time horizon, we also estimated Lifecycle Cost horizon as the model-estimated lifecycle cost at which agents have a 50% chance to succeed at the task. We find that the lifecycle cost horizon of models is also steadily improving, though with a longer doubling time compared to the time horizon trend. See Appendix F: Estimating Lifecycle Cost for Tasks for the lifecycle cost horizon trend (Figure 51) and corresponding logistic fits per model.

3.B.2 On domains where the ‘true’ quality of a solution is hard for the agent to determine due to prohibitively long feedback loops or requirements that the solution generalize, models appear to have worse judgment than human experts

A property we consider to be somewhat captured within lifecycle cost is the verifiability or ‘fuzziness’ of tasks. Tasks that are ‘fuzzier’ tend to be harder to automatically score, or more faithfully scored in ways that are too resource intense for a typical evaluation context. These ‘fuzzy’ tasks are typically taken to load on skills like ‘taste’ and ‘judgment’, and often include ‘research’ as a central example. We think that making a binary distinction between ‘fuzzy’ and ‘verifiable’ tasks can be somewhat misleading. Many tasks often categorized as ‘fuzzy’ are in fact verifiable in some way, given sufficient time to let the consequences of a given solution play out. For instance, making a ‘good app’ for some purpose might be considered a fuzzy task, but given enough time and exposure to use, we could eventually observe more objective and faithful measures of quality e.g. number of users or ratings. One way to operationalize this is in terms of the general costliness of assessing the quality of a solution in the long run.⁠40 Tasks where it is expensive to actually ‘play out’ the whole process to a point where long-run quality can be assessed (e.g. deciding a research agenda), are costlier-to-verify. We therefore expect these ‘fuzzier’ or ‘costlier-to-verify’ tasks to have higher lifecycle costs than typical for their length.

In this section we discuss evidence related to the ‘judgment’ capabilities of models and provide a few other examples of weakness related to managing one’s own feedback loops. As defined in Section 2, by judgment, we’re broadly referring to the ability to make good choices in the absence of clear and fast empirical feedback. This is often driven by strong argument and reasoning, or instilled via experience and expressed through the careful application of learned heuristics. In practice, this can short circuit otherwise necessary and expensive feedback loops. As a result, ‘judgment’ is particularly helpful on ‘costlier-to-verify’ tasks.

It is unclear to us the degree to which drastic acceleration of AI R&D will require judgment or related capabilities. We discuss this further in Section 5. However, we would consider weakness in ‘judgment’ and related capabilities to be reasonable candidates for what most delays drastic AI R&D acceleration.

As one measure of these domains, we ran LMCA,⁠ 41 a benchmark where models need to rate critiques of philosophical arguments. Models have shown steady improvement, and many have agreement above the estimated agreement level of expert humans who spent 5 minutes on the task, but all public models are still below the estimated agreement level of expert humans spending 15 minutes.⁠42

AI agents now build borderline-useful evaluation tasks, but not yet ones we consider especially informative

We made a task designed to test model ability to automate making our evaluations. The agent is asked to create a new task for our evaluation suite which is informative for METR’s assessment of model capabilities. None of the tasks agents produced were sufficiently useful to us to warrant inclusion in this post.⁠43

The models we tested generally favored making automatically scored optimization tasks, even when (in follow-up experiments) instructed to make tasks where "success is sensitive to decisions which have poor short term feedback." Agents did not independently try to improve their understanding of what tasks METR would find most useful, despite internet access. We did not observe agents produce novel threat modeling, or construct tasks based on particularly insightful hypotheses about model capabilities.⁠44

AI judges are overgenerous when judging tasks made by models stronger than themselves

We observed related weaknesses when models are asked to judge the quality of other model-made tasks. When asked to score tasks according to how useful they’d be for us, their agreement with our own ratings depends on the relative strength of the judge model and the task-creator model.

Frontier models show high agreement with our own ratings when the task is made by a weaker model than themselves, but give inappropriately high scores when the task is made by a stronger, or similarly strong, model, see Figure 9.⁠ 45 Opus 5.5, the judge that had the highest agreement with our human scorer, scores Astra’s tasks on average about 0.2 (out of 1) more highly than our human.⁠46

The most straightforward explanation for this is that models have poor judgment about what makes a good task. This poor judgment effectively caps the model’s performance during the task itself, because the model only has its own judgment to optimize against. With the end result being that when used as judges they systematically overestimate the quality of their own solutions, and those of stronger models. Another possibility is that strong models exhibit behaviors that cause weaker judges to overestimate their solutions.⁠47

Some caveats to our judgment related results:

Firstly, we think it is somewhat likely that models are underelicited on these kinds of problems. For example, in our setting and similar settings⁠48, we observe models significantly underutilize their provided token limits and affordances.

Secondly, we do see current public frontier models displaying good judgment (of unclear generality) on some ML tasks. For instance we observe that on days-to-weeks long ML evaluations with programmatic scoring, models are able to beat our human baseliners. This includes tasks that we explicitly designed to be novel, and that we do not believe to have been trained on. In related work, P-Zero Research also finds strong performance on TasteVal, which measures the compute efficiency of agents' experiment choices in up to 120hr long verifiable ML optimization tasks.⁠49 And this performance seems to persist under resource limits matched to those that humans work under (as in TasteVal or our Budget NanoGPT results). However, it seems plausible to us that the judgment displayed by models on these tasks is in some sense less general than the judgment displayed by humans. It also seems plausible that there is no fundamental difference.

3.C AI R&D Acceleration Relevant Capabilities Trends

3.C.1 Across TH1.1 and TH2 we find that capabilities are doubling roughly every 3.6 months

Across our TH1.1 and TH2 tasks, we find that time horizons are doubling roughly every 3.61 months (95% CI 2.08-4.94), or 110 days, see Figure 10. This is similar to the previous TH1.1 doubling trend of 105 days.⁠50 On these tasks, naive extrapolation would imply that models reach a 50% time horizon equal to the longest score threshold on our task set (200 hours) in July 2027.

In Appendix B: Time Horizon Diagnostics, we see similar results when noising our baseline estimates and when using model estimated times instead of our default time estimates.⁠51

When we estimate separate trends for task subsets, we observe a faster doubling time on TH2 verifiable tasks than on TH1.1 tasks. Our data is noisy, but for TH2 (verifiable) we see a doubling time of 1.65 months (95% CI 0.79 to 3.99), see Figure 11. This trend is faster than the combined TH1.1 + TH2 doubling time.⁠52

This faster doubling time could be taken as evidence of an acceleration in model capabilities in 2026 (at least as measured by time horizon, on these tasks). However, we think it is worth noting that we already observe similarly quick doubling times on MirrorCode in the second half of 2025 (another, easier, verifiable distribution of tasks), when TH1.1 was still growing at a slower rate.

We are not sure what the cause of the faster doubling time on TH2 (verifiable) and MirrorCode is. One possibility is a general acceleration in capabilities measured by time horizon, as mentioned above. In principle, such an acceleration could be hidden in the combined set by an artificial deceleration from switching over to TH2, which is generally a harder set of tasks at a fixed time horizon.⁠53 Some other possibilities include these distributions having data over a more restricted date range, or that tasks involved have properties that make them easier to improve at quickly, or combinations of these possibilities.

One possibility that we find plausible is that doubling times may tend to be shorter on less diverse sets of tasks. Essentially, when a set of tasks are very similar to one another, longer tasks might not be that much more difficult (e.g. if it is mostly more of the ‘same kind’ of work). Or alternatively stated, once models have learnt the main skills needed to do a kind of task, they are relatively quickly able to do longer versions of that kind of task. However, across a varied set of tasks, longer tasks are more likely to include different or multiple different kinds of work, such that models may overall tend to acquire the necessary abilities later. So relative lack of diversity may be a contributing factor towards MirrorCode and TH2 (verifiable)’s shorter doubling times.⁠54

Another possibility is that our selection for difficult tasks during development contributes to TH2 (verifiable) having a shorter doubling time. However, we would be somewhat surprised if this was the majority of the reason for the faster doubling time of TH2 (verifiable), since we e.g. kept tasks that were saturated during the suite’s development.

We also note that recently published data from OpenAI seems to imply a doubling time of 3.8 months when using their models on their own internal research tasks, which is similar to the 3.6 month doubling time we find over TH1.1 and TH2.⁠55

3.C.2 We do not observe a strong acceleration in capability across public frontier models, as measured by METR Capability Index (MCI) on our tasks. However, we do see uncertain evidence of developer-specific trend changes in some conditions.

If AI R&D were already being substantially sped up, we might expect to observe an acceleration (i.e., a positive change in growth rates) in the capability of models over time (even if they continued to have a jagged profile). We see rapid progress on tasks when measured in terms of time horizons. Given the uncertainty we have about whether ‘task length’⁠56 is a good metric of task difficulty, and the value of our time estimates, we also analyzed our data with MCI, an IRT model.

When we fit our MCI model (similar to metrics like Epoch's ECI, and Anthropic’s AECI) we do not observe an obvious acceleration over all public frontier models, see Figure 12. We believe this result provides moderate evidence against a general deceleration in model capabilities and weak to moderate evidence against a general acceleration over all the models we tested.

However, if we instead calibrate MCI and fit trends separately for each AI developer, we do observe some slope changes (i.e., increasing capability growth rates) in certain conditions. We find that slope changes on developer-specific MCI are generally comparable to those presented in METR’s Independent Assessment of AI R&D Acceleration at Anthropic in October 2026 and recent Anthropic system cards in the conditions that we are most able to reproduce, i.e. on public Anthropic models up to Opus 5.5. We are uncertain about the validity of these slope changes, since we find they are quite sensitive to various methodological choices. We discuss these AI developer specific slope changes and sensitivity to methodological choices in Appendix H: Changes in Capability Growth Rates.

Note that, when using this kind of IRT methodology to detect acceleration, we think it is worth bearing in mind some reasons that this method may ‘miss’ an actual acceleration. Firstly, it is possible that developers could be delaying public releases for longer as models get more capable,⁠ 57 which could cause us to not detect an acceleration. Secondly, MCI always has more uncertainty in the ability of the most capable AI model,⁠58 which we expect to delay our ability to detect an actual acceleration.

This methodology is also only able to detect acceleration in overall ability as measured by MCI, rather than acceleration caused by AI specifically. We do not know what the counterfactual trend would have been had AI developers not been using AI to help with their research. It’s entirely possible that without AI we would see a decelerating trend, and that AI use has in fact accelerated trends to the current level.

For more details on our methodology, see Appendix C: MCI Modelling Methodology, for robustness checks see Appendix E: MCI Robustness Checks, for additional discussion of limitations of our model and related IRT models, see Appendix D: Limitations of IRT.

4 Robustness Checks

We did various robustness checks for our evaluation results:

To check that our token budgets for runs were large enough we looked at how performance scales with increasing tokens. For each task, we aimed to set a token budget where performance plateaus, though this was not always achieved. For more details, see Appendix I: Inference Scaling.

We also checked for reward hacks. Runs with reward hacks were assigned a score of zero. To do this we manually looked through a subset of traces, and ran model scanners over the runs (sometimes with instructions on what to look for by task, informed by reading through the subset). Most of the reward hacking we found was by near-frontier agents,⁠59 such as GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, Fable 5, Fable 5.1, and Opus 5.5. For more details see Appendix G: Data Integrity and Reward Hacking and Appendix G: We find that models still often engage in reward hacking.

We excluded runs where classifiers blocked generation, or the model itself refused. This was quite rare and affected about 36 runs across both TH2 and TH1.1. We also performed some preliminary checks for sandbagging, see Appendix G: Refusals and Sandbagging for more.

Finally, we experimented with using “swarms” rather than our regular scaffold. We generally compared swarm runs to best-of-k (which typically performs better than a single very long serial run on these kinds of tasks). Though our data is noisy, we found that swarm scaffolds seem to increase performance over best-of-k controls on tasks with a clear metric to optimize. In other tasks, we found unclear or negative effects. In particular, we found that large swarms seemed harmful to performance when the task required creating a proxy metric for themselves. For more detail see Appendix J: Swarm Experiments.

These results lead us to believe that we may be somewhat undereliciting performance on our clean verifiable optimization tasks due to not using swarms. We expect that not using swarms on our other tasks is not a contributor to underelicitation. We generally found that swarms were very expensive to run; our swarm experiments used approximately $500k in tokens.

5 Discussion

As model capabilities have improved, some methods and evaluations that we have previously used to upper bound risk have become less useful, or obsolete. Current public models now have very strong performance on well-scoped ML, cyber, and coding problems. Therefore, it is now more difficult to bound risk by pointing to some easy-to-measure and broad incapability.

Instead, our understanding of risk from model capabilities is more dependent on questions related to the specific details of model capability profiles, and facts about the world. We do not feel that we currently have good answers to many of the most important questions here. For instance:

  • How much stronger are model capabilities on easier (or less-costly) to verify tasks, compared to harder (or costlier) to verify tasks? On our dataset, we observe weaker performance on the higher relative lifecycle cost subset of our tasks. However, the link between lifecycle cost and some notion of ‘realism’ remains very unclear, and we do not know if its associations with worse model performance apply to other task distributions.
  • Are models getting better at harder-to-verify tasks more slowly? If so, by how much? On our dataset, we see similar doubling times on the high and low relative Lifecycle Cost subsets of our tasks. However, we could imagine that the trend could be different on tasks with much higher lifecycle cost, or that were otherwise more realistic.
  • What capabilities are even necessary for drastic AI R&D acceleration? In this post we have largely avoided making claims about what is necessary and sufficient for drastic AI R&D acceleration, but this is a major source of uncertainty for us. It may in fact be possible to drastically accelerate AI R&D via easy-to-verify tasks alone. As a result, we think it is prudent to consider the possibility that AI may be able to drastically accelerate AI R&D before we see good performance on many ‘realistic’ tasks that may be necessary for the automation of ‘normal’ jobs. At the very least, AI developers are putting a great deal of active effort into getting their AIs to automate their own research,⁠60 which we might expect to bring forward automation relative to AI R&D’s ‘natural’ difficulty.

A Difficulty Estimation and Baselining

Our time horizon results, along with analyses that control for task length, depend on estimates of how long it would take a skilled human to reach a given score on a task.

For most tasks in our original time horizon suite, we measured this directly by having skilled professionals attempt the same tasks we evaluate models on, under the same conditions. We call these attempts human baselines.

Because our new tasks are far harder than those in our original suite, this was no longer feasible. It was often difficult to find qualified people who could commit the time, the cost of collecting these baselines was much higher, and models often outperformed even the experts we did recruit.

For tasks in the new suite, we use researchers’ estimates of the time needed to reach a range of scores. For most of the tasks we have evidence from humans doing the same or similar tasks, but usually not in a dedicated, self-contained setup. We ran formal human baselines for three of our twelve task families, though for two of them we also relied on other sources of data and on extrapolation, as we describe below.

The rest of this section covers our protocol for the formal baselines, how we recruited baseliners, and their results. We then explain how we produced human time estimates for each task.

Baselining Protocol

Each baseliner worked in a container identical to the one an AI agent is given, with the same information and resources. We avoided giving the baseliners guidance beyond what agents receive, so that humans didn't have an unfair advantage.

Baseliners were allowed to use Sonnet 5 for implementation, but we asked them not to rely on it for high-level decisions. This differs from TH1, where baseliners mostly couldn’t use AI.⁠61 We allowed AI assistance partly because many people now use AI in their work, and some people didn’t want to do baselines without it because the work would be too tedious. Another reason is that letting baseliners use AI makes baselines cheaper to collect, as they can spend more of their time on difficult work that requires their expertise and less on routine work. We didn’t allow access to a frontier model to ensure that human baselines were still mostly driven by human performance.

We also let baseliners access the internet.

We didn’t give baseliners hard time limits, though we did share a rough estimate of how long we expected the task to take. We also didn’t give them target scores or an indication of what strong performance would look like. Throughout the baseline, we periodically checked their progress and decided whether to have them continue.

Baseliners were paid $200–350/hr. We didn't use bonuses for reaching performance milestones. However, the progress check-ins meant baseliners who wanted to keep working had some incentive to perform well.

Recruiting Baseliners

With these baselines, we aimed to compare model capabilities with those of AI R&D experts, ideally people working at frontier AI companies. Of the three tasks we baselined, only Train a Program is directly an AI R&D task. The other two test skills we think are closely related to AI R&D. For Train a Program, we aimed to recruit people with skills comparable to AI company researchers. For the other tasks, we looked for people with a similar level of expertise in the relevant domain. This made recruiting challenging, as these people often have demanding, well-paid jobs, so they may be too busy to complete a baseline.

Here is some background on the baseliners for each of the three formally baselined tasks:

  • Reverse Conway. We had two baseliners for this task. One was on the second-place team in a closely related Kaggle competition, and the other was a PhD student working on SAT solving.
  • Minecraft Bot. We had one baseliner for this task, who had previous professional experience building bots for Minecraft and League of Legends.
  • Train a Program. We had four baseliners for this task. They all had backgrounds that made us expect them to do well, such as top results in programming or AI competitions or prior ML research. The baseliner with the highest score was a former International Grandmaster on Codeforces and the winner of the 2020 Kaggle ARC-AGI competition.

Overall, we’d weakly guess our best baseliners would do better on these tasks than a typical technical employee at a frontier AI company (since they have more relevant expertise), but they’d probably do worse than a typical frontier AI researcher in their own research area.

Baseline Results

On all three tasks we formally baselined, the best model outperformed every human baseliner.

That said, we think some aspects of our tasks are artificially unfriendly to humans, in ways that don’t reflect what it’s like to do this kind of work in more natural settings. This might contribute to humans underperforming models, since models are well-adapted to containerized task environments in general. One example is that on some tasks, a mistaken assumption about the setup or scoring can leave a baseliner with a much worse score, even if their underlying work is otherwise good. This seems to be less of a problem for models, likely because extensive RL training has made them very good at avoiding mistaken assumptions about these kinds of task environments. In future research, we think it might be worth giving humans some amount of feedback as they’re going.

Although models outperformed our baseliners on all three of these tasks, humans did better on some tasks not included in the final TH2 suite. On two social deduction game tasks, humans scored higher or the same as the models. On Sunlight, a manually scored task where models design an indoor setup to recreate sunlight and write a report on its optical properties, we think most people would prefer the human-written report, though agents’ submissions are better in other ways.⁠62

C MCI Modelling Methodology

Our goal with METR Capability Index (MCI) is to estimate AI capabilities and task difficulty along a single axis, in a similar spirit to the ECI and AECI. For these purposes, Item Response Theory has a few benefits, including allowing us to evaluate agents only on a subset of questions, accounting for differences in how difficult and discriminatory tasks are, and quantifying uncertainty in our capability estimates.

Data

First, we need to form a dataset of how an agent performed on various tasks. This is complicated somewhat by the fact that we have lots of closely related tasks, and sometimes have multiple agent attempts on a given task. Thus, we conceptualize our data in a hierarchical structure, where each hierarchy of data serves a different purpose in our IRT model:

  • families: these are related groups of tasks. For instance, we have the AZsPCs family where the tasks are the individual competitions. Our data has 113 families.
  • tasks: these are individual activities with goals the agents might pursue. Our data has 333⁠66 tasks, meaning an average of 2.9 tasks per family.
  • runs: We often evaluate an agent on a task multiple times. A run is an independent instance of an agent performing a task. Our data has 38,180 scored run-level observations, meaning an average of ~4.7 observations per evaluated agent-task pair.

The fundamental unit of analysis is a task, but we use runs to help more precisely estimate how an agent might do on a given task, and we use families to downweight tasks that are very similar.

Basic IRT Model

For each of our tasks, we define a scale in [0,1], where 0 corresponds to failure and 1 corresponds to full score⁠67, and fit a two parameter logistic for the score an agent m will get on a task t⁠:

pmt=σ(αt(θm−βt)),σ(x)=11+e−x

where pmt is the expected score agent m will get on task t⁠, αt is the task discrimination, βt is the task difficulty, and θm is the agent ability.

A difference of 0 between task difficulty, βt⁠, and agent ability, θm⁠, predicts that the agent will score 0.5 on that task. The slope parameter αt corresponds to how well a given task can distinguish between two agent abilities, in that a one unit increase in agent ability θm predicts an αt unit increase in the log score odds.

Task Dispersion

Since our task suite is very diverse, we want to downweight ‘noisy’ tasks where our observed data is not well fit by our model. So, we fit a noise parameter for each task, called the dispersion (φt⁠), which is a function of run-level deviance (dmtr⁠):

φt=∑m,rdmtrNt−2,dmtr=2[ymtrlog⁡ymtrpmt+(1−ymtr)log⁡1−ymtr1−pmt]

where ymtr is the observed score for a run of an agent on a task. Mathematically, dispersion is approximately the average of the difference in log-likelihood between the fitted curve and the observed curve. Intuitively, dispersion measures how much individual run outcomes depart from what the fitted curve predicts. Larger departures mean we treat the task as noisier and give it less weight.

Fitting the IRT Model

To fit our two-parameter logistic, we maximize the fractional-logit log-likelihood. To reflect certain features of our data, we introduce a few adjustments to the fit:

  • Downweighting of noisy tasks. We multiply by 1/φt⁠, which is the inverse of our task dispersion parameter, to downweight noisy tasks.
  • Partial downweighting on run count. We want more runs for a task to reflect increased confidence for a task, while not completely discounting tasks with only a few runs. So, we apply wmt=1/nmt⁠, where nmt is the number of scored runs for an evaluated agent-task pair.
  • Partial downweighting on family size. Since families often include similar tasks and we want our model to be robust to adding many tasks within a given family, we add family-level down-weighting, ft=1/qt⁠, where qt is the number of fitted tasks in task t⁠'s family.

Further, to help with model fitting, we add priors on our task discrimination and difficulties. In particular, our base priors are:

log⁡αt∣μ∼N(μ,32),βt∼N(0,102)

We scale each task's prior penalties by ftct⁠, where ct=∑mnmt∑mnmt⁠, the average run weight. We fit μ as the weighted mean log discrimination, ∑tftctlog⁡αt∑tftct⁠.

Our full objective thus becomes:

maxθ,α,β∑tftφt∑m,rwmt[ymtrlog⁡pmt+(1−ymtr)log⁡(1−pmt)]−∑tftct[(log⁡αt−μ)22⋅32+βt22⋅102]

The dispersion φt is not part of this maximization; we use double GLM, which allows parameters to have their own noise parameter. In particular, we alternate between fitting with φt held fixed and re-estimating φt from the fitted curves, until all the parameters stop changing. The dispersion φt is estimated from unweighted run deviances; neither family nor run weights enter its update.

We establish a scale for the ability and task difficulty axis by setting two anchor models: GPT-4o at -1, and Opus 4.6 at 0, and rescale the axis so that GPT-4o is at 0 and a one-unit change in ability is set equal to 633, which is the number of days between each model's release.

Confidence Intervals

To produce our confidence intervals, we do a hierarchical bootstrap over families and then runs. Each sampled copy of a family independently resamples within each model–task pair. We resample whole task families because related tasks may provide overlapping evidence about model capability. Resampling runs captures variation between repeated evaluations.

Differences from the ECI

Our analysis is similar in spirit to Epoch’s ECI, but we do have a few notable differences, including:

We have run-level data, and each run enters the fit as its own observation. The ECI fits one score per model-benchmark pair.

We have the structure of tasks being in families, whereas the ECI does not.

We downweight tasks according to the family size, so that certain families don’t dominate the analysis.

We estimate uncertainty using a hierarchical bootstrap over task families and runs, while the ECI reports posterior credible intervals.

D Limitations of IRT

IRT provides a useful way to summarize model performance, but the resulting ability estimates and trends should be interpreted with several caveats:

  • The ability axis reflects models’ patterns of strengths and weaknesses across tasks, rather than those of humans. In other words, this figure is not interchangeable with the original time horizons graphs – a linear trend on this axis may not necessarily correspond to linear growth in the human time required to complete tasks that the models can solve.
  • We think IRT methods will likely be a lagging indicator of acceleration. When the most capable models reliably succeed on tasks that distinguish weaker models, there may be few informative comparisons available to estimate the size of their ability advantage. Without regularization, this can lead to wide confidence intervals on their estimated abilities, making acceleration difficult to distinguish from statistical uncertainty. As a result, a substantial capability gain may only become clear retrospectively, once additional models or harder tasks provide more informative comparisons.
  • These trends do not account for internal models. It’s possible that internal models are far beyond the current frontier.
  • Results can be sensitive to the IRT modeling decisions. To account for this, we conduct a variety of robustness tests, and generally find similar trends (see IRT robustness checks in Appendix E: MCI Robustness Checks). We also purposefully do not place any priors on IRT ability, which can artificially lower confidence intervals.
  • Changes in task composition can also affect the trend if different capabilities improve at different rates. The distribution of tasks here also may not reflect the most important capabilities necessary for frontier lab uplift, or for AI risk scenarios. We do not find any multi-dimensionality in performance (see IRT robustness checks in Appendix E: MCI Robustness Checks). However, these checks cannot establish that our task suite captures all the capabilities relevant to AI R&D or AI risk.
  • Identifying acceleration is a product of good task-design. One consequence of moving away from human baselines is that, for some tasks, we no longer know for sure whether the highest possible scores are achievable. This makes it harder to measure progress, since models may approach an unknown performance ceiling and appear to improve more slowly, even when their scores are well below the line the task developer sets as the best possible performance. We believe that this hasn’t affected our tasks significantly thus far, but may be a bigger problem going forward.

Strength of penalties

We include prior penalties on αt and βt:

The αt penalty punishes tasks where the log discrimination is very far from the average log discrimination in the suite; βt penalty punishes tasks whose difficulty is far from the level at which Opus 4.6 would have a 50% chance of success. We find that changing the strength of the prior penalties changes our results very little.

Least Squares

In our main analysis, to fit our two-parameter logistic, we maximize the fractional-logit log-likelihood. We do this since fractional-logit gives greater weight to a given prediction error near 0 or 1 compared to least squares. This fits our noise structure better, because we believe that small score changes near 0 or 1 can represent meaningful capabilities differences that least squares gives little weight to.

In this robustness check, however, we try minimizing the least squares objective function. This involves a task-dispersion that looks like:

φt=∑m,rdmtrNt−2,dmtr=2(ymtr−pmt)2

And a full objective function that looks like:

maxθ,α,β−∑tftφt∑m,rwmt(ymtr−pmt)2−∑tftct[(log⁡αt−μ)22⋅32+βt22⋅102]

We find that changing to a least squares objective function changes our point estimates very little. It does slightly increase the ability scores for the pre-2025 models, and significantly widens their confidence intervals.

Sensitivity to Anchor

In our main analysis, we choose GPT-4o and Opus 4.6 to anchor our ability axis at -1 and 0, respectively (0 and 633 on the MCI scale the charts show). We use anchor models because our axes would be under-determined otherwise; different combinations of ability, task difficulty, and task discrimination would give the same predicted score. We choose these two anchor models in particular to get coverage across our suite: both of them have been evaluated across many of our TH1, TH1.1, and TH2 tasks.

In this robustness check, we see whether choosing two different anchor models would change our results.

We find that the choice of anchor models changes our point estimates very little. However, as is expected, the confidence intervals for models farther away from the anchor models generally increase; this is especially true if the two anchor models chosen do not have good coverage across our suite (e.g., Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.4 haven’t been evaluated much on the TH1 and TH1.1 suite, so using them as anchors results in larger confidence intervals for models that have only been evaluated on those suites).

Remove downweighting

In our task, we downweight noisy tasks with our dispersion parameter, partially downweight tasks by family membership which proxies for task similarity, and partially upweight tasks depending on how many runs it has.

Each of these weighting schemes is meant to handle our particular data structure. For instance, if we don't downweight tasks by family membership, certain categories of tasks (e.g., Slay the Spire and the Al Zimmermann contests) can contribute a disproportionate share of information constraining ability estimates. It happens to be that these families supply information that agrees with everything else, but weighting is a principled way to limit the dependence on those particular families. Note that there’s a sliding scale here; we can treat individual tasks as observations, which is like "no weighting" has occurred, or we can treat whole family units as observations, which is conceptually equivalent to weighting each task by 1/(number of tasks in the family). We instead take a middle-of-the-road approach, where we weight each task by 1/sqrt(number of tasks in the family). Similar logic applies to the weighting based on the number of runs. We think these are all reasonable choices given our data structure, but do complicate our model.

In this robustness check, we see how our results would change if all such weighting were removed. We find that point estimates don’t change much.

Link Function

In our main analysis, we use a logit link, which maps a model’s ability relative to a task’s difficulty to an expected score through a logistic curve. Logit is the standard choice for IRT.

In this robustness check, we try four other links, which each change a different part of the curve’s shape: probit, cauchit, complementary log-log, and log-log.

We find that changing the link function does result in very minor changes in our point estimates. Regardless, we feel that our choice is justified, considering that the logit link function is simple, standard, and suits our data well. Further, the point estimate changes don’t alter any of our conclusions.

Acceleration Detection

One concern is that our model might somehow artificially constrain the point estimates from showing acceleration. This robustness check shows that our model could show acceleration trends, if models had performed very well. We do this by multiplying the score odds by some factor, such that they stay between 0 and 1.

We find that our model is able to support "outlier" performance from models when we synthetically improve scores. However, this does not show that our task-design would necessarily allow for such trends, since we’re not entirely confident that a 100% is achievable on some of our tasks with no human baseliner.

F Estimating Lifecycle Cost for Tasks

We define Lifecycle Cost as the estimated cost of developing a task, then running and scoring it 100 times. We used an LLM-Judge (Opus 5.5) to estimate Lifecycle Cost given the task’s instructions, README, metadata and relevant source files, and a prompt as shown below.

With an iterative process, we roughly calibrated the model-generated estimates to our own for around 10 tasks from varying domains and cost ranges by tuning our pipeline and prompts.

We found that models were often poor at estimating total Lifecycle Cost for a task, but did better when provided a breakdown structure. We also found it better to make models estimate time when possible and procedurally convert that to cost accounting for considerations like labor rates, AI uplift, etc. We describe below more details on our methodology:

  • Breakdown cost. The model estimates the hours to develop a task in four phases: initial implementation, test runs and improvements, quality assurance, and difficulty estimation (including human baselines). We convert hours to cost at $150/hr rate for human labor.
  • Account for AI uplift. We divide each phase's cost by a rough speed-up from our own experience making tasks with AI: 4x for initial implementation, 2x for test runs and improvements, 2x for quality assurance, and 1x for difficulty estimation.
  • Include infrastructure cost to develop, run, and score. The model also estimates, in dollars, the infrastructure cost of development (e.g., tokens and GPUs for test runs) and the cost of running and scoring one attempt, including any manual scoring.
  • Amortize shared cost within task families. The model splits development into work that other tasks in the same family reuse, such as a shared harness or grader, and work specific to the task. We split the shared cost evenly over the family (e.g., MirrorCode).

We finally add up the parts above: a task's share of the shared development cost, its task-specific development cost, and 100 times the cost of one attempt to run and score. We do not use any totals reported by the model itself. We don’t feel strongly about our choice of 100 here, which was picked based on public speculation about RL environment usage (e.g., see this piece by Mechanize). We also still find worse model performance when lowering this to running and scoring just 10 times (see ablation below).

G Data Integrity and Reward Hacking

Reward Hacking Checks

Tasks can be flawed in a variety of ways. Features of the environment can make them trivially easy or impossible, or allow agents to succeed in ways that don't reflect the capabilities we intended to measure. We want to ensure that tasks are fair and achievable, so that we don't underestimate an agent's capabilities because a task was unreasonably difficult, or overestimate them because the agent exploited a bug or reward hacked the evaluation.

To ensure that our tasks are of high quality, we take the following steps across the development process:

Data integrity checks across the task development pipeline
Task ImplementationWhen tasks are initially developed, there’s a focus on good containerization practices (e.g., reasonable agent permissions, separate game-engine or hidden-test containers, etc.), and clear instruction writing (e.g., gesturing at the spirit of the task, explicit warding against obvious reward hacks, etc.). After an initial working implementation, the task developer undergoes an iterative process of putting an agent in a task, identifying what it tends to do, and shutting down reward hacking opportunities or bugs in the code.
Quality Assurance PhaseThe initial implementation will be handed off to another member of the team, who will both conduct a code review and an environment quality check. In the environment quality check, the human team member will act as the agent and attempt the task, with a special emphasis on trying to identify problems with the environment. Any problems are passed back to the task developer, who will fix them and iterate from there.
Official RunsAfter task development is completed, we’ll set off runs with a pre-determined set of agents, which gives us a list of "official runs" set aside for analysis. At this point, we do yet another reward hacking pass, where we see how many runs are invalidated either by agent reward hacking or by the task being overly broken. Another team member will first read the task instructions and look through select transcripts for notable trends in agent behavior. Then, they’ll generate a list of potential reward hacks or issues to look through, and have a scanner agent work through the transcripts to pick up any examples. This, too, is an iterative process, where each scan might generate new concerns that’ll warrant a new scan. This potential reward hacking list is peer-reviewed by yet another team member, who synthesizes a final set of runs that need to get cut or re-run for the analysis.

We’d estimate that at least 10% of human effort across the entire task development process is explicitly focused on preventing reward hacking. Further, we anticipate that this effort will only have to increase as time goes on, since we’ve found that increasingly competent agents will engage in increasingly niche ways of exploiting the task environment.

At the end of the process, we still found that ~10 runs (<0.1%) of our "official runs" had enough reward hacking that we set the run’s score to zero.

We find that models still often engage in reward hacking

We find many examples of AI agents reward hacking our evaluations, which we define as taking actions that increase their scores without fulfilling the intended tasks. We focus here on the TH2 tasks, and discuss some general trends in agent behavior.

Agents investigate the environment for shortcuts that bypass the intended task. For example, we’ve found agents attempting to access human-labeled datasets to obtain answers in a conceptual reasoning task, downloading existing bots to play Slay the Spire on their behalf, and checking for cheat codes in order to bypass challenges in Minecraft.

Refusals and Sandbagging

LLM providers have introduced monitoring systems and trained models to refuse certain requests relating to cybersecurity and AI R&D. While refusals are useful as safety measures, they also make evaluating model capabilities in these domains more challenging. We have found that, even with CVP and Daybreak models, a handful of our tasks hit very high rates of refusals. Some refusals don’t come in the form of a provider-level classifier trigger, but rather from the model itself, which can be more difficult to detect. Additionally there remains the possibility that models deliberately "sandbag" and underperform on some tasks, e.g. AI R&D or alignment-related tasks, for reasons besides safety.

We scan for these potential concerns using a combination of techniques:

  • Keyword and regex searches for words and phrases we expect might indicate refusals or sandbagging, which are then investigated by an individual agent.
  • Full-transcript LLM scans, where a transcript is split into several chunks, each chunk fed through a single model call to evaluate it for concerns, and any flagged results are aggregated and judged by more capable models.
  • Agentic LLM scans, where a separate agent with shell access is directed at each transcript and told to find concerns by understanding and searching through the transcript as a whole, rather than prescribing an access pattern. Results are flagged to a more capable agent, which itself can choose to look into more details or spin up more searches as necessary.

These techniques found a wide variety of refusals, but no meaningful cases of sandbagging in the sense we intend it. Most cases where a model explicitly verbalized related thoughts were small models who had plateaued on a task and attempted to give up. In one case, GPT-5.5 chose to submit early due to concerns about spending too much money on tokens. We expect that these scans would have caught easy-to-detect or explicitly verbalized decisions to sandbag, but not unverbalized or sophisticated sandbagging. However, combined with the evidence that we see significantly increasing scores across model generations in separate families (i.e., no sudden plateaus), we feel reasonably confident our results are unaffected.

Across all analyses, we drop observations in which models refused to engage in the task, or in which models were blocked from further action by provider-level monitors.

J Swarm Experiments

Overview

For several of our tasks, we ran small experiments with swarms, or collections of agents working together towards a common goal without a clear leader or subagent structure. These are of particular interest due to their recent emergence in real-world incidents.

The performance characteristics of small-scale swarms have been studied recently by other organizations, and our results are largely in line with others. We find that in some tasks and for some models, swarms of agents can consistently outperform a best-of-k control with matched total token spend. However other tasks are downlifted, there’s significant sensitivity to prompting and organizational structure, and there doesn’t appear to be a clean relationship between uplift and swarm size (or "swarm scaling law").

The tasks most uplifted appear to be verifiable and amenable to decomposition. When models had easy and quick ways to tell whether a given contribution or direction was good, and when chunks of independent work tended to build on each other, they could leapfrog ahead of parallel independent explorations (best-of-k).

On the other hand, tasks where models had to determine their own methods of verification without a ground truth tended to lead to propagating incorrect assumptions; further, tasks involving very long feedback loops, very limited resources, or interaction channels mostly resulted in coordination overhead.

While much smaller-scale than some "in-the-wild" swarms, these results seem to point towards performance improvements acting more like an amplification of a model's existing strengths rather than allowing for qualitatively new capabilities. They can do better via cooperation on highly verifiable tasks with immediate feedback, but tend towards overcommunication, propagating bad assumptions without proper skepticism, and are highly sensitive to initial conditions. It seems unlikely that swarms alone would allow for advances in unverifiable taste-loaded research capabilities that would otherwise be far outside the reach of any individual model with equivalent token spend.

Notably, because of budget constraints, we tested different swarm configurations somewhat unevenly, without systematically varying both team size and sample count. This means that many of the patterns we observe remain suggestive, and could change under different agent team sizes or different models.

Methodology

Our setup for these experiments was a straightforward forum Inspect agent scaffold, where tools are given to the agent to post threads and posts to a shared messageboard, read posts from others, catch up and sleep/wait on threads, etc. For every swarm of size k we compared to a best-of-k control, where we run the same number of agents in parallel and take the best score. We also experiment with some very long-running high token budget single agents, but find they plateau too early and produce worse performance than best-of-k, and so don’t make for useful controls.

Results

Tasks that are verifiable and amenable to decomposition often experience swarm uplift.

The only task in our suite that experienced swarm uplift relatively consistently was nanoGPT, in which agents were tasked to optimize a language-model training script based on the nanoGPT speedrun.

For this task, submissions are independent of one another and agents see the exact grade for each submission as they went, and they could submit repeatedly within a fixed budget. These task attributes seem particularly conducive to swarms being an uplift: agents can work independently while drawing on shared insights and breakthroughs to improve their own solutions.

Notably, we were only able to run a few swarm configurations for this task due to budget constraints, making it unclear whether we would see comparable results at very large swarm numbers.

Slay the Spire

Slay the Spire is a somewhat unique environment for swarms in that there is a stateful external environment with a very limited bandwidth channel. To avoid obvious failure modes, we included prompting to select a single driver agent who would send all actual gameplay commands, but coordinated with other agents to make decisions.

In this setting, having multiple agents sometimes helped and sometimes impeded progress. In particular, teammates sometimes encouraged obvious misplays or propagated misinformation. On the other hand, the increased scrutiny over each decision helped reduce early-game deaths and find lethals when they were present.

Notably, Slay the Spire runs with swarms took significantly longer due to high discussion volume for almost every turn.

We tested agents on 15 past AZsPCs contests that involve a set of computationally intensive optimization problems, generally involving sophisticated heuristics.

Each contest ran for about three months⁠77, during which participants could submit solutions across around 25 closely related problems. L Budget NanoGPT

Our Budget nanoGPT task is based off of the nanoGPT speedrun, a public project to minimize the wall-clock training time required to reach a target validation loss of 3.28 while pre-training a language model. In the speedrun, the hardware configuration and target validation loss are held fixed, while leaving the training recipe open, including architectural and optimizer modifications, hyperparameters, and systems-level optimizations.

In our interviews with nanoGPT contributors⁠79, they said that they spent 16 to 40 hours to get a record with about a 1 to 2% speedup. They mostly used a free Google Colab or a single H100 GPU and used a full 8xH100 node only for a few final confirmation runs. We give agents 250M tokens and an 8xH100 node for 48 hours.

This section compares agent performance to historical human performance on this task.

Agents outperform the majority of budget nanoGPT speedrunners. However, the very top human contributors still outperformed agents.

On our task, agents outperform competent speedrunners. However, top human contributors still reach much larger speedups. The best agent run reached a 15.7% speedup over our starting record in 48 hours. The author of a recent record (#92), with AI assistance for coding, estimated spending about 60 hours for a 41% speedup over the previous record.

Agents achieve speedups more cheaply than top human contributors

We find that frontier agents can achieve speedups at lower cost than top human contributors working without AI. Following our expenditure horizon (EH) method, we compare agents' returns to expenditure on NanoGPT with the local returns to human labor. EH is the equivalent human expenditure at which humans and agents reach the same speedup starting from the same baseline.

From interviews with contributors, a 1 to 2% record took a human 16 to 40 hours with minimal GPU usage, which we estimated at roughly $2,500 per 1% of speedup⁠80. By contrast, some agents achieved nearly 10% speedup at a total cost of around $1,000. Thus, we find that agents outperform and are cheaper than competent speedrunners, with an expenditure horizon value ranging from $2,900 to $25,100.

Notably, AI assistance is generating considerable uplift for human speedrunners. For example, a recent AI-assisted record (#92, Aug 2026) broke the consistent trend in human effort required for this task. We interviewed its author:

  • They estimated spending ~60h for a 41% speedup over the previous record.
  • They said that while 90% of the ideas came from them, Fable 5 implemented over 90% of the changes.
  • They retroactively estimated it would have taken about 300 hours without AI, a roughly 5x uplift.

Even against this specific author's much lower estimate of unassisted effort, frontier agents seem several times cheaper than a top human working without AI, at least up to about $10K of equivalent human expenditure.

The difference in cost may be a result of agents running many more training experiments than a human contributor would, and GPU time being cheap next to expert hours.

Agent solutions targeted different improvements than human solutions did.

Interestingly, in budget nanoGPT, we find that human and model PRs tended to target different areas. Human PRs were more likely to improve speed by reducing the number of training steps needed to reach the target loss whilst AIs were more likely to reduce the time each training step took.

AI agents usually approached this task by trying to systematically optimize the speed of a single training step, as opposed to making improvements that reduce the number of training steps needed to reach the target loss. They tend to initially set up cheap hillclimbable proxy measurements (e.g., profilers) that reveal a list of bottlenecks which tend to require kernel or systems engineering style work.

This was also reflected in the types of improvements that AI PRs vs human PRs tended to target. In human PRs, kernel-related improvements were responsible for on average 24% of speedup, whilst in AI PRs kernels were on average responsible for 64% of speedup.

We observe this distinction when we categorize solutions by the share of speedup contributed by different layers of the NanoGPT stack. For humans, kernels were responsible for 24% of speedup, training recipe changes for 25%, and architecture changes for 39%. Agent speedups tended to be more concentrated in kernels and training recipes, though this varies by model. On average agent speedups were 64% kernel related and 20% training recipe related.

Agent performance under resource constraints

NanoGPT contributors told us they mostly experimented on free Google Colab GPUs or a single H100, and almost always used an 8xH100 node only for final speedup confirmation runs.

To test agents under similar resource constraints, we varied the GPU hours afforded to an agent. This limits how many training experiments they can run, within the same 48h working-time limit.

We find that models perform worse on average as GPU budgets shrink, though our data is noisy (n=3 runs per model with large error bars). Interestingly, over the same working hours, capped agents spent the same share of time reasoning as their uncapped counterparts (e.g., 25% vs 24% at the 4-hour cap). Smaller budgets mostly stifled them and ended their runs sooner.

We think that additional budgets often helped the models turn promising and challenging changes into working improvements via repeated testing and repair. For example, writing faster kernels or low-precision GPU code often required checking whether the time saved outweighed any extra training needed to reach the required model quality (i.e., a target loss of 3.28). We observed agents often broke the trained model’s quality at first, and needed additional training runs to fix them.

Although we find resource constraints affect agents, frontier agents still achieve larger speedups than humans at budgets comparable to that used by typical nanoGPT contributors.

M Making METR tasks

We made a task designed to test model ability to autonomously make our evaluations. In this task, we give a model a reasonable set of resources for task development, and then manually grade the tasks based on whether we would include them in our task suite.

Setting and resources

The task-developing model has a token budget of 100M, and 48 hours of working time. It has 3 environments that it can use concurrently to test its task. In those environments it can run either a frontier model (Opus 5.5), or a lagging model (GPT-6 Luna). Each model available for testing has its own token budget of 100M, shared across all test runs. Additionally, a final run with the frontier model and a distinct 10M token budget is attempted at scoring-time.

Results (project files and scoring output) from all the agent’s test runs and the final run are made available to human manual scorers and agent judges. To help with the judging process, the task also requires agents to write arguments for the task’s relevance and feasibility, as well as notes on its iteration process.

Internet is allowed in all containers. Agents may specify that their task ought to be run without internet access. We additionally give the task-developing model selected files from a subset of our own tasks to give them a sense of the kind of tasks we’re interested in.

To improve elicitation, we’ve conducted a few cycles of iteration on the instructions and provided assets, for example providing example tasks and guidance on what types of tasks are of interest. If the agents were provided with more detailed threat modeling, they would likely perform better, but this would remove part of the task’s sensitivity that we are interested in.

Giving additional guidance

We additionally ran a selection of frontier models with a suggested direction (e.g. that the task should be judgment-loaded, require continual learning, or involve open-ended research), but these runs did not meaningfully outperform the standard instructions. Agents often still made hill-climbable machine learning tasks, for example GPT-6 Astra twice made tasks based on LCBench⁠81 data, which require the selection of hyperparameters for small MLPs on classification datasets.

We also ran frontier models for additional samples on this task with access to the descriptions of the previous model-made evaluations, and the human ratings and informal scoring notes on them. These runs did not produce meaningfully more useful outputs.

Models frequently made tasks which were saturated at first, but they were usually able to iterate away from perfect saturation and make the tasks significantly more difficult. For instance, they would introduce fundamental changes to the kind of task they were designing, or adjust the scoring formula. N Earthborne Rangers and Continual Learning

While playing an obscure boardgame, frontier models were worse than humans at improving over time.

Drastically accelerating AI R&D plausibly requires the ability to do research in novel areas or to acquire and use new knowledge over time. This could make learning from experience at inference time (aka continual learning) a relevant capability.

We briefly tested how well models continually learn in a novel board game setting, using a slightly modified version of Epoch’s EBR-bench, a text version of Earthborne Rangers. In the task, agents aim for as many of 21 objectives as possible, which they discover by playing the game many times and keeping notes that persist across playthroughs.

This game was chosen by Epoch for being obscure and strategy-loaded. We expect this task to be relatively novel and out-of-distribution since there is no public digital version of the game, and common heuristics for this genre do not apply⁠82. We made the original task harder for agents by banning the "Meditation Pillow" card, which allows models to bypass much of the game’s strategy.

We find that GPT-6 Astra learns a little from playthroughs, completing about 3 more objectives by the end, while Fable 5.1 barely learns. However, humans seem to learn much more in fewer playthroughs. For instance, the best human went from 9 to all 21 objectives in 6 playthroughs.

We see a similar lack of clear learning in a harder setting with Downpour weather every day, though we have no human baselines to compare against there.

One difference between Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra is how well each explores across playthroughs. Fable 5.1 settles on a deck early and replays minor variations of it without improving. GPT-6 Astra, by contrast, keeps trying new decks, some of which resemble those built by strong human players.

While Astra explores new decks across playthroughs, Epoch’s recent analysis suggests that it doesn’t learn better tactical decision-making. As a proxy for this, they track how much fatigue a player takes per turn, since taking less fatigue leaves more turns to play. They find that Astra stays at the same level across 10 playthroughs, while a human improves about threefold within three playthroughs.

O Qualitative and External Evidence on Models’ Strengths and Limitations

External evidence suggests that strong model capabilities on optimization and coding tasks extend beyond just benchmarks

There is various evidence that models’ strong optimization and coding abilities are ‘cashing out’ on ‘real’ tasks, including those directly related to accelerating AI R&D. We discuss below a non-exhaustive list of recent external evidence. Whilst this post mainly focuses on evidence about public models, some recent salient evidence involves non-public models. We also note that much of the below evidence is from internal research at frontier labs that we have not audited ourselves.⁠83 Evidence being included here does not mean that METR has formally reviewed or checked these claims.

AI developers have recently reported that models are speeding up their own R&D at various layers of the AI stack. Anthropic reported that models lead 26% of R&D as of August 2026, up from near zero in January. OpenAI reported that experiment velocity in August 2026 was 1.6x the 2025 level. As of mid-late 2026, OpenAI reports agents autonomously solving 1-4 hour long tasks with more than a 50% success rate; Google DeepMind reports success rates of 44% for similarly long tasks. For OpenAI, in January 2026, agents completed 51% of 1-2 hour tasks. Both OpenAI and GDM report low success rates on tasks more than 32 hours long (GDM reports 13% on week or longer tasks, and OpenAI 17% on 32-64 hour tasks).

These companies also state that models now carry out large internal projects that used to require several human engineers. In July 2026, the creator of Bun (now at Anthropic) reported using a pre-release version of Fable 5 to rewrite Bun in Rust in 11 days, a project he estimates would have previously taken a small team of engineers a full year. In Oct 2026, Anthropic reported migrating to an in-house version control system built by a small team with Claude in less than a year. OpenAI reports that GPT-5.6 Sol autonomously rewrote their production GPU kernels, which, together with other optimizations, cut end-to-end serving costs by 20%.

Beyond optimization and coding, we’ve also seen significant model capability improvements in both cybersecurity and mathematics.

  • In our Hugging Face incident investigation we found that a swarm of about 700 agents, some based on internal models, coordinated to hack an external company. There has also been a massive spike in the rate of public cyber vulnerabilities and exposures discovered.
  • In Sept 2026 OpenAI’s internal AI system produced a proof resolving a version of the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize Problem (building on earlier work). They also then went on to release over 700 AI-produced manuscripts on open math problems in Oct 2026, although many are yet to be verified. Similar to cyber, discoveries in math outside of internal results have somewhat accelerated recently.⁠84

We also find that models accelerate parts of our own work at METR. For instance, greatly speeding up the development of complex evaluations, writing low-level plotting code, and accelerating the development of a new transcript viewer.

However, public models still have deficiencies that prevent them from fully automating tasks involved in AI research work, and this aligns with our experience.

Models seem relatively weak at the less well-specified parts of AI research work and fall short of human researchers. We observe this across reports from frontier labs, external evaluations, and our own experience. We mainly discuss here a non-exhaustive list focused on qualitative observations in reports from frontier labs and external evaluators.

The Opus 5.5 System Card states that "Opus 5.5 does not seem close to being able to fully substitute for our Research Scientists and Research Engineers, especially relatively senior ones," and notes that internal users found it mostly tests incremental ideas and prefers less ambitious hypotheses. Similarly, Mythos 5.1’s main reported shortcomings relative to human researchers were that it "often states easy-to-check guesses as facts, exaggerates the completeness of its work, fails to verify important claims."

In Oct 2026, the Gemini 4 Argon Safety & Security Report shared that some components of AI R&D are getting automated, but substantial human participation and oversight remains particularly in tasks beyond 1h (Figure 3.16 in their report). In a survey of 12 senior leaders and researchers they also found that "automation is highest in well-specified, verifiable execution tasks and lowest in open-ended scientific ideation and long-horizon research taste."

We observe similar failure modes in AI research outside of the frontier labs.

Benchmarks such as Epoch’s InnovationEval and Crux Eval’s CRUX 2 test agents on end-to-end AI R&D tasks where agents recreate recent or non-public human innovations. In these evaluations, frontier models such as Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra produced papers that were rejected by experts. Some weaknesses include not coherently communicating main findings, and struggling to productively pivot and backtrack.

In a preliminary experiment, we re-ran a version of Epoch’s EBR-Bench and found similar in-context learning results. In particular, we found that frontier models did not learn to play a novel game as effectively over time compared to the best humans. We discuss this further in Appendix N: Earthborne Rangers and Continual Learning.

Epoch AI finds that current public frontier models cannot yet autonomously produce Epoch-quality work and replace Epoch workers. Models often converged on similar ideas in open-ended parts of their work. For instance, when agents were given Epoch's polling data, three of six models wrote about the same topic. Epoch also reports that models lacked the judgment to follow through on promising directions. In one evaluation, GPT-6 Astra gave the models it was testing too few tokens which caused the models’ responses to be cut off. GPT-6 Astra then attributed weak performance to the model instead of its own setup.

In our own work we also encounter similar limitations. For instance, models were not reliably able to carry out analysis of our evaluations on their own.⁠85 When a frontier model was asked to compare human and agent solutions to the Budget NanoGPT task, it was unable to come up with good differentiators. However, a METR researcher looking at several transcripts themselves was able to notice and show that agents generally tended to focus on improvements that optimized time per step (like kernels) whilst human improvements tended to focus more on improving overall efficiency (by reducing steps needed to reach the target loss). For more information see Appendix L: Budget NanoGPT.

Measuring the Self-Reported Impact of Early-2026 AI on Technical Worker Productivity

A survey of 349 technical workers finds a median 1.4–2x self-reported change in value of work due to AI tools, expected to grow over time, though there are reasons to be skeptical of the magnitude.

We build on our time-horizon work and analyze 9 benchmarks for scientific reasoning, math, robotics, computer use, and self-driving in terms of time-horizon trends; we observe generally similar rates of improvement to the 7-month doubling time in our original time-horizon work.
